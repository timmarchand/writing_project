# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 The phenomenon: online news commenting as public evaluative discourse

When readers of an online newspaper reach the end of an article, they frequently encounter a second text of a rather different kind: a stream of responses written not by journalists, but by other readers. Comment facilities of this sort have become an institutional feature of online news provision, and the contributions they host, which may be known as reader generated content (RGC), below the line discourse (BTL), but this thesis will primarily refer to as online news comments (ONC), constitute a form of public writing that would have had no clear equivalent before the spread of the networked computer. A comment is typically short, written asynchronously, addressed to an indefinite audience, and publically visible beneath the article that triggered it. In most cases, it is produced by an ordinary reader rather than a professional writer, it is rarely edited by the host organisation, and it enters a discursive space shared with hundreds or thousands of other contributions responding to the same prompt. Taken together, the comments appending a single article form a *thread*. and it is the thread, rather than the isolated comment, that most approximates the unit of participation as experienced by readers and writers alike.

Although comment sections might at first sight appear to be a peripheral by-product of online publishing, their scale suggests otherwise. Major news outlets have for many years attracted thousands of contributions to individual articles, and the cumulative volume of reader-produced text hosted by a single national broadcaster or newspaper may plausibly be measured in the hundreds of millions of words. It would seem reasonable to suggest that commenting on the news now represents one of the largest arenas of everyday public writing in English. For applied linguists, therefore, ONC offers an unusually rich record of how ordinary writers, rather than trained ones, behave when asked to do something linguistically demanding: to take a public position on events of the day in writing, before strangers.

The significance of the phenomenon extends beyond the description of a single regsiter. For research on second language (L2) use and development, comment sections represent a distinctive site of public language use, one whose functional demands differ markedly from those of academic writing on which learner corpora have overwhelmingly been built. That consideration anticipates the learner dimension of the thesis, to which Section 1.3 turns.

What commenters do in this space is, in the first instance, evaluative. As the emerging literature has begun to document, online news comments are strongly oriented towards the assessment of events, policies, institutions, an persons, and towards alignment and disagreement with other participants. Ehret and Taboada [-@ehret2020], in their multidimensional comparison of online news comments against traditional registers, characterise the register as a hybrid of argumentative and informational writing with markedly evaluative colouring; in a subsequent comparison across online registers, the same authors find comments to be distinctive among web varieties, although, in their careful phrasing, "by far not as involved and evaluative" as certain other online registers such as opinion columns and reader reviews [@ehret2021, p.11]. Work conducted within the Appraisal framework paints a broadly compatible picture from a different theoretical direction. Cavasso and Taboada [-@cavasso2021] report that evaluation in a large corpus of news comments is predominantly negative, and that it is realised chiefly through Judgement and Appreciation rather than through the direct expression of Affect, a distribution they interpret as a kind of detachedor depersonalised evaluation, in which the world, rather than the writer's feelings about it, is the grammatical focus of the appraisal. The construction of large dedicated resources, most notably the SFU Opinion and Comments Corpus [@kolhatkar2020], further attests to the degree of research attention the register has begun to command.
These properties of this discursive activity are worth singling out at the outset, because together they motivate the analytical focus of this thesis. First, commenting is positional. To comment is, in most cases, to take a stance: to evaluate a proposition, an actor, or an action, and to commit oneself publicly to that evaluation. Secondly, commenting is oriented. Comments are directed both towards the article, whose topic constrains and anchors the discussion, and towards an imagined audience of fellow participants, with whom agreement and disagreement are continually negotitated. Thirdly, commenting is conventionalised. Participation is subject to strong pragmatic expectations, and recurring communicative functions, such as assessing an opinion, expressing certainty or doubt, conceding a point, or challenging another contributor, all of which appear to be served by recurrent linguistic means. Stance, in other words, is not an incidental feature of the register; it would appear to be central to its communicative function.

Two further features of the discursive environment deserve mention, since both bear on the corpus design decisions of Chapter 4. The first is threading. In current BTL practice, comments are not simply concatenated; they may reply to the article or to one another, producing nested sub-discussions in which stance is often taken towards other participants' contributions rather than towards the news itself. The precise structure of threading, however, varies by platform and period: the earlies comment facilities, including the one form which the benchmark corpus of this thesis is drawn, offered flat comment streams without nested replies. The second is moderation. Contributions to a hosted facility such as the BBC's *Have Your Say* are subject to house rules and to varying degrees of pre- or post- moderation, so that the visible record is a filtered sample of what was submitted. Neither feature undermines the register characterisation offered here, but both impose contraints on comparability whenever two comment corpora collected under different conditions are set side by side, a point developed in Sections 1.10 and 1.12.

For the purposes of this thesis, an online news comments is defined as a reaader-produced text psoted to the comment facility attached to a news article, pulcaly displayed beneath that article, and composed without direct editorial intervention by the host outlet. This definition deliberately excludes the news article itself, contributions to general discussion forumms unconnected to specific news features, and social media posts, although all of these are neighbouring varieties with which ONC shares features and from which it must be analytically distinguisehed. The precise situational parameters of the register, and the evidence for treating it as a register in the first place, are taken up in Chapter 2. The purpose of the present section has been the more modest one of establishing the phenomemnon itself: a mass, public, evaluative form of everyday writing whose linguistic character, and whose use by non-native speakers of English, remain considerably less well understood than its social prominence would lead one to expect.

## 1.2 Online news commenting as a register

It would be a mistake to assume that the linguistic character of ONC can simply be read off from what is known about 'computer-mediated communication' (CMC) more broadly. Early work on internet language not infrequently proceeded as though electronically mediated constituted a single, homogenous variety, populary labelled with such terms as 'Netspeak' [citation], and set in contrast to offline speech and writing. Subsequent corpus-based research has demonstrated quite clearly that no such homogenous variety exists [citations]. Registers, in the sense in which the term is used throughout this thesis, are varieties associated with particular situations of use, characterised by prevasive linguistic features that are functionally motivated by those situations [@biberconrad2009]. When online texts are examined with this framework in mind, what emerges is not one 'online English', but a field of internally differentiated varieties. Grieves et al. [-grieve2010] showed that even within the single domain of blogs, substantial and systematic functional variation can be recovered; Biber and Egbert [-@biberegbert2016; -@biberegbert2018], analysing the searchable web at scale, identified a multidimensional space of online registers whose boundaries cut across platform categories in sometimes unexpected ways; and Ehret and Taboada [-@ehret2021] located online news comments within this space as a register in its own right, related to but distinct from its online neighbours. CMC, on this view, names a set of production and distribution circumstances, an environment rather than a variety, withinn which many registers coexist.

The register construct is central to the design of this thesis, and it is therefore worth stating explicitly what claiming register status for ONC does and does not entail. A register, following Biber and Conrad [-@biberconrad2009], is identified by its situation of use: its participants and the relations among them, its channel and mode, its production and comprehension circumstances, its setting, its communicative purposes, and its topics. ONC can be characterised by a broadly coherent set of situational parameters. Its participants are non-professional writers addressing an indeterminate public; its channel is asynchronous written text embedded in a news website; its production circumstances permit more planning than conversation, but less revision than published prose; its overriding purposes are evaluative and argumentative; its topics are supplied, at least initially, by the news article to which comments attach. If these situation characteristics are indeed functionally related to recurrent linguistic patterning, as the register hypothesis claims, then ONC should exhibit systematic linguitic characteristics of its own, and the preliminary evidence reviewed in Chapter 2 suggests that it does.

A brief comparison with neighbouring varieties may help to fix the register's profile more sharply. Relative to face-to-face conversation, with which it shares an evaluative and interpersonally engaged orientation, ONC is planned rather than spontaneous, persistent rather than ephemeral, and addressed to strangers rather than intimates; Ehret and Tabaoda [-@ehret2020] indeed pose the comparison explicitly, asking whether online news comments are like face-to-face conversation, and answer in the negative on dimensional grounds. Relative to the news article that anchors it, ONC is subjective where the article is (conventionally) objective, dialogic where the article is monologic, and unedited where the article is professionally produced. Relative to academic writing, the register most often accounted for in learner corpus research (LCR) [citations], ONC shares an argumentative orientation, but differs in audience, evidential conventions, and its tolerance of informality. Those contrasts will be developed systematically in Chapter 2; they are anticipated here only to make the point that ONC should not be treated as a diluted version of any of its neighbours.

Two consequences follow, and both are critical for what comes later. The first is negative. Because online registers are internally differentiated, findings established for one elctronically mediated variety cannot be assumed to hold for another: conclusions about chat, blogs, or tweets, whether concerning native speakers or language learners, cannot be imported into the study of news comments without empirical evidence. This point acquires particular force in the study of learner language, where, as Chapter 3 will document, the register provenance of learner corpora has not always been acknowledged or treated with the care it deserves. The second consequence is positive. If ONC is a register, then one can meanigfully ask of any populationof writers participating in it, including language learners, whether their production occupies the same register space as that of other participants. That question, posed of Japanese university learners of English, predicates the first research question of this thesis, and it presupposes exactly the register-theoretic footing that the present section has sought to establish.

## 1.3 The learner dimension

The writers whose language is examined in this thesis are not, however, the spontaneous participants of the register describe above. They are Japanese university students of English as a foreign language, commenting on the news as part of a content-based language course, and the corpus of their construction, the Japanese University Students' Online Comments corpus (JUSOC), therefore occupies an interesting intermediated position between naturalistic participation and instructed performance. Between 2012 and 2017, successive cohorts of learners, principally at one Tokyo university, with two further universities contributing to the 2012 and 2015 cohorts, read and commented on (semi-)authentic online news items (the articles themselves may be described as semi-authentic as they were often composed and modified with L2 readers in mind).In doing so, they produced a record of learner participation in an authentic public register across six annual cohorts, several hundred writers, and over ten thousand comments. The corpus and its origin will be described in detail in Chapter 4; for present purposes, what matters is that JUSOC represents something LCR has rarely had occasion to examine: learners writing at length, over extended periods, in a register whose functional demands are evaluative and argumentative rather than academic in the conventional sense. It should be noted that exposure to native-speaker production of the register was limited by design: native-speaker comments were presented as a worked example at the beginning of each cohort only, while participation itself was framed by opinon^sharing questions provided as a scaffolding, a feature of the course context that this thesis treats as a testable variable rather than an assumed complication (Section 1.10).

The design also sits squarely within the methodological tradition of LCR. Constrastive interlanguage analysis, in its original and reeappraised formulations alike, rests on the comparison of learner production against native-speaker reference data on the one hand, and against data from other learner populations on the other, with differences interpreted against the backdrop of learner variable such as first language proficiency, and task [@granger1996; -granger2015]. The phraseological strand of this tradition which examines learners' use of recurrent word combinations and bundles, has been especially productive, documenting systematic over- and under- use of formulaic sequences across first-language backgrounds and proficiency levels [@chen2010; -@chen2016; -@ishikawa2023]. What JUSOC contributes to this tradition is a change of register focus: the comparative logic is retained, but the texts compared are participants' contributions to a public evaluative register rather than responses to academic prompts. In that respect, the corpus can be seen as extending a research programme begun with the analysis of the native benchmark itself [@marchand2013], in which the register is held constant and the population varies.

That learner language differs systematically from native speaker language is the founding observation of learner corpus research (LCR), with a substantial body of work, from the early contrastive interlanguage studies onwards [@granger1998; -@granger2015, now built upon it. It is equally well established, however, that observed learner-native differences are not self-interpreting. A quantitative difference between a learner corpus and a native reference corpus is an observation, not an explanation, and the distance between the two is frequently underestimated [CITATION]. Suppose, as a purely hypothetical illustration, that a stance construction such as *it seems to me that* were found to be significantly less frequent in JUSOC than in a comparable native-speaker corpus of news comments. The difference might reflect L1 influence, in that equivalent Japanese constructions carry different pragmatic weight; it might reflect proficiency, in that the construction is acquired late; it might refect the task and its audience, in that learners commenting under course conditions address classmates and an assessing teacher rather than an annonymous public; it might reflect topic, in that the two populations were not discussing the same news; it might reflect register understanding, in that learners have not fully internalised the evaluative conventions of the genre; or it might reflection some interaction among all the above. The observation alone cannot adjucate among the candidates.

This interpretive problem is well known in principle, and LCR has developed an increasingly sophisticated methodological apparatus for managing it, including careful corpus matching, multivariate designs, and a growing insistence on register comparability [@gilquin2015; -@gilquin2022]. Nevertheless, the literature reviewed in chapter 3 suggests that the problem retains its force in the specific domain of stance and phraseology, where learner-native comparisons have been conducted predominantly on academic writing, on the basis of corpora collected under examination of or coursework conditions, with register, topic, and task frequently confounded with the learner variables of interest. The result is a body of findings whose transferability to informal, public, evaluative registers cannot simply be assumed. What is needed, it would seem, is a design in which the register is held constant, or as constant as the data allow, so that learner-native comparison is conducted within the register rather than across register boundaries. JUSOC, paired with HYSOC, comes closer to that ideal than most available resources. Both corpora consist of comments on news articles; the learners were being asked to participate in the register rather than merely produce an academic approximation of it; and proficiency information is available for the large majority of learner writers, permitting the within-learner comparisons that many learner corpora of non-accademic registers cannot support. None of this eliminates the confounds enumerated above, and Section 1.12 is explicit about the residual variation that remains. It does, however, shift the balance of interpretive risk; the register itself is shared, while periods, topics, audience, and task conditions are not, so that whatever differences survive are correspondingly more likely index the learner variables of actual interest. Providing such a design, and demonstrating what it can deliver, is one of the central ambitions of the thesis, as elaborated on in the next section which seeks to describe the resulting research problem. 

## 1.4 The research problem

### 1.4.1 The register-side gap

On the register side, the situational outline of ONC is now reasonably well establised, and its broad functional characterisatio, as an evaluative-argumentative hybrid, has converged across studies conducted within rather different frameworks [@ehret2020; -@ehret2021; -@cavasso2021]. What remains under-described is the recurrent phraseological resources through which the register's evaluative work is accomplished: which stance-related patterns are conventional in the register, how they are lexically realised, and how they are distributed across the register's characteristic communicative functions. This is a narrower claim than it may appear. It is not claimed that stance in online comments has gone unstudied; the Appraisal-based work cited above is explicit testimony to the contrary. It is claimed, rather, that the phraseological dimension, in the specific sense of recurrent word sequences serving identifiable stance functions, has received comparatively little register-focused attention, and that the available evidence remains scattered across studies with heterogeneous corpora, units, and selection procedures. The functions at issue are not particularly (idiosyncratic? exotic?): they include the assertion of opinion, the qualification of epistemic commitment, concession to an opposing view, agreement and disagreement with fellow participants, and the evaluation of actors and actions in the news. These are the acts of which comment threads are made, yet no inventory of their conventional phraseological realisations currently exist for the register, still less one derived under explicit, replicable selection criteria.

### 1.4.2 The learner-side gap

On the learner side, the picture is inverted. Stance and phraseology in learner language have been intensively studied, but the evidentiary base is heavily concentrated in academic and otherwise controlled registers: argumentative essays written under examination conditions, coursework assignments, and placement-test writing predominate [@chen2010; -@chen2016; -gilquinpaguot2008; -@hyland2008]. Japanese learners of English, the population of interest here, have been studied in the same registers, with the result that much of what is known about their stance repertoires, including their well-documented preferences for overt first-person epistemic marking and their relative neglect of more impersonal alternatives, derives from data whose genre conventions actively encourage or suppress the very features under investigation [@kamimur1998; -mccrostie2008; -@miki2010]. Whether the same patterning holds when Japanese learners participate in informal public evaluative discourse is, at present, largely unknown.

### 1.4.3 The intersection

The research problem addressed by this thesis lies at the intersection of these two gaps. The central question is not simply whether Japanese learners differ from native speakers, but whether and how they differ when participating in the same communicative register, and whether differences in broad register profile correspond to differences in the phraseological resources through which stance is realised. Framing the problem in this way imposes a particular architecture on the investigation. The register dimension must be established before the phraseological one can be interpreted;the phraseological inventory must be specified before the confirmatory comparison can proceed; and the comparison itself must be conducted against an appropriate benchmark of native-speaker particiapation in the same register, here provided by the Have Your Say Online Comments corpus (HYSCOC), a collection of posts to the BBC's commenting facility dating from 2001, assembled for earlier work on the register [@marchand2013] and expanded for the present study; its contributors were predominantly, though not exclusively, native speakers of English, and its composition will be documented in Chapter 4. The comparative logic is anchored in this benchmark throughout: learner frequencies are interpreted relative to native-speaker participation in the same register, and, where evidence from learner academic writing is brought to bear, the three-way comparison of learner comments, native comments, and learner essays serves to separate register effects from population effects insofar as the data permit. The remainder of this chapter unpacks the components of this architecture: the stance-phraseological unit of analysis (Section 1.5), the relaionship between register profile and phraseological realisation (Section 1.6), the research questions themselves (Section 1.7), and the expectations, design, contributions, and scope conditions that follow from them (Sections 1.8 to 1.12).

## 1.5 Stance as phraseological realisation

Of the linguistic resources implicated in commenting, stance is arguably the most central, for reasons already outlined above but repeated here: to comment is to evaluate, assert, qualify, agree, and disagree, and each of these acts requires the writer to signal a position towards a proposition and towards an audience, THe term stance is used here in the (by now) standard sense familiar in the corpus linguistics field: to cover the lexical and grammatical expression of personal feelings, assessments, attitudes, and epistemic judgements, including the marking of certainty and doubt (@biber1999; -@hylan2005]. Stance so defined is realised at every level of linguistic organisation, from individual modal verbs and evaluative adjectives to clause-level complementation patterns, and any attempt to study it must therefore make a principled choice of analytical unit.

The choice adopted in this thesis is phraseological, and it rests on two well-established observations. The first is that language use us pervasively patterneed: speakers and writers draw heavily on recurrent multi-word sequences or "chunks" [CITATIONS], and these sequences carry the conventionalised functions of the registers in which they circulate [@biber1999; -@biber2004]. The second is that stance meaning, in particular, is rarely carried by isolated items alone. Expressions of epistemic commitment, concession, or evaluation tend to be realised through characteristic configurations: a first-person pronoun with a cognition verb and a complement clause, an extraposed adjectival evaluation with a *that*- or *to*- clause, a modal verb framed by particular subjects, and the communicative force of such expressions is a property of the configuration rather than of any single word within it [@graybiber2013; -@hyland2008]. If stance is indeed central to the communicative function of the commenting register, and if evaluation is conventionally realised phraseologically, then the recurrent phraseological pattern is the natural unit at which to study how the register's central work gets done, and how the learners' execution of that work compares with those of native speakers.

A few illustrations will clarify the intended construct. Consider the act of advancing an opinion in a comment. A writer may realise it through an overtly interpersonal frame, such as *I think (that)* or *it seems to (that)*; through a concessive frame, such as *I agree with X, but*; through an impersonal evaluative frame, such as *it is (hardly) surprising that* or *the fact (remains) that*; or through a modalised assertion, such as *there is no reason (why)*. These realisations differ structurally, in the position they assign the writer relative to the proposition, and functionally, in the degree and kind of commitment they express, yet they are recognisably engaged in the same underlying discourse work. It is precisely this many^to^one mapping between recurrent structures and stance functions that the pattern construct is designed to capture [CITATION], and it is the distribution of such choices, rather than the presenece or absence of any single item, that the comparisons of RQ3 and RQ4 will target.

Terminological precision is required at this point, because the relevant literature employs several overlapping terms. Throughout this thesis, a **stance-related phraseological pattern** is the primary analytical unit: a recurrent structural configuration, defined in grammatica or tag-based terms, that conventionally serves an identifiable stance function. A **bundle**, in the usage adopted here, is a recurrent lexical realisation of such a pattern, an attested word sequence that instatiates it [SINCLAIR CITATION?]. The relationship is thus one of type and instantiation: lexical sequences of the relevant kind include *I think that*, *I don't think*, and *I suppose*, which illustrate the pattern of first-person pronoun plus stance verb (plus complementation); whether any such sequence qualifies as a lexcial bundle, however, is an empirical question settled by the frequency and dispersion criteria of Chapter 4. This usage should be distinguished from the 'lexical frame' of Gray and Biber [-@graybiber2013], which names a fixed grammatical frame with variable slots, and belongs to a somewhat different analytical tradition, although the constructs are plainly related. The pattern-first architecture has two methodological advantages that matter for what follows: it permits the inventory of analytical targets to be specified from prior theory and external evidence before any learner data are examined, and it separates the linguistic definition of the object of study from the statistical apparatus used to compare its frequencies. Both advantages are exploited in the design described in Section 1.10, and the approach builds directly on the tag-based operatiionalisation piloted in Marchand [@marchand2026]. Chapters 3 and 4 will outline the full terminological differences between the *pattern* and *bundle* constructs touched upon here, and the several senses in which 'lexical bundle','lexical frame', and related terms circulate in the literature. But for now, the definitions above will suffice to describe all ongoing usage.

## 1.6 Register profile and phraseological realisation

The architecture of Section 1.5 rests on a distinction that deserves a section of its own, because it structures the entire investigation: learner-native comparison can be conducted at (at least) two distinct linguistic levels, and sameness at one level does not entail sameness at the other. At the first level, that of register profile, the question is whether learner production, taken as a whole, occupies the same broad functional space as native-speaker production in the register: whether learner comments are similarly involved, similarly narrative, similarly explicit, and similarly persuasive in their global orientation, as these dimensions are operationalised in the multidimensional tradition [@biber1988]. At the second level, that of phraseological  realisation, the question is whether the specific linguistic means through which the register's central functions are executed is the same: whether learners draw on the same stance-related patterns, with the same lexcial realisations, to comparable extents.

It is crucial to the logic of this thesis that these two questions are independent. A learner corpus could replicate the register profile of its native benchmark almost exactly while realising the register's evaluative functions through a substantially different phraseological repertoire; conversely, a corpus could diverge in broad profile while sharing much of the same local forms. The first possibility is more than a theoretical nicety. In pilot work on the ICNALE corpus of Asian learner writing, Marchand [-marchand2026] found evidence consistent with what might be termed a routing pattern: learners and native speakers confronted with the same underlying communicative needs appeared to route those needs through structurally different realisations, with learners favouring overt interpersonal frames of the *I think* and *I agree with* types, while native speakers carried proportionally more of the same functional load through impersonal and noun-phrase-internal structures. If such routing were to prove general, it would suggest that functional parity and phraseological parity are separate developmental attainments, and that a learner population may sound register-appropriate in the large while remaining phraseologically distinctive in the small.

The present thesis is designed to test whether this is so in online news commenting. The formulation is deliberate: the thesis tests whether routing is evident in learner ONC; it does not presuppose that routing will be found, and it is constructed so that either outcome is informative. Should learner comments match their benchmarks in both register profile and stance phraseology, that would constitute evidence that participation in an authentic evaluative register, even under course conditions, can support target-like deployment of the register's conventional phraseology, a finding with obvious implications for both LCR and pedagogy. Should the levels dissociate, with approximate register parity coexisting with systematic phraseological divergence, the routing hypothesis would receive support from a register far removed from the academic writing in which the pattern was first observed. The benchmark  against which either verdict is reached is native-speaker participation in the same register, and the comparative logic is anchored throughout in that benchmark rather than in any abstract notion of error or deficiency, a point to which Section 1.12 returns.

## 1.7 Research questions

The considerations above resolve into four research questions, which the thesis addresses in sequence. Three principles guide their formulation. First, register precedes phraseology: no question about stance patterning is posed before the register frame within which that patterning occurs has been characterisd. Secondly, comparison is anchored to the thesis's own reference corpus benchmark: every learner-native questions is posed relative to native-speaker participation in the same register, not relative to an abstract native norm. Thirdly, the two levels of analysis distinguished in Section 1.6 are kept terminologically separate throughout, so that claims about register profile are never silently converted into claims about phraseological realisation, or vice versa.

**RQ1.** How does the register profile of Japanese learners' online news comments compare with that of native-speaker online news comments?

**RQ2.** What recurrent stance-related phraseological patterns are attested in online news comments, and how are they lexically realised and functionally deployed?

**RQ3.** How does the use of these stance-related phraseological patterns differ between Japanese learners and native-speaker writers in online news comments?

**RQ4.** How does the use of these patterns vary across proficiency levels among Japanese learners, and what developmental trajectories do they exhibit?

Before proceeding, it is best to note a scoping limitation for RQ2. This question is operationalised as an examination of stance-related phraseological patterns within the theoretically and empirically defined search space described in Chapter 4, rather than as an exhaustive corpus-driven inventory of all possible stance phraseology in ONC. The claim is deliberately bounded: the thesis asks how learners differ in their use of stance-related patterns that are demonstrably part of the benchmark register, rather than what innovative stance resources learners may produce, and learner-specific innovation falls outside the confirmatory inventory by construction, for reasons that Chapter 4 will make explicit.
THe four questions are not analytically homogenoues, and it is important to be clear about the role each plays, since the appropriate inferential methods differ across them. Table 1.1 offers a summary.

**Table 1.1.** *Research questions and their analytical roles.*


| Question | Object of inquiry | Analytical role |
|---|---|---|
| RQ1 | Broad register profile of learner vs. native ONC | Exploratory and descriptive: characterises register positioning and locates learner production relative to the benchmark and to external reference registers |
| RQ2 | Recurrent stance-related phraseological patterns in ONC | Descriptive and functional: specifies the inventory of patterns within the search space defined in Chapter 4, their lexical realisations, and their discourse functions |
| RQ3 | Learner vs. native-speaker use of the selected patterns | Confirmatory: tests pre-specified between-corpus differences under the modelling framework of Chapter 4 |
| RQ4 | Proficiency-linked variation within the learner corpus | Confirmatory: tests pre-specified proficiency effects and their trajectories within the learner population |

## 1.8 Relationships among the research questions

The four questions form a nested progression rather than a list. RQ1 establishes the register frame: whether learner comments inhabit the same broad functional space as native comments. RQ2 specifies the phraseological inventory internal to that frame: what the conventional stance SOMETHING of the register actually is. RQ3 and RQ4 then conduct the confirmatory comparisons between populations and then across proficiency levels within the learner population. Each question is therefore interpretable only in the light of its predecessors. A phraseological difference detected under RQ3 means one thing if RQ1 has established broad register parity, and something quite different if the learner corpus turns out to occupy a distinct register space altogether; likewise, a proficiency effect under RQ4 can be read as developmental only against the backdrop of a stable learner-native difference under RQ3.

One qualification regarding proficiency should be mentioned here. The proficiency evidence in JUSOC is mainly cross-sectional: writers are observed at a given point, assigned to proficiency bands, and compared across bands. While they are also followed over time across the course of one academic year (approximately nine to ten months), the main developmental trajectories in this thesis are quasi-longitudinal, patterns across proficiency levels from which developmental inferences are drawn cautiously and provisionally, rather than complete longitudinal records of individual change. A further design distinction should also be noted here: cohort membership, which indexes the year of data collection, is not equivalent to proficiency, and the two variables play different roles in the modelling of Chapter 4. These qualifications are developed fully in Sections 1.12 and Chapter 4.X.

The nested structure also determines the shape of the eventual interpretation. Taken together, the register-level and phraseological findings provide a basis for distinguishing whether learner participation is broadly convergent at both levels, or whether broad register similarity coexists with systematic phraseological divergence. It is worth stressing that the design is indifferent as to the outcome of this combination, and any result would SOMETHING a finding worth reporting.

## 1.9 Expectations

Although the confirmatory analyses of the thesis are pre-specified rather than exploratory, it is both possible and useful to state, in advance, what the existing literature leads one to expect. Three sets of expectations may be defined, corresponding to the register, phraseological, and developmental levels of the design.

### 1.9.1 Register expectations

At the register level, the expectation is one of broad comparability with possible local divergence. The situational parameters of participation are largely shared between the two populations, and under the Flagstaff school of register studies [SOMETHING, citations]. Both respond to news articles in a public comment facility, both write asynchronously for an indefinite audience, and both operate under the same broad functional imperative to evaluate and persuade. It therefore seems likely that learner comments will position themselves in approximately the same multidimensional space as the benchmark,  although divergences on particular dimensions, plausibly those associated with overt persuasion and involved production, would occasion no surprise given the learner register literature [@gilquinpaquot2008; -biber1988]. It should be noted, however, that the benchmark itself represents an early form of the register, dating from a period before its conventions had settled, so that parity with the benchmark would indicate convergence with the register's early conventionalisation rather than with its mature form,  a qualification to be developed in Section 1.12. The expectation of broad comparability also has a methodological SOMETHING: where novel or specialist varieties have been scored against established dimensions, they have typically proved interpretable within the existing dimensional space rather than orthogonal to it [@grieve2010; -@ehret2021], and there is no obvious reason to expect learner commenting to behave otherwise.

### 1.9.2 Phraseological expectations

At the phraseological level, the expectations are sharper, because the LCR evidence, although register-skewed, is consistent in direction. Previous research has frequently reported a preference among learners, including Japanese learners, for overt, first-person, interpersonal realisations of stance, of which *I think* provides a prominent example [@mccrostie2008; -@miki2010; -@chen2010], while making comparatively sparing use of the impersonal, extraposed, and noun-phrase-internal structures through which much stance work is conducted in proficient native-speaker writing [@graybiber2013; -@biber2004; -@hyland2008]. The pilot evidence of Marchand [-@marchand2026] points the same way from within a Japanese population. The working expectation is therefore a restricted and asymetrically distributed repetoire on the learner side: over-reliance on a small set of interpersonal bundles, under-use of the impersonal alternatives, and a hedge repetoire which is narrower in range even where it is comparable in overall frequency. The two-pole candidate inventory developed from the literature in Chapter 4 operationalises exactly this expectation. Two additional strands of evidence informs this. The first concerns proficiency: bundle use has been shown to vary systematically across rated proficiency levels in learner writing, with higher-rated scripts making more target-like use of stance and referential bundles [@chen2016], so that within-learner differentiation is to be expected as well as learner-native difference. The second concerns L1: contrastive work on Japanese learners, and on Asian learner populations more broadly, has associated this population with particular phraseological preferences that plausibly have an L1 basis, although the attribution of specific features to transfer remains,as ever, a matter of inference rather than observation [@ringbom1998; -petchtyson1998; -@ishikawa2023].

### 1.9.3 Developmental expectations

At the developmental level, finally, no single uniform trajectory is expected, and it would be methodologically unwise to impose one. The phraseological development literature, together with the pilot findings, suggests that different patterns are likely to exhibit different proficiency profiles. Several trajectory types can be anticipated and are distinguished in the analyses to come, defined jointly by the direction of any divergence from the benchmark and by its course across proficiency bands. Sustained divergence, whether sustained overuse or sustained underuse, describes a pattern that remains consistently above or below the benchmark across all bands. Attenuation, or partial recovery, describes movement towards the benchmark with proficiency that stops short of it, from either direction. Convergence describes trajectories that reach benchmark levels at the higher bands, again from either direction. U-shaped development describes profiles that change direction across the bands, rising and then falling or the reverse, as new forms are acquired and then integrated. Two further cases complete the set: sustained parity, in which a pattern is used at benchmark levels from the outset, and the absence of any systematic proficiency association, in which band-to-band fluctuation follows no describable curve. The second is not, strictly speaking, a trajectory, and with sparse counts some such fluctuation is to be expected from sampling variability alone; it is included because the categorical treatment of proficiency allows it to appear and because its interpretation differs accordingly [CITATIONS FOR EXAMPLES OF EACH TRAJECTORY??].

 The typology is heuristic, naming landmarks against which the estimated profiles can be read. The modelling consequence is unchanged: proficiency is treated as a categorical variable throughout the confirmatory analyses, precisely so that changes of direction are estimable rather than assumed away (Section 1.10; Chapter 4). The trajectories matter beyond description, moreover, because they discriminate among developmental stories that aggregate frequencies cannot: sustained overuse suggests a stable interlanguage preference and sustained underuse an entrenched avoidance; attenuation suggests gradual approximation to register conventions and convergence its successful acquisition; and U-shaped profiles suggest the restructuring that accompanies the integration of newly acquired forms. The pedagogical implications of these patterns differ accordingly, a thread picked up in Chapter 7.

### 1.10.2 Pattern identiication and functional analysis

RQ2 is addressed descriptively. The inventory of stance-related phraseological patterns is identified from the register itself, using the native benchmark corpus as the primary identification base, and is then analysed along two dimensions [TWO ASPECTS?]: lexical realisation, that is the bundles by which each pattern is instantiated, and functional deployment, that is the discourse work each pattern performs in context. ### 1.10.3 Confirmatory comparison (RQ3 and RQ4)

RQ3 and RQ4 are addressed confirmatorily, through count models of bundle frequency, and unlike RQ1, the unit of analysis moves to the individual comment. The primary modelling framework is Poisson regression with a log link and an exposure offset, estimated by pseudo-maximum likelihood with cluster-robust inference. This choice of framework was motivated by a number of factors that SOMETHING: because the outcome variables are potentially sparse counts of pattern realisations in each textual unit; because such counts are strongly overdispersed; and because the units vary in length. The suitability of this approach for sparse evaluative bundle frequencies in learner corpora is demonstrated in Marchand [-@marchand2026]. Between-corpus differences (RQ3) are estimated as corpus effects against the native benchmark; proficiency effects (RQ4) are estimated within the learner corpus with proficiency as a categorical predictor, supplemented by a pre-committed test of scaffolding effects ariing from the course context. Because textual units are nested within both writers and threads, inference throughout takes explicit account of clustering at these levels, and the benchmark-relative magnitude of estimated effects is assessed against the variation attributable to threads and writers themselves, so that a 'significant' corpus effect is interpreted in the light of the variability native speakers display among their own discussions. Model specification, robustness safeguards, and the treatment of multiplicity[WORD CHOICE?] are detailed in Chapter 4.

### 1.10.4 Separation of exploratory and confirmatory inference

One design principle cuts across all four research questions and is stated here since it governs the interpretation of the results reported in Chapters 5 and 6. The additive MDA and benchmark-relative dimension comparisons provide an exploratory characterisation of register. They do not determine which stance-related patterns are selected for confirmatory analysis. Bundle selection is specified independently from prior theory and external evidence, and the resulting candidate set is fixed beforee confirmatory modelling. This separation protects the confirmatory analyses from the circularity that arises when the same data are used both to generate hypotheses and to test them, and it embodies a broader methodological commitment: that the linguistic object of study should be specified independently of the statistical inference used to test it.

## 1.12 Scope and limitations

The claims of this thesis are bounded in several respects, and these should be clarified before any readings of the interpretations in Chapter 7. The following five limitations registered here will be elaborated upon where necessary in later chapters.

First, learner status is not a causal variable. JUSOC is a convenience corpus in a sense that its writers are learners enrolled in a particular course, taught princippaly at one Tokyo university with contributions from two further institutions in two cohorts, and any learner-native difference observed is an association, attributable in principle to proficiency, first language, educational background, task conditions, or their combination, and not the causal effect of just 'being a learner'. The within-register design narrows the space of competing explanations, but it does not eliminate it altogether.

Secondly, the native benchmark is a benchmark, not a gold standard. HYSOC represents one population of commenters, writing on one platform, in one period, under one moderation regime; its contributors were predominantly, though not exclusively, native speakers of English, and native-speaker status is inferred rather than verified for much of the corpus, so that the term 'native-speaker corpus' is used throughout with this qualification understood [CF EXPERT CORPUS??]. Nor does the facility's moderation certify nativeness: it SOMETHING, at most, on the proficienc of what passed the filter. It is also worth noting that the benchmark writers were themselves among the first generation of participants in a register that had not yet conventionalised. This cuts both ways. On the one hand, it narrows one potential asymmetry between the two populations, since neither the benchmark writers not the learner were socialised experts in an established genre [IS IS FOR THIS REASON that native-speaker corpus is preferred to expert corpus??]. On the other hand, it means that HYSOC is a snapshot of the register's early conventionalisation rather than its mature form, and a snapshopt of that kind cannot serve as a gold standard for particiaption over a decade later, even in principle. Divergence ffom HYSOC, in any case, is divergence from that population; it is not error, deficiency, or non-nativelikeness in any normative sense, and nothing in the thesis should be read as implying otherwise.

Thirdly, corpus matching is imperfect. The two corpora differ markedly in period of collection, the benchmark dating from 2001 and the learner corpus from 2012 to 2017; in the topics discussed, since the two populations were not responding to the same news; in moderation environment; in threading structure; and in the presence of pedagogical scaffolding on the learner side. These differences are documented in Chapter 4, controlled where the data permit, and carried as interpretive caveats where they do not.

Fourthly, as noted in Section 1.8, the proficiency evidence is cross-sectional. Developmental trajectories are inferred from differences across proficiency bands observed at a single point in time, and longitudinal confirmation would require SOMETHING [CAVEAT about individual change over the year??]

Finally, the bundle inventory is not exhaustative.
