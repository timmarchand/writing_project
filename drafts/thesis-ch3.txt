## 1. Introduction: The learner dimension of the ONC problem

### 1.1 From Chapter 2 to the learner corpus

As outlined in Chapter 1, research on computer-mediated commincation (CMC) and online discourse provides the basis, through register theory, for treating online news commenting as a distinct communicative environment. The central conclusion of that chapter was that online news comments (ONC) would appear to constitute a distinctive evaluative written register: publice, asynchronous texts in which lay readers react to news events, characterised by involved, stance-heavy features that set them apart from both the news reports they respond to, and from casual conversation [@BiberConrad2009; @BiberEgbert2018; @EhretTaboada2021]. Chapter 2 also identified the first of the two gaps motivating this thesis: although the register may be defined by evaluation, it would seem that no register-specific inventory of its recurrent stance phraseology has yet been compiled, i.e. of the multi-word sequences built around tagged stance features such as evaluative adjectives, stance adverbs and suasive verbs - the means through which evaluation is conventionally expressed in comment discourse. [CLOSING SENTENCE]

The present chapter turns to the second dimension of the study: learner language. In reviewing Learner Corpus Research (LCR), this chapter seeks to establish how learner-native differences hve tradionally been investigated and, crucially, why such comparisons require attention to register. The corpus as the heart of this thesis introduces a complication that Chapter 2 left undeveloped: the data under study are learner-produced, comprising six cohorts of online news comments written by Japanese university students learning Engish as a foreign language (EFL). With learner texts, the descriptive question of Chapter 2 can no longer be separated from an interpretive one. Learner language is known to diverge systematically from native-speaker usage even when no error is present, though the quantitative overuse and underuse of perfectly well-formed items [@Granger1998chapter; @Granger2003]; the interpretation of any such divergence, however, depends on what is known about the learner language, about the register, and about the learner population in question.

Given this complication, the task of the present chapter is to establish the framework within which a learner-native comparison of ONC stance phraseology can be interpreted, identifying the competing explanations that must be addressed before a difference may be attributed to learner status, to first language (L1), or to register knowledge. In doing so, the chapter establishes the thesis's second gap: research on second language (L2) stance and phraseology, including the substantial body of work on Japanese learners, has been conducted almost entirely on academic or otherwise controlled writing, and the intersection of learner language, stance, phraseology and informal online evaluation appears to remain insufficiently understood.

## 1.2 Why learner-native comparison is not straightforward

When learner language differs from native language, the question of *what* is responsible is not a straightforward one. Such differences may reflect the learners' L1, through the transfer of rhetorical or linguistic habits; their proficiency or developmental stage; the task conditions under which the texts were produced; the genre or register sampled; the topic; the period of data collection; or the learners' educational background and prior instruction. @Granger1996 insisted from the outset of Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis (CIA) that such external variables should be controlled, so that comparisons are made "like with like", and SOMETHING has since proved consequential: proficiency is "best described as a fuzzzy variable" in learner corpora [@Carlsen2012], task and prompt conditions measurably alter learner output [@AlexopoulouEtAl2017], and register effects in learner writing would appear to rival or exceed those of writer status or L1 [@LarssonPaquotBiber2021].

A worked example may serve to show how these explanations confound. [EXAMPLE HYPOTHETICAL - TO BE REPLACED WITH REAL DATA LATER]. Suppose the comparison finds that the Japanese learners'  comments contain a particular hedged evaluative frame, say a recurrent sequence built around a stance adverb qualifying an evaluative adjective, at a normalised frequency of 6.2 occurences per 10,000 words, against 11.8 in the BBC reference corpus. One reading attributes the gap to proficiency: epistemic and phraseological repertoires are known to be narrower at lower proficiency and to widen as learners advance [@ChenBaker2016; @HylandMilton1997], so the learners may simply not yet command the device. A second reading attributes it to register: learner writing is pervasively prone to drift toward informal, speech-like patterning?? [@GilquinPaquot2008], so the learners may be producing an informalised variant of the comment register, in which case the frequency difference measures register mismatch rather than learner deficit. A third reading invokes the L1, a fourth the classroom conditions of collection, a fifth the topics addressed, and a sixth the period separating the two data collections. The arithmetic of 6.2 versus 11.8 is identical under all six readings; nothing in the raw difference itself decides among them.

What discriminates among the readings is, therefore, design rather than arithmetic. A proficiency explanation requires evidence that use of the frame varies with proficiency within the learner corpus, which the six-cohort structure of the present data can supply; a register explanation requires that the two corpora demonstrably represent the same situational variety; and L1, task, topic and time explanations require corresponding internal comparisons or explicit acknowledgement. The comparability principle adopted in Section 2, that a learner-native difference is attributable to learner status only to the extent that other major sources of variation have been controlled or accounted for [@ConnorMoreno2005], is thus the condition under which corpus frequencies become evidence at all. It should be noted, however, that the two corpora compared in this thesis also differ in period and collection circumstances, which is why Chapter 2 framed the BBC data as a benchmark rather than a matched control: the comparison describes how the learners' production relates to the register, not its distance from an idealised native target.

### 1.3 The argument of the chapter

The argument developed across this chapter rests on four governing propositions. First, Learner Corpus Research establishes the value of systematic learner-native comparison: confronting large collections of authentic learner production with native data makes visible the overuse and underuse patterns that error analysis could not detect [@Granger1996; @Granger2003; @Granger2015]. Secondly, such comparisons require an appropriate reference corpus and sustained attention to competing sources of variation: the native corpus functions as a reference population rather than a prescriptive norm, and L1 composition, proficiency and task conditions must be documented or controlled before differences are interpreted [@ConorMoreno2005; @Gilquin2022; @Granger2015]. Thirdly, register, genre and task substanitally affect learner language, so learner-native comparison must be sensitive to register: linguistic choice varies systematically with the situational context in all language users [@Biber1988; @BiberConrad2009], learner production would appear to be no exception, and comparisons across mismatched registers risk attributing register effects to learner status [@LarssonPaquotBiber2021]. Finally, existing L2 stance and phraseology research, including research on Japanese learners, has focused overwhelmingly on academic or controlled writing [@ChenBaker2010; @ChenBaker2016; @HylandMilton1997; @AdelErman2012], and the major Japanese learner corpora are likewise built on essays and elicited speech [@GrangerEtAl2009; @Ishikawa2013; @Tono2007]. The intersection of learner language, stance, phraseology, and informal online evaluation remains correspondingly under-described.

The thesis of the chapter follows directly from these propositions: register may be regarded as a necessary condition for interpreting learner-native differences, since every alternative explanation for such differences (L1, proficiency, development, task, topic and time) operates within, and interacts with, the register in which the language was produced.

The chapter develops this argument in four steps, before bringing them all together in a final synthesis. Section 2 reviews Learner Corpus Research and its comparative method, examining the two configuations of CIA, the interpretation of learner-native differences, the design variables that condition comparability, and the progression from pioneering examples like the International Corpus of Learner English (ICLE) and its native reference corpus, the more tightly controlled examples like the International Corpus Network of Asian Learners of English (ICNALE). Section 3 develops the chapter's central claimm, that register is a necessary variable in learner corpus research, drawing on multidimensional analyses of learner writing and on the academic-register bias of the field's SOMETHING. Section 4 turns to stance and its phraseological realisation in L2 writing, reviewing what is known about learners' epistemic calibration, lexical bundle use and phraseological development. Section 4 will also explore what this research cannot tell us, concentrated as it is on institutional genres. Section 5 narrows the focus to Japanese learners, reviewing corpus evidence on their stance and phraseological choices and setting out the complete explanations (L1, proficiency, task, register, topic and time) that any learner-native comparison of this population might disentangle. Finally, Section 6 draws the strands together and outlines the double gap that emerges when the learner-side gap established here is coupled with Chapter 1's register gap. It concludes with the methodological requirements that Chapter 4 must meet to answer that SOMETHING.

## 2. Learner Corpus Research and Comparative Logic

This thesis compares a corpus of online news comments written by Japanese learners of English as a foreign language (EFL) with a reference corpus of native-speaker comments from the BBC Have Your Say forum. Any such comparison presupposes?? a methodological framework that specifies what may legitimately be compared, against which reference data, and under what interpretive constraints. This section therefore reviews Learner Corpus Research (LCR), the branch of corpus linguistics concerned with the systematic study of learner language, and its signature comparative method, Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis (CIA). The argument developed below is that learner-native differences cannot be meaningfully interpreted independently of register: the legitimacy of such a comparison depends on the comparability of both the populations and the situations in which their language was produced. This section traces the emergence of LCR (2.1), presents the two configurations of CIA and their relation to the register-based SOMETHING of Chapter 1 (2.2), examines how learner-native diferences should be interpreted (2.3), reviews the design variables that conndition comparability (2.4), and situates this study within the development of major learner corpora, such as the International Corpus of Learner English (ICLE), the Louvain Corpus of Native English Essays (LOCHNESS), and the International Corpus Network of Asian Learners of English (ICNALE) (2.5) [MORE EXAMLPES??).

### 2.1 The emergence of Learner Corpus Research

Before the advent of learner corpora, empirical evidence about second language (L2) production came largely from three sources, each with recognised limitations: introspection and elicitation, which reveal what learners can do under experimental conditionsa rather than what they do naturally; error analyis (EA), which reconstructed the learner's developing system from observable errors, revauled as evidence of transitional competence [@Corder1967]; and small, hand-collected datasets whose "considerable variation in the numbers of subjects, in the backgrounds of the subjects, and in the empirical data" [Odlin, 1989, p. 151, as cited in @Granger2003, p. 541] made cumulative generalisation difficult.

Error analysis, hoowever, was structurally blind to quantitative divergence. @Schachter1974 demonstrated that learners may avoid structure they find difficult, so that an error count captures only what learners attempt and fail, not what they decline to attempt at all. A learner might use a linguistic feature correctly but far more often, or far less often, than a native speaker would, and this phenomenon, later termed *overuse* and *underuse*, lay outside EA's descriptive reach [@Granger1998chapter; @Granger2002].

Learner Corpus Research emerged in the early 1990s as a systematic, corpus-based answer to these data problems. A computer learner corpus is defined as "an electronic collection of authentic texts produced by foreign or second language learners", assembled, like all corpora, "according to explicit design criteria", but requiring "extra care" in data collection "given the large number of variables affecting the learning/acquisition process" [@Granger2003, p. 538; cf. @Granger2002; @Nesselhauf2004]. The founding resource was the International Corpus of Learner English, first presented by @Granger1993 as a cross-l1 corpus of advanced learners' English essays in which the learner's first language (L1) was a central design variable [see also @Granger2024], and the edited volume *Learner English on Computer* [@Granger1998book] supplied early demonstrations of cross-linguistic lexical profiling and overuse/underuse analysis [@GrangerRayson1998; @Ringbom1998; @Lorenz1999].

CIA should be distinguished from both error analysis, including its computer-aided descendant [@DagneauxDennessGranger1998], which remains focused on the classification of non-target-like forms, and from corpus-based seccond language acquisition (SLA) research, which uses learner corpora to test acquisition hypotheses [@Myles2015; @TracyVenturaPaquotMyles2021]. It is a specifically comparative methodology defined by its systematic confrontation of learner data with native-speaker data, r with other learner data, and it supplies the comparative logic of the present study.

### Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis

Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis was introduced by @Granger1996 as the corpus-based counterpart of Contrastive Analysis: whereas Contrastive Analysis compared different languages, CIA was designed to establish comparisons "between native and interlanguage varieties (NL vs IL) and between interlanguage varieties (IL vs IL)" [@Granger2024, p. 247; see also @Granger2003, p. 541].

The first configuration, learner versus native-speaker comparison, uncovers "the patterns of use distinguishing learner data from native data", which "fall into two categories: qualitative differences (misuse) and quantitative differences (over- and underuse)" [@Granger2003, p. 541]. A text may thus contain few overt errors yet still produce a distinctly non-native impression because the frequency distribution of perfectly well-formed items diverges from native norms [@Granger1998chapter], as early studies of connector usage [@GrangerTyson1996] and adjective intensification [@Lorenz1999] demonstrated.

The second configuration, learner versus learner comparison, assesses the effects of the L1 (the classic transfer question),, proficiency, age, learning setting, medium and task type. @Granger1996 already underlined the importance of controlling such external variables, insisting that researchers compare "like with like" [Granger, 1996, as discussed in @Granger2002]. Because IL versus IL comparisons isolate these variables, they are indispensable before any observed pattern is attributed to learnerhood in general [@Granger2002]. The present study adopts the first configuration (Japanese EFL learners versus native speakers) while holding constant, as far as corpus design permits, the variables that the second configuration has shown to matter.

#### 2.2.1 CIA and the logic of Chapter 1

The parallel between CIA and the register-analytic framework establised in Chapter 1 is worth making explicit since the thesis combines the two. Register analysis, in the tradition of @BiberConrad2009, compares a target register against a reference register to identify lingusitic features functionally characteristic of a situation of use; CIA compares a learner corpus against an expert or native-speaker corpus to identify the features characteristic of learner production. Both are fundamentally comparative in design, and both are uninterpretable without an approrpiate comparison population: a register feature is only "characteristic" relative to other registers, just as a learner feature is only "overused" relative to some reference variety. This is the same premise that connects this chapter with the last chapter. Chapter 2 showed that a register cannot be identified except by comparison with other registers; the present chapter shows that language specific to a learner group cannot be interpreted except against an approrpiate comparison population. The two chapters thus echo each other in SOMETHING the importance of comparative design.

The two design are nevertheless not equivalent, and conflating them is a methodological risk best to be avoided. Register analysis targets situational variation: its axis of comparison is the situational context (purpose, channel, participants, topic9, and its populations may both consist of expert native users. CIA targers learner-status variation: its axis of comparison is the writer or speaker's status as either learner or native user, and its situations are ideally held constant. The distinction has a direct consequence for th design of the present study: if the learner corpus and the native-speaker reference corpus differ not only in learner status, but also in register, then any observed differences are ambiguous between the two axes. Chapter 2's characterisation of online news comments (ONC) as a distinctive evaluative written register therefore plays a double role in this thesis: it motivates the choice of the BBC *Have Your Say* data as a reference corpus, and it frames the interpretation of any learner-native difference that survives the matching.

### 2.3 Interpreting learner-native differences

Comparability of design does not by itself settle the prior question of what a learner-native difference means. This subsection reviews the debate over the native norm that will inform the interpretive stance of this thesis.

#### 2.3.1 The native corpus as reference population, not as idealised target

Early CIA studies were sometimes read, and occaisionally writtern, as if the native corpus represented an idealised target, and that every quantitative divergence from it a deficiency. This reading has been progressively abandoned within LCR itself. Granger's [-@Granger2015] reappraisal of the method she introduced two decades earlier is decisive here: the revised model, CIA2, replaces the single idealised native norm with multiple possible reference language varieties, and reconceives the two comparison types as informing each other bidirectionally. @Gilquin2022 systematises the point: while learner corpous studies "can dispense with a native norm, they usually rely on one", and choosing the most approporiate among the avaialble native and non-native norms is of crucial importance. The native corpus is thus best understood as a referenc population, a sample of what a defined community of users actually does in a defined situation, rather than as a standard of correctness.

This interpretation also reflects a fact about native reference corpora that is often underplayed: they are themselves samples from particular populations. LOCNESSS, the reference copus of the ICLE tradition, consist of student rather tham professional writing [@Granger1998book; see 2.5.2 below]; treating it as a prescriptive model would both overvalue native production and misrepresent the particular native population sampled.

#### 2.3.2 The comparative fallacy

The strongest caution against target-relative analysis predates learner corpora. @BleyVroman1983 indentified what he termed the "comparative fallacy", "the mistake of studyimg the systematic character of one language by comparing it to another" (p. 6), arguing that constructs defined relative to the target language, such as error, omission and substitution, risk obscuring the internal logic of the interlanguage system. Taken as methodlogical caution rather than a prohibition, the point constrains interpretation rather than design; comparison with native data remains legitimate as a discovery procedure, provided that interlanguage is not thereby defined as failed nativeness and that quantitative divergence is not automatically read as deficit [@Granger2015]. For the present thesis the implication is specific: the BBC *Have Your Say* corpus is used as reference population, a documented sample of how one native community actually wrote in the ONC register, and not as a prescriptive representation of correct English, so that divergence from it calls for explanation rather than correction.

#### 2.3.3 Implication for this study

These considerations determine the status of the BBC *Have Your Say* corpus in this thesis. It does not function as a prescriptive model of how the Japanese learners ought to write, and still less as a matched control in an experimental sense: the two corpora differ in period, collection circumstances and the communities sampled, and no attempt is made to equate them beyond register. This is precisely the "benchmark, not matched control" framing established in Chapter 1, now given its LCR justificatins. Consistent with CIA2 [@Granger2015] and Gilquin's [-@Gilquin2022] account of corpus-derived norms, a learner-native difference identified against this benchmark will be interpreted as evidence about the learners' register-sensitive production, and not as an error count or a measure of distance from an ideal.

### 2.4 Learner corpus design and comparability

If a learner-native comparison is to support inferences about learner status, the corpora compared must differ, as far as possible, only in learner status. This subsection reviews the principal desgin variables that condition such comnparability, L1 composition, proficiency operationalisation, and task and prompt conditions, and closes with the comparability principle that governs interpretation in later chapters.

The first variable is L1 composition. Multi-L1 corpora such as ICLE support cross-linguistic comparison and the identification of patterns shared across learner populations [@Granger1993; @Granger 2003]; but when a single L1 group is the object of the study, a multi-L1 design confounds with institutional context, since each national subcorpus is collected in a difference educational system under different local conditions [CITATION?]. The case for an L1-homogeneous, Japanese-only learner sample in the present sample [HEDGE AS 98% Japanese L1] is correspondingly strong; it isolates the population of interest and aligns with existing Japanese learner resources such as the JEFLL Corpus [@Tono2007] and the Japanese component of ICNALE [@ishikawa2013]. Recent large-scale evidence also counsels humility about L1 effects: cluster analysis of over a million words of ICNALE data from more than 2,300 Asian learners suggest that L1-based similarity may be considerably lower than generally believed once other variables are controlled [@Ishikawa2025], strengthening the case for controlling, rather than assuming, L1-related variation.

The second variable is proficiency operationalisation. In the ICLE tradition, proficiency was assigned by institutional status, with CEFR-based rating added only later [@GrangerEtAl2009]. This reliance on institutional status is not an idiosyncrasy of ICLE but the dominant practice in SLA research generally, whose operationalisations range from impressionistic judgement and institutional status to standardised tests [@Thomas1994]. @Carlsen2012 concludes that proficiency is "best described as a fuzzy variable in computer learner corpora, representing a potential source of error" in LCR. More recent designs respond with explicit measurement: ICNALE combines a standard vocabulary size test with self reported standardised test scores, and classifies all writers into CEFR-linked bands [@Ishikawa2013;@Ishikawa2018]. For the present thesis, proficiency is treated as a variable controlled and SOMETHING ABOUT Chapter 4.

The third variable is the task. Prompts, timing, length-constraints and access to reference tools systematically shape the vocabulary, grammar, stance and phraseology of learner output. In a large-scale analysis of the EFCAMDAT corpus, @AlexopoulouEtAl2017 show that prompt and task requirements have measurable effects on linguistic complexity and accuracy. Corpus documentation acknowledges the point: ICLE's task conditions (timing, dictionary use, prompt) were not uniform across contributing teams and are recorded as metadata [@GrangerEtAl2009], and JEFLL' documentation likewise notes task effects on writing quality [@Tono2007].

It is at this point that the analytical distinction between task, genre and register, maintained throughout this thesis following @BiberConrad2009, becomes a crucial methodological consideration. A task is a concrete elicitation event (a prompt, a time limit, a tool policy); a genre is a culturally recognised, conventionally structured text category; a register is a situationally defined language variety characterised by pervasive functional linguistic patterns. Learner essays, for example, constitute a task and a genre but also, at the situational level, a register whose communicative purpose (displaying assessed academic argumentation [CITATION?) differs sharply from that of public evaluative commentary. A learner-native comparison that confounds these level risks attributing to learner status what in fact belongs to the elicitation task or to the situational variety sampled [CITATIONS]. The formulation of the comparability principle adopted in this thesis follows directly: a learner-native difference is attributable to learner status only to the extent that other major sources of variation, register and genre above all, but also task conditions, proiciency and L1 composition, have been controlled or accounted for.

The considerations converge on whay may be called the comparability principle, the requirement for a common platform of comparison, in cross-linguistic and cross-population research [@ConnorMoreno2005; @Moreno2008]. @Connor Moreno2005 argue that a common platform is required "at all levels of the research", from corpus compilation to the identification of the linguistic resources compared; @Moreno2008 specifies the relevant similarity constraints, genre, subject matter, writers' expertise and mode; and @GablasovaBrezinaMcEnery2017 address the statistical side of comparing frequencies across corpora [GRIES THREE LEVELS OF CORPUS COMPARISON??].

#### 2.5.1 ICLE and the learner-essay tradition

The International Corpus of Learner English established the template of large-scale, systematic learner writing: approximately 3.7 million words of mostly argumentative essays by advanced EFL and ESL learners from sixteen mother-tongue backgrounds, among them Japanese [@GrangerEtAl2009]. The ICLE design made cross L1-comparison and large-sample overuse/underuse analysis possible for the first time. Its limitations for present purposes follow directly from 2.4: the tradition is centred on academic and argumentative writing produced under conditions that varied across contributing teams, and its population is uniformly advanced and adult. As @Granger2024 notes, the early corpora were constrainedin age (adults), proficiency (advanced), medium (written) and text type (argumentative essays), a profile that overlaps only partially with the informal, public, evaluative register examined in this thesis.

#### 2.5.2 LOCHNESS as native reference

Because CIA's first configuration requires native comparison data, the Louvain team collected the Louvain Corpus of Native English Essays, "the mirror of the ICLE" [@GrangerEtAl2009, p. 42]: 324,304 words of mostly argumentative essays by British A-level pupils and British and American university students [@Granger1998book]. Two design features carry the wider lesson. The comparison is meaningful only because the datasets are comparable in purpose and genre, so that differences could plausibly be attributed to learner status rather than text type; and LOCHNESS is deliberatlya novice-writer reference, written by students rather than professionals, which forecloses any interpretation of it as idealised prose [@Granger1998book]. Both features prefigure the treatment of the BBC corpus in this thesis: register-matched, population specific, and non-prescriptive.

### 2.5.3 Why ICNALE was developed

The International Corpus Network of Asian Learners of English was developed explicitly in response to the uncontrolled variation documented above. Reviewing earlier learner corpora, @Ishikawa2011 observes that differences in writing conditions may influence the language of essays, concluding that "a more controlled learner corpus is needed for a more reliable cross-interlanguage study [pp. 3-4]. In the Written Essays module, writing conditions are "controlled more strictly compared with other major learner corpora" [@Ishikawa2013]: two fixed prompts, twenty to forty minutes, 200-300 words, no reference tools, and proficiency operationalised through a vocabulary size test and/or standarised test scores mapped onto CEFR-linked bands [@Ishikawa2011; @Ishikawa2013; @Ishikawa2018]. The JEFLL Corpus [@Tono2007] had already demonstrated the value of tight task control within a single L1 Japanese population, 10,038 secondary school students writing twenty-minute compositions on assigned topics without assistance, but at lower proficiency and without an integrated native component;  ICNALE combined this degree of control with adult, cross-regional coverage and native comparison data [@Ishikawa2018; @Ishikawa2023].

#### 2.5.4 ICNALE as methodological precedent

ICNALE constitutes the methodological precedent closest to the present study: it shows that learner-native and cross-L1 comparisons of Asian, and specifically Japanese, learner English are most reliable when L1 composition, proficiency, topic and task are controlled at the point of data collection, rather than adjusted for *post hoc* [@Ishikawa2013; @Ishikawa2023]. The present thesis adopts the same logic but shifts its object:  where ICNALE controls the task by fixing it, this study controls the register by matching it, comparing learner and native texts produced in the same situational variety, the online news comment, rather than under the same elicitation task. This shift raises the question that the remainder of Chapter 3 addressess, and it is here that SOMETHING: even the most highly controlled learner corpora remain corpora of predominantly argumentative, academic writing. The patterns documented in them, overuse and underuse of stance devices, distinctive phraseological profiles, sensitivity to proficiency, were established in a register of assessed academic display. Do those patterns extend to an informal, public, evaluative online register whose situational characteristics, and hence whose functional linguistic demands, differ systematically from the learner essay? Register theory [@BiberConrad2009] predicts that they should not transfer unchanged; the following sections of this chapter provide the framework within which the thesis tests that prediction.

## 3. Register as a necessary variable in learner corpus research

The preceding sections have established what learner corpus research can reveal about the differences between learner and native-speaker writing, and what methodological cautions such comparisons should observe. This section advances the chapter's central arguement: that learner-native differences cannot be meanignfully interpreted independently of register. A learner who produces an informal, speech-like argumentative essay may not be displaying a deficient interlanguage so much as a mismatch between the situational demands of the task and the linguistic choices the writer has (or has not) learned to make. If register effects are large, then comparisons drawn from a single regiser, or from registers mismatched across writer groups, risk attributing to "learner status" what is in fact variation in communivative context. The section proceeds from the general principle that all language varies by register (3.1), through the multidimensional evidence on learner writing organised around four questions (3.2), to an in-depth treatment of the study that highlighted the importance of register as a variable in learner corpus research (3.3), and developing register awareness of learners (3.4), the academic register bias of the field (3.5), and the near absence of research on learner language in informal online registers (3.6). A synthesis (3.7) draws out the consequences for the study of stance in online news comments.

### 3.1 Learner language varies by register and task 

The foundational insight of register research is that no speaker or writer produces a single invariant variety. This was discussed in the last chapter (section 2.X), but here I will offer a brief recap. Linguistic choice varies systematically with the situational characteristics of communicative events: their purpose, audience, topic, mode and degree of interactivity. Biber's [-@Biber1988] factor-analytic study of twenty-three spoken and written registers deomnstrated that this variation is organised into a small number of underlying dimensions, such as *involved* versus *informational* production, each defined by clusters of co-occuring lexico-grammatical features with shared cmmunicative functions. The cross-linguistic extension of this work showed that analogous dimensions structure register variation across typologically unrelated languages, suggesting that register is a cross-linguistically general determinant of linguistic form [@Biber1995]. @BiberConrad2009 subsequently codified the analystical apparatus, distinguishing register (a variery defined by its situation of use), genre (a variety defined by its conventional textual structure), and style (a variety defined by aesthetic or individual preference). The distinction matters for learner corpus research because task, genre and register are often conflated in corpus design, even though they are analytically separable and can have indepenednet effects on output.

If register variation is a basic property of language use among mature speakers, there is no principled reason to expect learner language to be exepmt from it. Learners, too, adjust their linguistic choices to purpose, audience and mode, although the extent and appropriateness of that adjustment is itself a developmental question (see 3.4). The implications for corpus design were recognised early; @Granger1996 stressed that external variables, including proficiency and task type, must be controlled so that comparison are made "like with like". The register-functional pespective adds a pedagogical motivation: because feature distributions differ across registers, frequency information differentiated by register, as opposed to intuitions blind to it, should inform what learners are taught and how their production is assessed [@BiberReppen2002]. What changed over the following two decades was not the principle but the availability of methods and corpora capable of quantifying regsiter effects in learner data at scale. The multidimensional analysis of learner writing, reviewed next, is the most direct expression of this development.

### 3.2 Multidimensional analysis and learner writing: four questions

Multidimensional analysis (MDA), the methodlogical framework introduced by @Biber1988, identifies dimensions of variation from the statistical co-occurence of lexico-grammatical features across texts and registers. Applied to learner data, it allows the learner-native comparison to be reframed:instead of asking whether learner writing differs from nativs writing in the aggregate, one can ask on which dimensions, under which situational conditions, and relative to which comparison varieties the difference arise. The literature can usefully be organised around four questions: (1) Do learner and native writing differ multidimensionally? (2) Do such differences vary by proficiency? (3) Do they vary by task or register? (4) Does register account for variation that would otherwise be attributed to learner status or influence of first languages?

#### Question 1: Do learner and native writing differ multidimensionally?

The earliest multidimensional analyses of learner corpora answered this question affirmatively, but with important qualifications. Working with a corpus of Tswana Learner English, @vanRooy2008 and @vanRooyTerblanche2009 compared learner writing with L1 student writing and published writing on @Biber1988 dimensions, finding learner essays to be more involved and speech-like than the comparison varieties. @FriginalWeigle2014 and WeigleFriginal2015 extended the approach to timed impromptu test essays, finding that they differed markedly on several dimensions from authentic disciplinary writing in the Michigan Corpus of Upper-level Student Papers (MICUSP), so that the exam register proved a poor proxy for the writing that students must actually produce at university. These studies established that L1-L2 differences are real and multidimensional, but that also contained the seed of a problem: the comparison varieties differed from the learner data in register as well as writer status, leaving open how much of the observed differences was attributable to which factor.

#### Question 2: Do mutidimensional differences vary by proficiency?

Developmental work shows that observed differences are strongly conditioned by proficiency, but in ways that are contingent on register. @BiberGrayPoonpon2011 argued that the clausal subordnination indices long used to measure writing development are characteristic of conversation rather than academic prose, and proposed instead a phrasal-commplexity hypthesis: development in academic writing consists largely in the increasing use of phrasal modifiers within noun phrases. Corpus evidence broadly supports the register-functional account. In the Britishh Academic Written English (BAWE) corpus, phrasal commplexity increased and clausal complexity decreased across levels of study, with strong additional effects of discipline and genre family [@StaplesEtAl2016], and longitudinal L2 disciplinary writing develops over time towards register norms [@BiberEtAl2020longitudinal]. Crucially, proficiency effects interact with task: @BiberGrayStaples2016 showed that both proficiency level and TOEFL iBT task type predicted lexico-grammatical complexity profiles, and @BiberEtAlcomplexity cautioned that complexity measures conflate description with prediction unless register is explicitly modelled. Proficiency, in short, is not register neutral.

#### Question 3: Do differences vary by task and register?

Here the evidence is unusually consistent across research groups and corpus types. Within L1 student writing, @HardyRomer2013 identiied four dimensions of variation across MICUSP's disciplines and paper types, demonstrating substantial register and genr differentiation even among succesful writers, and @GardnerNesiBiber2019, in a new MDA of BAWE, argued that situational parameters (discipline, level of study, genre family) outperform variables of writer background in accounting for variation in student writing. Within learner writing, task effects of comparable magnitude have been documented from large-scale data: @AlexopolouEtAl2017 showed that linguistic complexity and accuracy in EF Cambridge Open Langugage Database (EFCAMDAT) varied systematically across narrative, descriptive and professional task types, and interacted with proficiency; @QuinUccelli2016 found Chinese EFL learners' writing performance to differ across genres, serving different communicative functions; and @YoonPolio2016 found that longitudinal L2 development itself followed different trajectories in narrative and argumentative genres.  @StaplesReppen2016, analysing first-year L2 university writing across L1 backgrounds, genres and language ratings, found genre effects as strong as or stronger thatn L1 and rating effects. Perhaps most consequentially for assessment-driven research, @StaplesBiberReppen2018 demonstrated through situational and linguistic comparison that the register of TOEFL iBT writing tasks is mismatched with authentic disciplinary writing, undermining generalisations from exam essays to academic writing ability. ### Register as source of learner-native variation: Larsson, Paquot and Biber (2021)

The study that crystallised the register argument for learner corpus research is @LarssonPaquotBiber2021 multidimensional analysis of learner writing. Its design brings an unusual range of writer populations and registers into a single analytical frame. Learner data came from two corpora of university writing by intermediate-to-advanced L2 students from five L1 backgrounds (Spanish, French, Dutch, Swedish and Norwegian): argumentative essays from ICLE and research papers from the Varieties of English for Specific Purposes dAtabase (VESPA). These were compared with L1 English university students' essays (LOCHNESS) and research papers (BAWE and MICUSP), and with expert production in the form of published scientific articles (LOCRA). A new exploratory factor analysis of lexico-grammatical features yielded two interpretable dimensions: one counterposing "personal versus topic-focussed style"  and the second "evalautive style versus factual descriptions". Against these dimensions, the study modelled the relative contributions of register (essay, research paper, research article), writer status (learner, L1 student, expert) and L1 background.

The findings reorder the usual hierarchy of explanations. Statistically significant differences emerged between expert and non-expert writers on both dimensions, and significant differences also emerged among the five L1 learner groups, confirming the need for multi-L1 designs. But the register effects were large: learner essays patterned very differently from both L1 and L2 research papers and from epert articles, which means that many features conventionally described as "learner" characteristics reflect the essay register in which learner data have overwhelmingly been collected, rather than learner status as such. L1 effects, though presesnt, were secondary to register and interacted with it. The analytical weight of this result lies in where the variation is located. Because the learner data themselves spanned two registers, the study demonstrates that register produces substantial variation within learner writing, not merely between learner and native production: the same learner populations display one kind of pattern when writing essays, and another when writing research papers. A learner corpus sampled from a single register therefore yields a portrait of the learner that is bounded by tht register, and any difference observed between such a corpus and native data from a different register confounds learner-related with register-related differences, with no way of apportioning the two after the fact. The authors' conclusion is the strongest primary statement of the position this chapter adopts: "if we want to describe and better understand learner production, it is essential that we start recognizing the importance of register in studies of learner language. Results from single-register studies only provide a very partial picture of what learner language is" [@LarssonPaquotBiber2021, p. 255].

The conceptual turning point can be stated as a methodological constraint [CHANGE?]. When learner essays are compared with native writing of a different register (published prose, or even native student research papers), the design confounds writer status with register, and any observed difference is uninterpretable as a property of interlanguage. Comparisons across mismatched registers therefore risk misattributing register effects to learner status, and register matching is accordingly a prerequisite for interpretable learner-native comparison, not a desirable extra that can be waived when convenient data are to hand. This is the same logic that @StaplesBiberReppen2018 documented for exam writing and that @GardnerNesiBiber2019 documented for situational parameters within university writing, generalised here to the core comparative design of learner corpus research itself. For the present thesis, the constraint has immediate consequences: a comparison of Japanese EFL learners' online news comments with native-speaker writing must use a reference corpus that is matched for register (here, the early 2000s BBC *Have Your Say* comments) precisely because no other design can separate the effects of learner status from the effects of producing public, interpersonal, evaluative discourse. In terms of this chapter's guiding question, whether Japanese learner-native differences can be interpreted without taking register into account, @LarssonPaquotBiber 2021 study is the point at which the answer becomes clear: they cannot, because register is among the largest sources of the very variation under study [TOO FLAMBOYANT]?

### 3.4 Register development and register awareness

If register conditions observed learner-native differences, a further question arises: do learners develop the ability to adapt their linguistic choices to communicative context? Addressing this requires distinguishing development from increasing native-likeness. Development need not mean converging on a single native norm; it may equally mean an increasing ability to differentiate one's linguistic choices by context, deploying more informational packaging where the situation demands it, and more involved, interpersonal features where it does not.

Evidence for such register flexibilty, though still limited, is suggestive. @QinUccelli2020 had 263 EFL learners from three L1 backgrounds (Chinese, French and Spanish) each produce two texts on the same topic: a personal email to a close friend, and an academic report to an educational authority.  Learners adjusted lexical, syntactic and discourse-organisational features across the two contexts, and higher proficiency was associated with greater differentiation of complex noun phrases between the academic and colloquial conditions, although the relationship between proficiency and flexibilty was not consistent across all linguistic levels and L1 groups. @LarssonKaatari2020 compared learner writing against four BNC registers ranked on a formality cline (academic prose, popular science, news, fiction) and found that phrasal complexity tracked the cline:  the learners "generally exhibited appropriate register awareness", differing from published writers chiefly in their use of complex nominals [@LarssonKaatari2020, Abstract]. Perception studies complicate the picture only mildly: novice writers' and EAP teachers' judgements of formality across registers align only partially with corpus-derived fomality measures [@DixonEtAl2023; @LarssonEtAl2023]. Set against this partial sensitivity is the well-documented leakage of informal, speech-like features into learners' academic prose. @GilquinPaquot2008 analysis of ICLE argumentative essays against L1 student writing showed learners overusing features typical of conversation (questions, imperatives, second-person pronouns, vague nouns), a pattern they interpreted not as simple difficiency, but as register confusion conditioned by input and teaching materials dominated by speech. @Larsson2019 showed that learners' overuse of grammatcial stance markers varies by register and argued that formality is better treated as a cline rather than a dichotomy, since both learners and native speakers modulate stance marking acrosss registers of graded formality. Taken together, this literature supports a developmental reading: register awareness in L2 writers is real but incomplete, and it apparent absence in single register studies may say more about the eliciting task than about the learners. The developmental programme around grammatical complexity [@BiberGrayPoonpon2011; @StaplesEtAl2016; @BiberEtAl2020longitudinal] makes the same point from another direction: what develops is the capacity to meet register specific functional demands, not an abstract increase in "complexity".

### 3.5 The academic register bias of learner corpus research

The register problem in learner corpus research is not only analytical but institutional: the field has builts its generalisations on a narrow register base. From the earlies ICLE-based studies onwards, learner corpora have consisted predominantly of timed argumentative essays, a bias documented systematically in Gilquin's [-@Gilquin2015] survey of learner corpus design, where register diversity appears as the exception rather than the rule. The rationale was the perceived needs of English for Academic Purposes (EAP) pedagogy, for which learner corpora were explicity framed as the "missing link" [@GilquinGrangerPaquot2007]. The cost of this focus is that "learner language" as described in much of the literature is, in effect, learner language in one or two registers.

Two consequences follow. First, the target ecology is far wider than the data: university students must handle classroom teaching, office hours, institutional documents and a range of written genres, of which the argumentative essay is only a small part [@Biber2006]. Second, the register base determines what can be discovered: single-register learner corpora cannot reveal register flexibility, cannot support register-matched native comparisons[???], and, ad @GilquinPaquot2008 showed, can mislead when the elicited register itself invites informal leakage. The field has begun to correct course: the VESPA project extends learner data to  disciplinary  research papers and reports across multiple L1s [@PaquotEtAl2022], and @LarssonPaquotBiber2021 supplied the analytical demonstrationn of why such diversification matters; but the correction has so far operated within the academic sphere, and the diversified registers remain, overwhlemingly, university registers.

This bias matters for the present thesis because the register under investigation differs from academic writing on almost every situational parameter. Online news comments (ONC), as characterised in Chapter 2, are written for public participation rather than assessment of credentialing (but see details of data collection in Chapter 4.X); their purpose is interpersonal evaluation and reaction to news events; their audience is diffuse readership of strangers rather than a known instructor; and their production conditions are informal, undeited and asynchronous. It would nonetheless be an overclaim to describe ONC as simply "informal": the register analyses reviewed in Chapter 2  show online comments occupying an intermediate, hybrid position, combining involved, stance-heavy interaction with the permanence and public address of writing [@EhretTaboada2021; see also @BiberEgbert2018 on the broader ecology of web registers]. The question this bias poses for the present thesis should be stated precisely [CHANGE]. It is not the historical question of whether findings derived from academic learner writing can be reasonably generalised to an informal, evaluative, public online register. The answer the reviewed literature supports is that they cannot, at least not automatically. What can safely be claimed is that the communicative conditions of ONC fall outside everything the academic register tradition of learner corpus research has sampled, so that generalisations from essay-based studies to learners' online evaluative writing have no empirical SOMETHING.

### 3.7 Synthesis: register as a necessary variable

The literature reviewed in this section converges on a single conclusion. Learner language cannot be interpreted independently of communicative context. Register and task produce substantial, systematic variation within learner populations, variation that is routinely of the same order as and sometimes larger than, the variation attributed to proficiency or L1 background [@AlexopoulouEtAl2017; @StaplesReppen2016; @GardnerNesiBiber2019; @LarssonPaquotBiber2021]. Proficiency itself manifests differently in different registers, since what counts as development depends on the functional demands of the target variety [@BiberGrayPoonpon2011; @YoonPolio2016]. Comparisons across mismatched registers confound these effects, and the field's historical concentration on academic essays has meant that many well-known "learner features" are entangled with the register in which they were observed [@GilquinPaquot2008; @Gilquin2015; @LarssonPaquotBiber2021].

These conclusions are especially consequential for the study of stance, the focus of Section 4. Stance marking is among the linguistic phenomena most sensitive to situational variation: the expression of attitudes, evaluations and epistemic commitments responds directly to a text's purpose, its imagined audience and the interpersonal relatonship between writer and reader [@Biber1988; @BiberConrad2009; @Larsson2019]. A register such as ONC, organised around public reaction and interpersonal evaluation, creates communicative pressures for stance marking that the academic essay does not; and learners' developing (but incomplete) register awareness means that their stance choices in such a register may reflect register knowledge, or its absence, as much as stance competence in any general sense. Interpreting Japanese EFL learners' stance in online news comments therefore requires, as a methodological precondition, a native speaker reference that matches the register, and an account of what that register itself demands. Section 4 turns to the stance literature with this precondition in view.

### 4.1 Stance in L2 writing:from concept to empirical question

Chapter 2 established the conceptual space within which this thesis operates [TOO GRANDIOSE]: stance as the writer's textual projection of attitude, evaluation and epistemic commitment. Perhaps the most influential operationalisation of this came through Hyland's [-@Hyland2005] model of stance and engagement, in which hedges, boosters, attitude markers and self-mention constitute the writer-oriented resources of academic interaction. While Chapter 2 establised the functional and theoretical concept of stance (which will not be repeated here), the present section reviews what L2 research has found about learners' realisation of stance. What it asks is an empirical question: what does L2 writing research show about how learners deploy these resources, and through what linguistic means are learner-native differences realised? The answer, developed across the subsections that follow, is that L2 writers differ from native writers not so much in the gross quantity of stance marking as in its calibration??: the range of devices they command, the precicion with which they grade commitment, and the fit between their rhetorical habits and the norms of the target register [@HylandMilton1997; @Adel2006; @BaxNakatusharaWaller2019]. Crucially for the argument of this chapter, almost all of this evidence comes from institutional, academic or examination genres, a limitation taken up in Sections 4.4 and 4.8.

### 4.2 Epistemic stance: hedging and boosting

The epistemic dimension of stance, the expression of doubt and certainty, has attracted the largest share of L2 research attention, partly because its pedagogical stakes were identified early. @Hyland1994 demonstrated that hedging is a defining characteristic of academic writing, allowing writers to express uncertainty and deference to readers, yet found that textbooks widely used English for Academic Purposes (EAP) classes displayed "an ignorance of empirical usage", neglecting precisely the forms whose misuse produces pragmatic failure for L2 science students. His book-length treatment of hedging in scientific research articles framed hedging and boosting as the negotiation  of knowledge claims within disciplinary commmunities rather than as optional stylisitic ornament (@Hyland1998], setting the agenda for the learner corpus work that followed.

The canonical emipirical study remains @HylandMilton1997. Drawing on a corpus of approximately one million words, they compared the expression of doubt and certainty in examination scritps by 900 Cantonese-speaking Hong Kong school leavers and 770 British students of similar age and level; because the scripts had been graded by the examination authority, the design also permitted a comparison by proficiency, with higher-graded scripts exhibiting patterns closer to the native benchmark. They also found that the L2 writers differed significantly from the native speakers "in relying on a more limited range of items, offering stronger commitments, and exhibiting greater problems in conveying a precise degree of certainty" [@HylandMilton1997, p. 183]. The differences, in other words, concern the calibration of epistemic commitment rather than the amount. Miscalibration can run in either direction: writing may appear "too direct, runnning the risk of being considered as either brusque or dogmatic, or as too tentative, and therefore seem as equivocal, different or naive" [@HylandMilton1997, p. 186]. While these learners were from a different L1 background to the present study, @HylandMilton1997 provide SOMETHING about L2 epistemic stance in general.

Subsequent work complicated any simple "learners hedge less" narrative further. @Hinkel1997, analysing 150 argumentative placement essays by native speakers and by learners from Chinese, Korean, Japanese and Indonesian background, found that L2 writers used vagueness and ambiguity markers more frequently than native writers, while not differing in conventional lexical hedges, cautioning against equating indiretness with hedging. Her larger-scale comparisons converged on a restricted and skewed epistemic repertoire, with L2 essays over-relying on amplified intensifiers and strong modal obligations while underusing conventional hedging forms [@Hinkel2002; @Hinkel2005]. The complementary overuse side of the picture was established by @Adel2006, whose comparison of argumentative essays by advanced Swedish learners (the Swedish ICLE component) with native British and American student essays found that the learners' general overuse of metadiscourse, particularly of writer-visible devices, "strongly marks them as lacking in communicative competence." That advanced learners can simultaneously over-mark stance and under-differetiate it is the strongest available evidence that the locus of the learner-native difference is calibration to genre and audience rather than quantity. This interpretation has since been corroborated in an assessment context: @BaxNatatsuharaWaller2019, examining some 900 examination scripts rated at CEFR B2 and C2, found that higher-level writers used fewer metadiscourse markers overall, but a significantly wider range across most marker classes, a proficiency-graded echo of @HylandMilton1997 range-and-calibration account. Quality, moreover, cuts across nativeness: @LeeDeakin2016 report that successful argumentative essays, whether written by L1 or L2 undergraduates, deployed more hedges and engagement markers than less successful ones.

### 4.3 The development of stance

A second strand of SOMETHING treats stance not as a static L1-L2 contrast, but as a developmental accomplishment. The most comprehensive corpus evidence here is @AullLancaster2014, who compared the stance expressions across more than 4,000 argumentative essays by incoming first-year students, upper-level undergraduate writing from the Michigan Corpus of Upper-level Student Papers (MICUSP) and published prose. Although first-year essays shared many stance markers regardless of students' educational context, the clearest distinctions separated first-year writers from all more advanced groups, and the features indexing the developmental trajectory were specific: approximate hedges and boosters, code glosses, and adversative or contrast connectors. First-year writers underused these delimiting resources while overusing intensifying boosters such as *definitely* and *extremely*. @AullLancaster2014 interpret advanced academic stance as "a process of delimiting one's stance in a way that accounts for the view of others" (abstract), a formulation that aligns development with the calibration theme of Section 4.2: maturing writers learn not to assert more or less, but to position assertions more precisely in relation to alternative voices. Two caveats govern the use of this study in the present chapter. First, it is a novice-expert comparison of predominantly L1 writers and is therefore developmental evidence along an academic register, not an L1-L2 contrast. Second, proficiency remains a live alternative explanation whenever developmental stage and language background are confounded, a point to which Section 4.7 returns.

Genuinely longitudinal L2 evidence, though scarcer, points in the same direction. @CrosthwaiteJiang2017 tracked Hyland's four stance categories in a 205,682-word corpus of Hong Kong university students' writing collected across a semester of  EAP instruction, finding that hedging rose while boosting and self-mention fell, leaving students with "a more careful, narrower, less polarising and less personal range of expressions with which to convey their stance over time" [@CrosthwaiteJiang2017, p. 92]. Taken together with the cross-sectional findings of @BaxNakatsuharaWaller2019 and @LeeDeaking2016, the developmental strand suggests that stance development in L2 writing involves the progessive differentiation and discipliing of the epistemic repertoire rather than a monotonic?? increase or decrease in stance marking.

### 4.4 Stance and register

Section 3 of this chapter concluded that register is consequential for the interpretation of learner language, and the stance literature illustrates why. Academic writing, the register from which nearly all the evidence reviewed above derives, conventionally rewards cautious, citation-based and disciplinary qualified commitment [WORDY??]. Hyland's [-@Hyland1998] scientists hedge because knowledge claims must be negotiated with a sceptical peer community; @Hyland3005 eght-discipline comparison shows that even this generalisation is internal to a particular register, since the soft disciplines take "far more explicitly involved and personal positions" (p. 187) than the hard disciplines. The norm against which learners are judged deviant, in other words, is itself a register-specific and discipline-specifc norm.

Online news commenting (ONC) can be expected to operate under quite different conditions. Public commenting on news stories places a premium on direct evaluation, affect, assertion, agreement and disagreement, and on orientation toward a general readership rather than an assessor or disciplinary peer; there is no institutional incentive to hedge, and considerable interactional incentive to take a position. Two implications follow. First, the "deficit" patterns reported for L2 acacemic writing, such as over-reliance on string commitment, restricted hedging range and excessive writer visibility, may partly reflect a mismatch between learners' rhetorical habits and the specific norms of academic registers, rather than a general inability to calibrate stance. Second, the empirical base for any claim about L2 stance in ONC is thin in both directions: the flagship L1-2 stance studies all use argumentative or examination genres [@HylandMilton1997; @Hinkel1997; @Hinkel2005; @LeeDeakin2016], and where L2 epistemic stance has been examined outside institutional writing, it has proved systematically sensitive to situational factors. @GablasovaEtAl2017stance, analysing spoken production by 132 advanced L2 speakers across four tasks, found "systematic variation in L2 speakers' stance-taking choices across the four tasks", the largest contrast opposing monologic and dialogic conditions; @GablasoveEtAl2024 similarly report that epistemic stance frequency in computer-mediated L2 speaking assessment was influenced by both proficiency and task type. If L2 stance is this malleable in speech, findings from academic writing cannot automatically be assumed to generalise to informal writing. Whether the calibration problems documentented above are reproduced, attentuated or transformed in register that actively licenses direct evaluation is an open question for this thesis.

### Stance as phraseological patterning

The studies reviewed so far mostly operationalise stance through individually tagged items: modal verbs, hedging adverbs, attitude adjectives, metadiscursive signals. A complementary tradtion holds that stance, like much of discourse, is realised through recurrent multi-word combinations rather than isolated lexical choices. The foundational statement is *corpus-based* (cf. Chapter 2.X): @BiberEtAl1999 [p. 990] define lexical bundles as "recurrent expressions, regardless of their idomaticity, and regardless of their structural status". They demonstrate that such sequences are pervasive building blocks that can differntiate register: far denser in conversationn, where they are predominantly clausal and interpersonal, than in academic prose, where the smaller inventory is predominantly phrasal and referential. The theoretical motivation for treating such sequences as psychologically real units comes from research into formulaic language, where a formulaic sequence is defined as "a sequence, continous or discontinuous, of words or other elements, which is, or appears to be, prefabricated: that is, stored and retireved whole from memory at the time of use" [@Wray2002, p. 9]; the implications of formulaicity for second language acquisition are surveyed in @SiyanovaChanturiaPellicerSanches2019 and not reviewed here.

Two developments made this tradition directly relevant to stance research. First, @BiberConradCortes2004, comparing bundles in university classroom teaching and textbooks with conversation and academic prose, proposed the functional taxonomy, stance expressions, discourse organisers and referential expressions, that nearly all subsequent learner studies have inherited; they also show that bundles "function as basic building blocks of discourse" whose distribution tracks communicative purpose [@BiberConradCortes2004]. Second, the scope of bundle inventories was progressively documented for various regsiters. @BiberBarberi2007 extended the analysis across the full range of university spoken and written registers, finding that bundle density and functional composition track communicative purpose rather than mode alone, with stance bundles dominating speech-oriented registers and referential bundles dominating informational prose. @Hyland2008, analysing research articles and postgraduate theses across four disciplines, demonstrated that bundle inventories are strongly SOMETHING to discipline, with fewer than half of the top fifty bundles in any discipline occuring in any other. @SimpsonVlachEllis2010 complemented this line methodologically, deriving an academic formulas list from frequency, association measures and expert judgement, with separate spoken and written components. The cummulative lesson of this tradition suggests that phraseological patterning is conditioned by register at every level, so that what counts as a nativelike stance expression is a moving target defined by community practice. The bundle approach has also been turned directly on stance itself: @DeCockGranger2021 contrast the recurrent sequences of press releases with those of business news reports, showing that stance bundles differentiate the evaluative profiles of news-adjacent register, the closest precedent for the analytic unit adopted in this thesis. ### 4.6 Learner-native differences in lexical bundles

When the bundle method is turned on learner writing, the learner-native differences documented in Section 4.2 reappear at the level of recurrent sequences. An important antededent predates the bundle terminology itself. Working with comparable corpora of native and learner speech, @DeCock1998 extracted recurrent word combinations and classified them functionally, showing that advanced learners rely heavily on formulae while commanding a differently distributed repertoire. The companion analysis of learner writing esatblished the finding in its canonical form: advanced learners use as many prefabricated sequences as native speakers, and sometimes more, but the sequences they prefer are not the same ones, are not used with the same frequency, and do not serve the same syntactic and pragmatic functions [@DeCockEtAl1998]. De Cock's [-DeCock2000] extenstion to writing added the register dimension: advanced EFL production is repetitively formulaic in both speech and writing, with a less marked difference between the modes than native speakers display, an early corpus demonstration of the register insensitivity discussed in Section 3.4.

The fullest statement of this early line is De Cock's [-@DeCock2004] comparison of fifty French-speaking learners from the Louvain International Database of Spoken English Interlanguage (LINDSEI) with the Louvain Corpus of Native English Conversation (LOCNEC corpus). Quantitatively, learner speech proved repetitive, drawing on relatively few recurrent sequence types; qualitatively, the sequences the learners preferred were less interactional and less involved than those of the native speakers. Where native speakers signalled intersubjective alignment with vagueness tags such as *sort of*, *kind of* and *and things like that*, the learners reached for the more formal *and so on* and *etcetera*, underused *I mean*, and deployed *of course* in non-idomatic ways. Learners, she concludes, lack "routinized ways of interacting and building rapport with their interlocutors and of toning down and weaving the right amount of imprecisson and vagueness [@DeCock2004, p. 243]. 

De Cock's functional classification of recurrent sequences anticipated the taxonomy that @BiberConradCortes2004 would establishbas the standard apparatus of bundle research, with its division into stance expressions, discourse organisers and referential expressions. Once that taxonomy was in place, learner-native comparisons were recast in its terms. @AdelErman2012 compared four-word bundles in advanced Swedish learners' and native speakers' undergraduate linguistic essays, finding that the native writers commanded a lsrger and more varied repertoire of bundle types, including hedging and negation bundles, and drew more heavily on participant-oriented stance bundles, while the learners' narrower repertoir was skewed towards discourse organising frames. @ChenBaker2010 triangulated the comparison across three corpora, published academic prose, L1 student writing and Chinese L2 student writing: published writing exhibited the widest bundle range and L2 writing the narrowest, with the L2 writers overusing informal expressions rarely found in expert prose while underusing high-frequency expert frames, and relying more on verb phrase based bundles and discourse organisers than either native group. @BychkovskaLee2017 supply a partial dissent??: in high-rated argumentative essays by L1 Chinese and L1 English undergraduates, the L2 writers used substantially more bundle types and tokens, preferred clausal over phrasal bundles, and produced significantly more stance bundles, a pattern the authors attribute partly to register mixing and direct transfer from the L1. Read against @ChenBaker2010 and @AdelErman2012, the contrast suggests that the direction of learner-native bundle differences is itself sensitive to task and population, which strengthens rather than weakens the calibrarion argument: gross quantity is no discriminator. Extending the design developmentally, @ChenBaker2016 tracked bundles in learner essays rated at CEFR B1, B2 and C1, showing that bundle repertoires discriminate across proficiency levels, with lower-rated essays leaning disproportinately on a small set of high-frequency frames. Assessment data complicate the developmental picture in a compatible waay: in TOEFL iBT writin responses, lower-rated writers used more bundles overall, including more bundles reproduced from the prompts themselves, while the functional distribution of bundles remained broadly similar across levels [@StaplesEtAl2013].

The field surveyed in this section can be summarised in terms of what is now reasonably well established. ## 5. Japanese learners and sources of variation

Sections 2 to 4 of this chapter have argued that learner-native comparisons depend on comparable data, that register is a substantial source of variation in learner production, and that current knowledge of second-language (L2) stance and phraseology derives overwhelmingly from academic or otherwise controlled writing. The present section narrows the fouce from learner language in general to the population at the centre of this thesis: Japanese learners of English a a foreign language (EFL). The aim is not to characterise a homogenous "Japanese learner"; a construct that the critique of contrastive rhetoric has rendered untenable [@Kubota1999], but to do three more limited things: establish what corpus research has documented about this population, identify the explanations that compete whenever it is compared with native-speaker data, and assess how far findings drawn largely from argumentative essays and elicited speech extend to informal, online, evaluative discourse.

### 5.1 Japanese learners in learner corpus research: a well-studied population

Japanese learners constitute one of the most intensively corpus-studied EFL populations in the world [TOO STRONG??], and it is important to be precise about the nature of the gap this thesis tries to address: the problem is less an absence of research but more the restricted register base on which that research rests. The Japanese component of the International Corpus of Learner English (ICLE) placed Japanese university students' argumentative writing within the first large-sacle multinational learner corpus [@GrangerEtAl2009] and underpins early findings on connectors and pharseology discussed below [@Kaneko2007; @NaritaSatoSugiura2004]. At the school level, the Japanese EFL Learner (JEFLL) corpus comprises 10,038 short in-class compositions produced under controlled conditions (twenty minutes, no dicitonary, fixed topics) across the six years of Japanese secondary schooling, providing a quasi-longitudinal record of early instructed English development [@Tono2007]. At the university level, the International Corpus Network of Asian Learners of English (ICNALE) supplies written and spoken modules collected under strictly standardised conditions, with fixed prompts, proficiency information for every contributor, and matched native-speaker data (@Ishikawa2013; @Ishikawa2023]. Spoken proficiency is served by the NICT JLE Corpus of 1,281 rated oral proficiency interviews [@IzumiEtAl2004], and Miki's [-@Miki2010] work compares learner essays with their native-speaker corrections. Claims about Japanese learners can this in principle be tested against large, well-documented datasets spanning school and university, speech and writing, and beginner to advanced proficiency.

### 5.2 Japanese learner proficiency and corpus research

A second well-developed strand concerns proficiency, which the major corpora operationalise differently, each solution carrying consequences for interpretation. ICNALE assigns writers to four bands linked to the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR), but those bands are derived by converting heterogeneous external measures such as TOEFL, TOIEC and IELTS scores or the Vocabulary Size Test, so they are best described as CEFR-linked rather than independently rated [Ishikawa2013]. JEFLL exploits the near-universal start of English instruction in the first year of secondary schooling, so that school year functions as a coarse but clean proxt for instructional exposure [@Tono2007], while the NICT JLE corpus relies on trained ratings from a standardised speaking test [@IzumiEtAl2004]. Corpus-based developmental research on this population is correspondingly mature, beginning with Tono's [-@Tono2000] analysis of the acquisition order of English grammatical morphemes in Japanese learner writing. Extending to recent large-scale work, @Suzuki2024 analysed over a thousand learners across spoken and written corpora, and finds that the variety and frequency of epistemic stance devices increase with proficiency, but in a non-linear fashion complicated by the multifunctionality of the devices themselves.

The implication for the present thesis is that proficiency is a live alternative explanation or any learner-native difference in stance or phraseology. @Miki2010 shows that the overuse of *I think* diminishes across TOEIC bands, with advanced writers deploying the expression more selectively at points in persuasive prose where it marks the writer's position on a controversial issue. @Ishikawa2021 similarly reports a substantial gap between the vocabulary Japanese and other Asian learners are estimated to know (around 3,300 words on average) and the considerably smaller set they deploy in writing, constraining how any apparent phraseological restriction should be interpreted. The proficiency profile of the participants in the present study, and the procedures used to establish it, are accordingly treated as design variables in their own right and are described in Chapter 4.

### 5.3 L1 Influence and contrastive rhetoric

The oldest and most contested explanation for distinctive features of Japanese learner writing is first language (L1) influence, and its intellectual home is contrastive rhetoric. @Kaplan1966 initiated the field with the claim that "Logic (in the popular, rather the logician's sense of the word) which is the basis of rhetoric, is evolved out of a culture; it is not universal" (p. 2). proposing patterns of paragraph development that are culture specific, including a famously indirect pattern attributed to "Oriental" writing. @Connor1996 consolidated this tradition into a research programme that sought to explain second-language composing difficulties by reference to the rhetorical strategies of the first language, and @Hinds1987 supplied its most influential claim as regards Japanese discourse: that Japanese prose is "reader-responsible", leaving interpretive work to the audience, in contrast to "writer-responsible" English. @Connor2002 subsequently repositioned the field as intercultural rhetoric, broadening its methods in response to the critiques.

These critiques, however, substantially qualify what contrastive rhetoric can license for the present study. @Kubota1997, [-@Kubota1999] showed empirically that direct, deductive organisational patterns coexist with inductive onew n Japanese expository writing, and argued that the image of a uniquely indirect Japanese rhetoric is a discourse construct rather than a descriptive fact. @KubotaLehner2004 and @Atkinson2004 generalised the objecti0on, contending that contrastive rhetoric has operated with static, essentialised cultural dichotomies that obscure variation within cultures, across genres and over time [see also @Leki1991]. Empirically, transfer itself appears partial rather than wholesale: @Kubota1998 found that Japanese university students carried some organisational features from L1 to L2 writing but by no means all. The constructive alternative is Matsuda's [-@Matsuda1997] dynamic model, in which L2 text feature emerge from the interaction of L1, proficiency, genre knowledge, instruction and individual variation. The position adopted in this thesis is accordingly a qualified one. Japanese L1 background is treated as one plausible explanatory factor among several, not as the first link in an inference from Japanese learners to Japanese rhetorical culture to distinctive stane phraseology; any putative L1 effect must survive comparison with the alternatives examined in Section 5.5, and the study's design, set out there and in Section 6.4, attempts to distinguish learner-related differences from register-related and other sources of variation.

### 5.4 Japanese learners' discourse-pragmatic and stance choices

The evidence most directly relevant to this thesis concerns how Japanese learners deploy specific linguistic resources: hedging devices, assertive and self-mention devices, organsiational connectors, evalautive language and recurrent phraseological sequences. This subsection organises that evidence by resource rather than by study. Two things should be clarified here:  the evidence base consists almost entirely of argumentative essays and elicited speech, so it would be a stretch to generalise to other registers; the findings themselves admit several explanations, so they are reported here in descriptive terms, with interpretation to follow in Section 5.5.

#### 5.4.1 Hedging and epistemic modality

A recurring finding is a narrow epistemic repertoire (which is admitedly sometimes mismatched by register). @HollmannFujimotoKuroda2024 examined the modal verbs *may*, *might* and *could* in Japanese undergraduates' academic writing and found that *could* was used with high frequency, but often informally an insufficientlu in its epistemice-possibility sense; notably, their central interpretive frame is register, following Gilquin & Paquot's [-@GilquinPaquot2008] account of learner writing as "too chatty", rather than L1 influence. @Suzuki2024, applying a Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL) value framework to spoken and written corpora, concurs with earlier studies that Japanese learners rely on epistemic adverbs and lexical verbs (such as *I think*, *I guess* and *maybe*) rather than modal verbs to realise epistemic modality, and that small repertoires concentrate on a few high-frequency items, especially at lower proficiency. @Pemberton2020, analysing a purpose-built corpus of 190 handwritten argumentative essays, adds two findings that complicate cultural explanations: the frequency of modal devices varied by topic, and learners used more hedges than boosters on every topic, a pattern that correlated with the school curriculum rather than any cultural variable. Taken together, these studies may suggest that Japanese learners hedge, but with a restricted inventory whose deployment is sensitive to topic, instruction and register rather than to culture alone.

#### 5.4.2 Boosting, direct assertion and writer visibility

Counter to the stereotype of the indirect, non-commital Japanese writer, several corpus studies report overuse of assertive and self-mention devices. @Kobayashi2016, comparing six L1 groups in ICNALE using a Hyland-style metadiscourse taxonomy [@Hyland2005??], found that Japanese learners used self-mentions, boosters, frame markers and attitude markers more frequently than other groups, explicitly against the prediction that writers from a "reader-responsible" language background should underuse such markers. The single most studied item is *I think*. @McCrostie2008 documents its overuse, particularly in sentence-initial position, in a corpus of over 300 Japanese university essays, and @Miki2010, using native-speaker corrections as the comparison baseline, confirms significant overuse across proficiency bands while cautioning against a purely transfered-based account. "Not all of the categorical misuses by the Japanese learners are due to L1 transfer; they are lacking in the knowledge of the proper uses of *I think*" (pp. 178-179). @Natsukari2012 adds a functional qualification, arguing that learners' *I think* is often closer in force to native-speaker *I believe*, a marker of committed stance rather than a hedge.  The compostite picture is one of calibration rather than avoidance: stance is expressed, but sometimes at the wrong strength, in the wrong position or in the wrong register.


#### 5.4.3 Discourse organisation

The classic connector study is @NaritaSatoSugiura2004, who compared the ICLE Japanese subcorpus with LOCHNESS and found that Japanese learners "significantly oversue the logical connectors in sentence initial positions", overusing connectors such as *for example*, *of course* and *first*, while underusing *then*, *yet* and *instead* (p. 1174). Crucially for the question of competing questions, a parallel comparison with French, Swedish and Chinese learner data showed that the four groups share a common core of over- and underused connectors, with [L1-specific residue superimposed]??; the authors attribute the overuse largely to learners' incomplete grasp of the distinction between adverbial connectors and conjunctions, offering a developmental or instructional explanation rather than one derived from a cultural-rhetorical background. This pattern, in which an apparently Japanese feature turns out to be shared across L1 groups, recurs throughout this section, and is central to Section 5.5.

#### 5.4.4 Evaluation and interpersonal positioning

Evaluative and affective resources have been less extensively studied than modality, but the available evidence again resists the deficit-and-indirectness stereotype. @Kobayashi2016 reports overuse of attitude markers alongside boosters and self-mentions in Japanese ICNALE esssays, and @KamimuraOi1998 identified differences in argumentative strategies between American and Japanese students' English essays, including the use of generalisations and of *I think* as a softening rather than emphatic device, locating group differences in how evaluation is packaged rather than whether it is present. Evidence from informal online interaction remains extremely thin: @Matusoka2021 reports that Japanese EFL learners in online forum discussion used a limited range of modal functions for social interaction, with certain pragmalinguistic norms appearing to have stabilised, but this is a small conference-scale?? study and stands virtually alone as corpus evidence from an online register in Japan [MARCHAND AKUTSU??].

#### 5.4.5 Phraseology and recurrent sequences

Finally phraseological research suggests that Japanese recurrent sequences form a restricted repertoire that is partially distinctive due to L1. @Ishikawa2021, analysing trigrams in the ICNALE edited essays across four Asian L1 groups, found that learners overused sequences built around basic verbs, bare singular nouns an certain logical connectors, while underusing subjunctive and epistemic modal constuctions; of particular relevance here, Japanese learners were distinctive in their high-frequency trigram profiles. @Kaneko2007, in an early small-scale study of two- to four-word clusters in Japanese English majors' writing, likewise described a restricted repertoire in which a few function words combine around a core content word; given its scale, its findings are best treated as suggestive. Whether such repertoires persist in, or are reshaped by, informal evaluative registers is unknown, because the phraseological studies reviewed here and in Section 4 all concern academic or argumentative writing.

### 5.5 L1, proficiency, task, register, topic and time as competing explanations

The studies reviewed above share a methodological vulnerability that matters directly for the design of the present thesis: each documents a difference between Japanese learner output and some native-speaker baseline, yet the explanations on offer are rarely pitted against one another within a single design. This subsection therefore confronts the inferential question on which the whole thesis turns: if a learner-native difference is observed, what may legitimately be inferred from it? To make the problem concrete, consider a hypothetics finding [OR OBSERVED ON??] of the kind that the present study might produce: a corpus of Japanese learners' online news comments shows marked underuse, relative to the BBB *Have Your Say* reference corpus, of a particular recurrent stance bundle, for example a concessive or hedged evaluative frame characteristic of native comment discourse. At least six explanations are available, each with prima facie support in the literature reviewed in this chapter.
First, **L1 influence**: the function the bundle serves in English comment discourse may be realised by different rhetorical means in Japanese, and learners may transfer those means, or avoid the function altogether [@Connor1996]. Against this, transfer has repeatedly shown to be partial [@Kubota1998], and patterns attributed to Japanese rhetoric have turned out to be shared across unrelated L1 groups [@NaritaSatoSugiura] [PLUS more  CITATIONS SUPPORTING TRANSFER]?.Second, **proficiency**: the bundle may simply be beyond the learners' current repertoire, since epistemic and phraseological repertoires expand, non-linearly, with proficiency [@Ishikawa2021; @Suzuki2024], and even robust overuses such as *I think* attenuate across proficiency bands [@Miki2010]. Third, **task**: the conditions under which the comments were produced, whether timed or untimed, prompted or spontaneous, individually written or classroom-assigned, measurably alter linguistic output [@WayJoinerSeaman2000], and the learners' production conditions almost certainly differed from those of the native commenters. Fourth, **register**: the learners may be producing a different register from the reference corpus, or an informalised version of the target register; learner writing is pervasively "too chatty" [@GilquinPaquot2008], and Japanese learners' epistemic choices in particular have been characterised as register-inappropriate rather than ungrammatical [@HollmannFuhimotoKuroda2024]. Fifth, **topic**: stance and modality frequencies vary with subject matter, as @Pemberton2020 demonstrates directly for Japanese learners, whose modal usage tracked topic and curriculum rather than culture, and as topic effects on syntactic and lexical measurees confirm more generally [@YangLuWeigle2015]. Sixth, **time**: the two corpora differ in their period of data collection, and registers are not static. The BBC comments date from the early 2000s, and some part of any learner-native difference may reflect diachronic drift in the register itself, or in the wider ecology of online discourse, rather than any property of the learners. This is one reason why Chapter 2 framed the BBC corpus as a register benchmark rather than a matched control (see 2.3.3).

It is here that @LarssonPaquotBiber2021 can again help us in this chapter: beyond establishing that register is a major source of variation in learner writing (Section3.3), their additive multidimensional analysis modelled L1 background and register as co-predictors across five L1 groups and multiple written registers. Their demonstration that significant differences emerge both between writer groups and across registers implies that in the case of Japanese learners, register cannot simply be acknowledged and set aside: it must be actively disentangled from L1 proficiency. Concretely, an L1 account requires evidence that the effect is specific to Japanese learners relative to other L1 groups of comparable proficiency writing in the same register; a proficiency account requires that bundle use vary with proficiency within the Japanese sample; task and topic accounts require comparisons across tasks and topics; and ruling in or out requires, at minimum, that learner and reference corpora represent the same register, the comparability requirement outlined in Section 2, supplemented where possible by situating both corpora among a wider range of native registers. A time-based explanation, finally, is mitigated by treating the BBC data explicitly as a benchmark of the register at a defined historical moment, rather than as a timeless native norm. ICNALE's design, with fixed prompts, controlled conditions and proficiency bands for ten Asian L1 groups [@Ishikawa2013], shows what such disentangling looks like for argumentative writing; no equivalent design exists for informal online evaluative discourse. ### 5.6 Synthesis

Japanese learners of English are among the most extensively studied EFL populations in learner corpus research, supported by large, well-documented resources spanning school and university, speech and writing, and multiple proficiecny levels [@GrangerEtAl2009; @Ishikawa2013; @IzumiEtAl2004; @Tono2007]. This research has documented differences, relative to native-speaker norms, in vocabulary development, grammatical development, discourse organisation, stance marking and phraseological patterning; crucially, the strongest studies simultaneously supply the counter-evidence that undermines single-cause accounts: connector overuse is shared across L1 groups [@NaritaSatoSugiura2004], modality tracks topic and curriculum rather than culture [@Pemberton2020], *I think* overuse reflects incomplete register knowledge as much as transfer [@Miki], and Japanese learners tend to overuse, rather than avoid, assertive and self-mention devices [@Kobayashi2016]. The contrastive-rhetoric tradition that once offered a deterministic cultural explanation for such findings has been thoroughly qualified [@Atkinson2004; @Kubota1997; @Kubota1999; @KubotaLehner2004], leaving L1 as one influence among many [@Matsuda1997]. The pervasive limitation is one of register coverage: virtually everything known about this population comes from argumentative essays, examination conditions  or elicited speech, and Japan-specific evidence from informal online interaction remains confined to a handful of small-scale studies [@Matsuoka2021]. Whether the patterns documented in academic and controlled writing extend to informal, online, evaluative discourse, are attenuated there, or are transforemed, remains unclear.

## 6. Synthesis: From learner corpus research to learner stance in ONC

### 6.1 What Chapter 2 established

Chapter 2 characterised online news comments (ONC) as a distinctive evaluative written register: public, asynchronous and unedited discourse organised around reaction to news events and interpersonal evaluation, occupying a hybrid position between casual conversation and the news article to which it responds [@EhretTaboada2021]. On that basis, the chapter formulated the thesis's first gap: despite growing register-analytic?? description of online discourse, there exists no register-specific inventory of the recurrent phraseology through which stance is realised in ONC. The present chapter has introduced the complication that defines the thesis as a whole. The ONC data under investigation were not written (or moderated by) by native speakers, but by Japanese learners of English as a foreign language (EFL), so the register-level gap of Chapter 2 must be pursued through the comparative logic?? of learner corpus research, with all the interpretive contraints that logic imposes.

### 6.2 What Chapter 3 has established: a chain of propositions

The argument of this chapter can now be stated as a chain of six propositions, each defended in the preceding sections and each a premise fot the next.

First, learner corpus research (LCR) establishes the value of systematic learner-native speaker comparison. By confronting?? learner production with native-speaker production at scale, Constrastive Interlangauge Analysis (CIA) makes visible quantitative divergences, overuse and underuse of perfectly well-formed items, that error analysis was structurally unable to detect [@Granger1996; @Granger1998book]. Seconc, CIA shows that such comparison is only as strong as its reference population: the native corpus is a sample of what a defined community of users does in a defined situation, not an idealised targer [@Granger2015; @Gilquinn2022], which is why the BBC *Have Your Say* corpus functions in this thesis as a register-specific benchmark rather than a prescriptive model. Third, register research shows that learner production varies substantially with communicative context. Multidimensional studies repeatedly find register and tasks effects of the same order as, or larger than, proficiency and first langauge (L1) effects [@GardnerNesiBiber2019; @LarssonPaquotBiber2021; @StaplesReppen2016], and learners themselves show partial but real register awareness [@LarssonKaatari2020; @QinUccelli2020]. Fourth, L2 stance research shows systemic learner-native differences in interpersonal and epistemic resources, differences located in the calibration of commitment rather than its quantity: restricted repertoires, stronger and less precisely graded claims, and simultaneous over- and under-marking [@HylandMilton1997; @Adel2006; @AullLancaster2014]. Fifth, phraseological research shows that these differences extend to recurrent multiword sequences: learners command narrower and structurally different bundle repertoires whose composition varies with proficiency and register [@ChenBaker2010; @ChenBaker2016; @AdelErman2012; @BiberBarbieri2007]. Sixth, Japanese learner research identifies L1 and proficiency relevant, competing sources of variation, and repeatedly demonstrates that apparently L1-specific features are shared across unrelated L1 groups, attenuate with proficiency, or track topic and curriculum rather than culture [@Miki2010; @NaritaSatoSugiura2004; @Pemberton2020].

Taken together, the six propositions yield this chapter's central conclusion. Every explanation available for learner-native difference in stance phraseology, whether transfer, proficiency, task or topic, is sentsitive to register, and register effects are independently large. Therefore register cannot be treated as a background variable when interpreting learner-native differences in stance phraseology: it is a constitutive condition of their interpretation.

### The double gap

The conclusion of Chapter 2 and the conclusion of the present chapter intersect. Chapter 2 establishes a registter gap: the phraseological realisation of stance in online news commenting is not adequately understood, and no complete account exists [TOO MUCH??] of how native commenters habitually express evaluation, commitment and reader orientation through recurrent sequences. The present chapter establishes a learner-context gap: research on L2 stance and stance phraseology, including the extensive work on Japanese learners [@Ishikawa2013; @Ishikawa2023; @Tono2007], has concentrated on academic or otherwise controlled writing, so that findings from those registers cannot be assumed to generalise to informal online evaluation. The thesis addresses the intersection of the two gaps, asking how Japanese learners realise stance phraseologically when producing in the ONC register. Stated as a single problem: *the phraseological realisation of stance by Japanese learners in an informal evaluative online register has not been adequately investigated.* Investigating it requires addessing both gaps at once, since a register-aware description of native ONC stance phraseology is the benchmark against which learner usage becomes interpretable at all.

### 6.4 Methodological implications for Chapter 4

The double gap translates into six explicit requirements that the design reported in Chapter 4 must satisfy.

1. **Register comparability.** Because register effects can rival or exceed learner-status effects [@LarssonPaquotBiber2021], the learner and native corpora must represent the same broad register. The comparison is accordingly between Japanese learners' online news comments and native-speaker online news comments, not between learner comments and native academic or journalistic prose.
2. **Task comparability** Production condition shape linguistic output independently of register [@Granger1996]. The communicative conditions under which the learner comments were produced must therefore be documented and, where possible, rendered comparable to those of the native data, with residual differences acknowledged rather than concealed.
3. **Learner comparability.** Since L1 and proficiency are live competing explanations [@Miki2010; @Suzuki2024], the learner corpus must have both variables fully documented, so that both can be controlled and examined rather than assumed.
4. **An appropriate reference population.** Consistent with the CIA2 reinterpretation of the native norm [@Granger2015; @Gilquin2022], the BBC *Have Your Say* corpus is used as a register-matched benchmark documenting what a defined native population actually did in the target register, not as an abstract native-English model, or a matched experimental control.
5. **Phraseological analysis.** Because learner-native differences manifest in recurrent combinations and not only in isolated items [@ChenBaker2010; @AdelErman2012], the analysis must capture recurrent multiword sequences rather than relying on counts of individual stance markers alone.
6. **Functional interpretation.** Frequency differences are evidentially inert until interpreted against discourse function [cf. @HylandMilton1997; @ChenBaker2016]: overuse or underuse of a form must be read in terms of what the form does in ONC, and raw counts alone cannot decide among the competing explanations reviewed in Section 5.

These requirements jointly operationalise the comparability principle defended in Section 2: a learner-native difference is attributable to learner status only to the extent that other major sources of variation have been controlled or accounted for [@ConnorMoreno2005].

### Handoff to Chapter 4

Chapter 3 established that online discourse cannot be treated as a homogeneous category and that online news commenting is a communicative environment with distinctive register characteristics. The present chapter has shown that learner language likewise cannot be interpreted independently of the register in which it is produced. If register influences the linguistic resources selected by native speakers and learners alike, then learner-native differences can only be meaningfully interpreted when the register under comparison is held as constant as possible. The research problem is therefore not simply whether Japanese learners differ from native speakers, but whether and how they differ while participating in the same communicative register.

The three literatures?? reviewed in this chapter supply the components of the required response: learner corpus research supplies the comparative framework and its interpretive cautions, register research the situational controls, and stance and phraseology research the analytical level at which learner-native differences in evaluation and epistemic commitment become visible. They are individually well developed, but they have not been jointly applied to informal, evaluative online discourse produced by Japanese learners. Chapter 4 describes the corpora and analytical design through which this thesis does so: it details the construction of the six-cohort learner corpus and the BBC *Have Your Say* reference corpus, and it sets out a two-stage analysis combining an additive multidimensional analysis (MDA) that siutates both corpora among a wider range of nataive registers with the identification of stance bundles, recurrent multiword sequences retrieved through recurrent tagged stance features, namely evaluative adjectives, stance adverbs and suasive verbs [PLUS ANY OTHERS??].
