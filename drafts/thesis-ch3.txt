## 1. Introduction: The learner dimension of the ONC problem

### 1.1 From Chapter 2 to the learner corpus

As outlined in Chapter 1, research on computer-mediated commincation (CMC) and online discourse provides the basis, through register theory, for treating online news commenting as a distinct communicative environment. The central conclusion of that chapter was that online news comments (ONC) would appear to constitute a distinctive evaluative written register: publice, asynchronous texts in which lay readers react to news events, characterised by involved, stance-heavy features that set them apart from both the news reports they respond to, and from casual conversation [@BiberConrad2009; @BiberEgbert2018; @EhretTaboada2021]. Chapter 2 also identified the first of the two gaps motivating this thesis: although the register may be defined by evaluation, it would seem that no register-specific inventory of its recurrent stance phraseology has yet been compiled, i.e. of the multi-word sequences built around tagged stance features such as evaluative adjectives, stance adverbs and suasive verbs - the means through which evaluation is conventionally expressed in comment discourse. [CLOSING SENTENCE]

The present chapter turns to the second dimension of the study: learner language. In reviewing Learner Corpus Research (LCR), this chapter seeks to establish how learner-native differences hve tradionally been investigated and, crucially, why such comparisons require attention to register. The corpus as the heart of this thesis introduces a complication that Chapter 2 left undeveloped: the data under study are learner-produced, comprising six cohorts of online news comments written by Japanese university students learning Engish as a foreign language (EFL). With learner texts, the descriptive question of Chapter 2 can no longer be separated from an interpretive one. Learner language is known to diverge systematically from native-speaker usage even when no error is present, though the quantitative overuse and underuse of perfectly well-formed items [@Granger1998chapter; @Granger2003]; the interpretation of any such divergence, however, depends on what is known about the learner language, about the register, and about the learner population in question.

Given this complication, the task of the present chapter is to establish the framework within which a learner-native comparison of ONC stance phraseology can be interpreted, identifying the competing explanations that must be addressed before a difference may be attributed to learner status, to first language (L1), or to register knowledge. In doing so, the chapter establishes the thesis's second gap: research on second language (L2) stance and phraseology, including the substantial body of work on Japanese learners, has been conducted almost entirely on academic or otherwise controlled writing, and the intersection of learner language, stance, phraseology and informal online evaluation appears to remain insufficiently understood.

## 1.2 Why learner-native comparison is not straightforward

When learner language differs from native language, the question of *what* is responsible is not a straightforward one. Such differences may reflect the learners' L1, through the transfer of rhetorical or linguistic habits; their proficiency or developmental stage; the task conditions under which the texts were produced; the genre or register sampled; the topic; the period of data collection; or the learners' educational background and prior instruction. @Granger1996 insisted from the outset of Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis (CIA) that such external variables should be controlled, so that comparisons are made "like with like", and SOMETHING has since proved consequential: proficiency is "best described as a fuzzzy variable" in learner corpora [@Carlsen2012], task and prompt conditions measurably alter learner output [@AlexopoulouEtAl2017], and register effects in learner writing would appear to rival or exceed those of writer status or L1 [@LarssonPaquotBiber2021].

A worked example may serve to show how these explanations confound. [EXAMPLE HYPOTHETICAL - TO BE REPLACED WITH REAL DATA LATER]. Suppose the comparison finds that the Japanese learners'  comments contain a particular hedged evaluative frame, say a recurrent sequence built around a stance adverb qualifying an evaluative adjective, at a normalised frequency of 6.2 occurences per 10,000 words, against 11.8 in the BBC reference corpus. One reading attributes the gap to proficiency: epistemic and phraseological repertoires are known to be narrower at lower proficiency and to widen as learners advance [@ChenBaker2016; @HylandMilton1997], so the learners may simply not yet command the device. A second reading attributes it to register: learner writing is pervasively prone to drift toward informal, speech-like patterning?? [@GilquinPaquot2008], so the learners may be producing an informalised variant of the comment register, in which case the frequency difference measures register mismatch rather than learner deficit. A third reading invokes the L1, a fourth the classroom conditions of collection, a fifth the topics addressed, and a sixth the period separating the two data collections. The arithmetic of 6.2 versus 11.8 is identical under all six readings; nothing in the raw difference itself decides among them.

What discriminates among the readings is, therefore, design rather than arithmetic. A proficiency explanation requires evidence that use of the frame varies with proficiency within the learner corpus, which the six-cohort structure of the present data can supply; a register explanation requires that the two corpora demonstrably represent the same situational variety; and L1, task, topic and time explanations require corresponding internal comparisons or explicit acknowledgement. The comparability principle adopted in Section 2, that a learner-native difference is attributable to learner status only to the extent that other major sources of variation have been controlled or accounted for [@ConnorMoreno2005], is thus the condition under which corpus frequencies become evidence at all. It should be noted, however, that the two corpora compared in this thesis also differ in period and collection circumstances, which is why Chapter 2 framed the BBC data as a benchmark rather than a matched control: the comparison describes how the learners' production relates to the register, not its distance from an idealised native target.

### 1.3 The argument of the chapter

The argument developed across this chapter rests on four governing propositions. First, Learner Corpus Research establishes the value of systematic learner-native comparison: confronting large collections of authentic learner production with native data makes visible the overuse and underuse patterns that error analysis could not detect [@Granger1996; @Granger2003; @Granger2015]. Secondly, such comparisons require an appropriate reference corpus and sustained attention to competing sources of variation: the native corpus functions as a reference population rather than a prescriptive norm, and L1 composition, proficiency and task conditions must be documented or controlled before differences are interpreted [@ConorMoreno2005; @Gilquin2022; @Granger2015]. Thirdly, register, genre and task substanitally affect learner language, so learner-native comparison must be sensitive to register: linguistic choice varies systematically with the situational context in all language users [@Biber1988; @BiberConrad2009], learner production would appear to be no exception, and comparisons across mismatched registers risk attributing register effects to learner status [@LarssonPaquotBiber2021]. Finally, existing L2 stance and phraseology research, including research on Japanese learners, has focused overwhelmingly on academic or controlled writing [@ChenBaker2010; @ChenBaker2016; @HylandMilton1997; @AdelErman2012], and the major Japanese learner corpora are likewise built on essays and elicited speech [@GrangerEtAl2009; @Ishikawa2013; @Tono2007]. The intersection of learner language, stance, phraseology, and informal online evaluation remains correspondingly under-described.

The thesis of the chapter follows directly from these propositions: register may be regarded as a necessary condition for interpreting learner-native differences, since every alternative explanation for such differences (L1, proficiency, development, task, topic and time) operates within, and interacts with, the register in which the language was produced.

The chapter develops this argument in four steps, before bringing them all together in a final synthesis. Section 2 reviews Learner Corpus Research and its comparative method, examining the two configuations of CIA, the interpretation of learner-native differences, the design variables that condition comparability, and the progression from pioneering examples like the International Corpus of Learner English (ICLE) and its native reference corpus, the more tightly controlled examples like the International Corpus Network of Asian Learners of English (ICNALE). Section 3 develops the chapter's central claimm, that register is a necessary variable in learner corpus research, drawing on multidimensional analyses of learner writing and on the academic-register bias of the field's SOMETHING. Section 4 turns to stance and its phraseological realisation in L2 writing, reviewing what is known about learners' epistemic calibration, lexical bundle use and phraseological development. Section 4 will also explore what this research cannot tell us, concentrated as it is on institutional genres. Section 5 narrows the focus to Japanese learners, reviewing corpus evidence on their stance and phraseological choices and setting out the complete explanations (L1, proficiency, task, register, topic and time) that any learner-native comparison of this population might disentangle. Finally, Section 6 draws the strands together and outlines the double gap that emerges when the learner-side gap established here is coupled with Chapter 1's register gap. It concludes with the methodological requirements that Chapter 4 must meet to answer that SOMETHING.

## 2. Learner Corpus Research and Comparative Logic

This thesis compares a corpus of online news comments written by Japanese learners of English as a foreign language (EFL) with a reference corpus of native-speaker comments from the BBC Have Your Say forum. Any such comparison presupposes?? a methodological framework that specifies what may legitimately be compared, against which reference data, and under what interpretive constraints. This section therefore reviews Learner Corpus Research (LCR), the branch of corpus linguistics concerned with the systematic study of learner language, and its signature comparative method, Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis (CIA). The argument developed below is that learner-native differences cannot be meaningfully interpreted independently of register: the legitimacy of such a comparison depends on the comparability of both the populations and the situations in which their language was produced. This section traces the emergence of LCR (2.1), presents the two configurations of CIA and their relation to the register-based SOMETHING of Chapter 1 (2.2), examines how learner-native diferences should be interpreted (2.3), reviews the design variables that conndition comparability (2.4), and situates this study within the development of major learner corpora, such as the International Corpus of Learner English (ICLE), the Louvain Corpus of Native English Essays (LOCHNESS), and the International Corpus Network of Asian Learners of English (ICNALE) (2.5) [MORE EXAMLPES??).

### 2.1 The emergence of Learner Corpus Research

Before the advent of learner corpora, empirical evidence about second language (L2) production came largely from three sources, each with recognised limitations: introspection and elicitation, which reveal what learners can do under experimental conditionsa rather than what they do naturally; error analyis (EA), which reconstructed the learner's developing system from observable errors, revauled as evidence of transitional competence [@Corder1967]; and small, hand-collected datasets whose "considerable variation in the numbers of subjects, in the backgrounds of the subjects, and in the empirical data" [Odlin, 1989, p. 151, as cited in @Granger2003, p. 541] made cumulative generalisation difficult.

Error analysis, hoowever, was structurally blind to quantitative divergence. @Schachter1974 demonstrated that learners may avoid structure they find difficult, so that an error count captures only what learners attempt and fail, not what they decline to attempt at all. A learner might use a linguistic feature correctly but far more often, or far less often, than a native speaker would, and this phenomenon, later termed *overuse* and *underuse*, lay outside EA's descriptive reach [@Granger1998chapter; @Granger2002].

Learner Corpus Research emerged in the early 1990s as a systematic, corpus-based answer to these data problems. A computer learner corpus is defined as "an electronic collection of authentic texts produced by foreign or second language learners", assembled, like all corpora, "according to explicit design criteria", but requiring "extra care" in data collection "given the large number of variables affecting the learning/acquisition process" [@Granger2003, p. 538; cf. @Granger2002; @Nesselhauf2004]. The founding resource was the International Corpus of Learner English, first presented by @Granger1993 as a cross-l1 corpus of advanced learners' English essays in which the learner's first language (L1) was a central design variable [see also @Granger2024], and the edited volume *Learner English on Computer* [@Granger1998book] supplied early demonstrations of cross-linguistic lexical profiling and overuse/underuse analysis [@GrangerRayson1998; @Ringbom1998; @Lorenz1999].

CIA should be distinguished from both error analysis, including its computer-aided descendant [@DagneauxDennessGranger1998], which remains focused on the classification of non-target-like forms, and from corpus-based seccond language acquisition (SLA) research, which uses learner corpora to test acquisition hypotheses [@Myles2015; @TracyVenturaPaquotMyles2021]. It is a specifically comparative methodology defined by its systematic confrontation of learner data with native-speaker data, r with other learner data, and it supplies the comparative logic of the present study.

### Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis

Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis was introduced by @Granger1996 as the corpus-based counterpart of Contrastive Analysis: whereas Contrastive Analysis compared different languages, CIA was designed to establish comparisons "between native and interlanguage varieties (NL vs IL) and between interlanguage varieties (IL vs IL)" [@Granger2024, p. 247; see also @Granger2003, p. 541].

The first configuration, learner versus native-speaker comparison, uncovers "the patterns of use distinguishing learner data from native data", which "fall into two categories: qualitative differences (misuse) and quantitative differences (over- and underuse)" [@Granger2003, p. 541]. A text may thus contain few overt errors yet still produce a distinctly non-native impression because the frequency distribution of perfectly well-formed items diverges from native norms [@Granger1998chapter], as early studies of connector usage [@GrangerTyson1996] and adjective intensification [@Lorenz1999] demonstrated.

The second configuration, learner versus learner comparison, assesses the effects of the L1 (the classic transfer question),, proficiency, age, learning setting, medium and task type. @Granger1996 already underlined the importance of controlling such external variables, insisting that researchers compare "like with like" [Granger, 1996, as discussed in @Granger2002]. Because IL versus IL comparisons isolate these variables, they are indispensable before any observed pattern is attributed to learnerhood in general [@Granger2002]. The present study adopts the first configuration (Japanese EFL learners versus native speakers) while holding constant, as far as corpus design permits, the variables that the second configuration has shown to matter.

#### 2.2.1 CIA and the logic of Chapter 1

The parallel between CIA and the register-analytic framework establised in Chapter 1 is worth making explicit since the thesis combines the two. Register analysis, in the tradition of @BiberConrad2009, compares a target register against a reference register to identify lingusitic features functionally characteristic of a situation of use; CIA compares a learner corpus against an expert or native-speaker corpus to identify the features characteristic of learner production. Both are fundamentally comparative in design, and both are uninterpretable without an approrpiate comparison population: a register feature is only "characteristic" relative to other registers, just as a learner feature is only "overused" relative to some reference variety. This is the same premise that connects this chapter with the last chapter. Chapter 2 showed that a register cannot be identified except by comparison with other registers; the present chapter shows that language specific to a learner group cannot be interpreted except against an approrpiate comparison population. The two chapters thus echo each other in SOMETHING the importance of comparative design.

The two design are nevertheless not equivalent, and conflating them is a methodological risk best to be avoided. Register analysis targets situational variation: its axis of comparison is the situational context (purpose, channel, participants, topic9, and its populations may both consist of expert native users. CIA targers learner-status variation: its axis of comparison is the writer or speaker's status as either learner or native user, and its situations are ideally held constant. The distinction has a direct consequence for th design of the present study: if the learner corpus and the native-speaker reference corpus differ not only in learner status, but also in register, then any observed differences are ambiguous between the two axes. Chapter 2's characterisation of online news comments (ONC) as a distinctive evaluative written register therefore plays a double role in this thesis: it motivates the choice of the BBC *Have Your Say* data as a reference corpus, and it frames the interpretation of any learner-native difference that survives the matching.

### 2.3 Interpreting learner-native differences

Comparability of design does not by itself settle the prior question of what a learner-native difference means. This subsection reviews the debate over the native norm that will inform the interpretive stance of this thesis.

#### 2.3.1 The native corpus as reference population, not as idealised target

Early CIA studies were sometimes read, and occaisionally writtern, as if the native corpus represented an idealised target, and that every quantitative divergence from it a deficiency. This reading has been progressively abandoned within LCR itself. Granger's [-@Granger2015] reappraisal of the method she introduced two decades earlier is decisive here: the revised model, CIA2, replaces the single idealised native norm with multiple possible reference language varieties, and reconceives the two comparison types as informing each other bidirectionally. @Gilquin2022 systematises the point: while learner corpous studies "can dispense with a native norm, they usually rely on one", and choosing the most approporiate among the avaialble native and non-native norms is of crucial importance. The native corpus is thus best understood as a referenc population, a sample of what a defined community of users actually does in a defined situation, rather than as a standard of correctness.

This interpretation also reflects a fact about native reference corpora that is often underplayed: they are themselves samples from particular populations. LOCNESSS, the reference copus of the ICLE tradition, consist of student rather tham professional writing [@Granger1998book; see 2.5.2 below]; treating it as a prescriptive model would both overvalue native production and misrepresent the particular native population sampled.
