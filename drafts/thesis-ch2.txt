## Introduction: Online news comments as computer-mediated communication

### 1.1 Online news comments as a form of CMC: why study language below the line

The present thesis analyses a learner corpus of online news comments (ONC) written by Japanese university students of English across six cohorts, against a reference corpus of comments from the BBC's *Have Your Say* platform dating from the early 2000s. The object of study first requires situating: ONC are a form of computer-mediated communication (CMC), and CMC research supplies the context for a linguistic analysis of news commenting.

@Herring1996 \[p.1\] canonical definition describes CMC as "communication that takes place between human beings via the intrumentality of computers". @Herring2001 later distinguished *computer-mediated communication*, the socio-technical phenomenon, from *computer-mediated discourse* (CMD), the language produced through it, and it is the later term that is adopted here. Within the ecology of CMD, ONC occupy a distinctive niche: asynchronous, text-based, persistent, one-to-many, typically pseudonymous and editorially moderated messages append to professionally produced news texts [@Herring2001; @Herring2007].

The case for studying the discourse rests partly on scale: within a decade of its introduction, commenting became one of the most widespread forms of public writing in English, characterised as a new "opinion pipeline" running alongside, and sometimes against, official editorial opinion [@Santana2-11]. Yet news comments have been studied far more intensively by journalism and communication scholars than by linguists [see, for example, CITATIONS]: little is know about their register characteristics, genre structure, or stance patterning. This asymeetry motivates the two traditions reviewed in Section 2.2.

The chapter's ultimate problem can be stated explicitly at the outset: existing research establishes ONC as an evaluative written register, but it has not adequately described the recurrent lexico-grammatical phraseology through which stance is realised in this register. The chapter therefore proceeds as an accumulation of evidence toward that problem, assembling the theoretical and methodological resources that its resolution requires.

### 1.2 Two research tradtions: communication studies and linguistic approaches

Research on news commenting has proceeded along two largely paralle tracks. The dominant tradition, rooted in journalism and communication studies, treats comments as participartory media practice, investigated through surveys, newsroom ethnographies and content analysis [CITATION of a general review]. Early studies documented the uneasy integration of user-generated content into professional frameworks [@HermidaThurman2008; @Thurman2008; @DomingoEtAl2008]: commenting was embraced rhetorically but managed as a moderation burden, a "clash of cultures*, and participatory spaces remained structurally peripheral. A second wave of work coded large samples for civility and deliberative merit: anonymous comment boards are substantially more uncivil than identified ones [@Santana2014]; incivility is platform and design dependent rather than inherent to online talk [@Rowe2015]; and active journalist engagement in threads improves deliberative norms [@StroudEtAl2015]. @DiakopoulosNaaman2011, working computationally, analysed over a million *New York Times* comments to identify markers of high-quality discourse and inform moderation design. @Reader2012 counters that the industry's "civility" framing itself constrains free expression, and @KsiazekSpringer2018 synthesise this literature around civility, deliberation, and platform governance.

The linguistic tradition, by contrast, treats comments as discourse. Its methodological anchor is @Herring2004a computer-mediated discourse analysis (CMDA), a "language-focused content analysis" that adapts the concerns of discourse analysis (structure, meaning, interation management, social phenomena) to online language. Linguists have approached ONC through pragmatics, notably working on impoliteness in reader responses to British online news [@NeurauterKessels2011; see also @RababahAlali2020], and increasingly through corpus methods: KehoeGee2019 reveal stable topic-based communities in 6.2 million *Guardian* comments, @CollinsNerlich2015 apply corpus-driven analysis to climate-change threads, and @TrnavacTaboada2023 compare engagement markers in English and Russian comments. The two taditions are complementary: the first asks who comments, why, and with what democratic consequences; the second, what comments are like as language. They have nonetheless rarely been integrated. This thesis aligns with the linguistic tradition while drawing on the first for institutional context.
### 1.3 From hybrid register to new variety: early spoken-written cline studies

The first wave of linguistic CMC research askedd where electronic language sits on the continuum between speech and writing [@Baron1984], addressed empirically through @Biber1988 multi-dimensional model of spoken and written variation. @FerraraBrunnerWhittemore1991 described real-time computer conferencing as an "emergent register" blending speech and writing; @CollotBelmore1996 read "electornic langauge" against Biber's dimensions as a single new variety; @Ytes1996b placed computer conferencing between spoken and written norms; and @Crystal2001 went furthest: Internet language is "something fundamentally different from both writing and speaking" [@Crystal2006, p. 24].

These studies established that CMD is linguitically patterned and measurable, amd their comparative designs remain methodological models. Yet they presupposed a relatively homogenous object; variation *within* CMC soon proved to exceed variation between CMC and offline modes [CITATION]. CMC,in short, is not linguistically homogenous, and the analysis of any online variety must begin from its particular situation: that is the move made by @Herring2007 faceted scheme.

### 1.5 Genre evolution: from letters to the editor to below-the-line comments

A parallel line of research addresses the genesis of digital genres. @YatesOrlikowski1992 structural account posits?construes? genres as typified communicative actions that migrate across media:  communities enact a *genre repetoire* in which a new medium reconfigures existing genres [@OrlilowskiYates1994], distinguishing reproduced genres from emergent genres adapted to the Web [@CrowstonWilliams2000]. HerringEtAl2005 characterised the weblog as a "bridging genre", at once derivative and novel [see also @GiltrwoStein2009].

ONC fit this account neatly. Their functional ancestor is the letter to the editor: online commentaries are more personal, more dialogic, and less gatekept than printed letters [@LanertJucker2011; @Landert2014], and anonymous comments expressed more extreme and diverse opinions than moderated letters during the 2008 US immigration debate [@McCluskeyHmielowski2012]. ONC thus reproduce the letters-to-the-editor function with new immediacy, interactively, pseudonymity, and scale. Yet apparent demographic differences in blog language are largely mediated by sub-genre [@HerringPaolillo2006], so corpus comparisons, learner and native alike, are valid only when genre is held constant.

### 1.6 A short history of below-the-line commenting: the early 2000s ecology

The early 2000s constitute a distinct era of commenting, which underwrites the choice of the BBC *Have Your Say* corpus as the reference baseline. BBC News Online launched in November 1997; *Have Your Say* was among the first institutional spaces for large-scale reader discussion, consolidated in 2005 within the BBC's user-generated content hub under public-service gatekeeping [@Harrison2010; @WardleWilliams2010]. Commercial commenting proliferated slightly later: the *Guardian*'s *Comment is Free* and five other UK sites in 2006 [@KehoeGee2019; @GrahamWright2015], the *Washington Post* in 2006 and the *New York Times* in 2007 [@Santana2011; see also @HermidaThurman2008].

@Herring2004b supplies the periodisation logic: CMC was then " slouching toward the ordinary". An early-2000s *Have Your Say* corpus thus captures the first generation of moderated, public-service, below-the-line commenting. It functions here as a genre and register benchmark rather than a perfectly matched control: the learner comments differ in production context, period, and writer status, difference that are themselves part of the study's interest, as Chapters 3 and 4 develop.

### 1.7 Chapter roadmap

The remainder of the chapter builds the scaffolding for the analysis: Section 2 reviews the theories of genre and register, Section 3 surveys the four approaches to corpus analysis, Section 4 turns to genre and register studies of CMC and ONC, and Section 5 reviews research on stance and evaluation; Section 6 foreshadows the thesis's combined methodology of multi-dimensional analyis and stance bundles. Two further pivots organise what follows beyond this chapter. Chapter 3 is a second literature review, of learner corpus research (LCR), necessary to contextualise the dynamics of L2 English writing within which the learner comments must be interpreted. Only then does Chapter 4 operationalise the methodology, introducing the corpora of interest and the analytical design.

## 2. Genre and register: theoretical foundations

Section 1 situated online news comments (ONC) within computer-mediated communication (CMC) and traced the emergence of commenting as a recognisable discursive practice. Before the empirical literature on ONC can be reviewed, however, the two contstructs on which most of the literature silently relies, *genre* and *register*, must be disentangled; the terms are used inconsistently across disciplines, and in one influential tradition they are effectively inverted. This section reviews the three major traditions of genre theory (Section 2.1), contrasts them with the text-linguistic account of register developed by Biber and colleagues (Section 2.2), and considers how both constructs have been extended to digital environments (Section 2.3), where the hybrid, fast-evolving character of online texts puts classical definitions under particular strain.

### 2.1 Three traditions of genre theory

Genre theory consolidated in the 1980s as a reaction against formalist approaches that described texts without reference to their contexts of use. @Hyon1996 comparative review distinguished three schools that have structured the field since: English for specific purposes (ESP), North American New Rhetoric, and Australian systemic functioonal linguistics (SFL). All three treat genre as socially situated communication, but locate it differently and assign different weight to textual form and social context [@Hyon1996]. 

#### 2.1.1 The SFL tradition: register as field, tenor and mode; genre as staged, goal-oriented social process

Systemic functional linguistics grounds genre and register in a theory of language as social semiotic, in which situation an linguistic form are systematically, not accidentally, related [@Halliday1978]. @HallidayHasan1989 gave this insight its canonical shape: context of situation is described through *field* (what is happening), *tenor* (who is taking part), and *mode* (what role language is playing), each variable resonating with one metafunction, and register is a functionall variety of language determined by situation [@HallidayMattheseen2004].

@Martin1984 introduced the departure that defines the Sydney School: register is recontrued as the context of situtation itself, while *genre* is located at the higher level of context of culture as a staged, goal-oriented social process [@Martin1992], the genres of a culture mapping into families of stories, histories, reports, explanations and procedures [@MartinRose2008]. The contribution is double: a metafunctionally grounded account of register, and a stratified model in which genre sits *above* register as the contextual plane that orchestrates it.

#### 2.1.2 The ESP tradition: communicative purpose, move analysis and the complete text

The ESP tradition, developed for the teaching of academic and professional English, anchors genre in the commincative purposes of discourse communities. @Swales1990 [p. 58] suppled the traditions's canonical definition:

> A genre comprises a class of communicative events, the members of which share...style.

Communicative purpose is explicitly "a priveleged criterion" [@Swales1990, ibid]: genres are identified by what they are for, and their schematic structure, the ordered series of *moves* and *steps*, follows from that ratinale. Move analysis thus made genre operational for complete texts rather than decontextualised linguistic features. @Bhatia1993 [p. 75] extended the framework to professional settings, each move subservient to the genre's overall communicative purpose; @Bhatia2004 later recast genre as a socio-cognitive construct whose interdiscursivity and *generic integrity* account for genre hybridisation across professional worllds.  @AskehaveSwales2001 cautioned from within that communicative purpose cannot be the sole criterion, since purposese are often layered, contested or attributed post-hoc, a caution relevant when a single purpos is later assigned to a multi-party text type such as the online news comment.

#### 2.1.3 The New Rhetoric tradition: genre as social action

North American New Rhetoric shifted attention from the text to the action it performs. @Miller1984 [p. 159] defined genres as "typified rhetorical actions based in recurrent situations": genre knowledge is acquired through participation rather than formal instruction, and classification is legitimate only once genre has been understood as action [@Miller1984]. @Bazerman1988 traced the emergence of the eperimental research article, sowing genre form and scientiic activity to be mutually SOMETHING, and later developed the concept of *genre systems*, interlocking sets of genres organising complex social activity [EXAMPLE??] [@Bazerman1994]. @BerkenkotterHuckin1995 added a socio-cognitive dimension, construing genre knowledge as simultaneously cognitive (schemas built from prior genre experience) and social (embedded in disciplinary culture and power relations).

#### 2.1.4 Convergences and tensions between the three traditions

The three traditions converge on fundamentals: genres are recurrent, socially recognised, purposeful, and inseparable from social context, and recurring situations leave systematic traces in discourse [@Hyon1996]. They diverge in emphasis and method: the Sydney School offers the most elaborate semiotic architecture [@Martin1992; @MartinRose2008]; ESP is the most textually operational [though see @AskehaveSwales2001]; and New Rhetoric's ethnographic methods resist generalisation across large text collections [@BerkenkotterHuckin1995]. ### 2.2 Register and genre in the text-linguistic tradition

The corpus-linguistic tradition associated with Douglas Biber approaches the same territory from the opposite direction. @Biber1988 established the multidimensional (MD) approach, subjecting 67 lexico-grammatical features across 23 spoken and written varieties of English to factor analysis and deriving six dimensions of variation, including Involved versus Informational Production and Narrative versus Non-Narrative Concerns. Two principles are key. First, text varieties are defined by *external*, situational criteria (who produces the text, for whom, in what channel, for what purpose) rather than by linguistic similarity, with @Biber1988 [p. 70] explicitly distinguishing such varieties from "text types", defined by linguistic form. Second, register differences are not defined by categorical features, but by the quantitative and gradient SOMETHING of register markers pervasive features which co-occur throughout texts [@Biber1988].

@BiberConrad2009 systematised this position into three analytical *perspectives* on text varieties: the register perspective combines pervasive linguistic features with the variety's situation of use; the genre perspective shares the situational analysis, but focuses on conventional features of complete texts (openings, closings, move like elements) that may occur only once yet recur in all texts of the type; and the style perspective attends to features not functionally motivated by situational context [@BiberConrad2009, p.2]. A register, accordingly, is "a text variety whose linnguistic structure is determined through the situation of usage, i.e. the context of production" [@BiberConrad2009, pp. 2, 6]. Register analysis presupposes comparison: because register features are matters of frequency and co-occurrence, they are identifiable as characteristic only by contrasting one variety against others [@BiberConrad2009]. The framework also accomodates the everyday sense of register as formality, and regards register variation itself as a linguistic universal [@BiberConrad2009]; the second edition updates the treatment of electronic registers and adds an analysis of *hybrid* registers combining features of distinct varieties [@BiberConrad2019], a development of obvious relevance to online discourse.

This now puts us in a position where we can SOMETHING the terminological relationship between the Flagstaff school and those presented in Section 2.1. What Biber and Conrad call the *genre* perspective corresponds roughly to the formal residue?? of SFL and ESP genre analysis (the conventional staging of complete texts), while their *register* perspective covers what the Sydney School distributes across register and genre together. The frameworks answer different analytical questions rather than competing over the same one, and for that reason offer complementary perspectives for the present study: genre analysis captures the macro-structure and social purpose of whole texts, register analysis the pervasive linguistic texture that situational factors engender. This complementarity is claimed for the study's purposes without adjudicating the genuine theoretical differences between genres construed as context of culture, as communicateive event, and as rhetorical action. Crucially, register theory also scales to the largest text populations: @BiberEgbert2018 sampled documents from the searchable web, coded their situational characterisitcs, and demonstrated through MD analysis that register categories survive online, albeit in new hybrid combinations. Because register is situationally defined and characterised by pervasive, countable features, it is the perspective most readily operationalised by corpus methods, a connection taken up in Section 3.

### 2.3 Genre and register in digital environments

Digital environments test both constructs. @ShepherdWatters1998 coined the term *cybergenre*, proposing that web-based genres evolove from offline antecedants through *replication* and *variant* stages towards *novel* genres unique to the medium, and that genre identification online must consider functionality alongside content and form: what a digital artefact *does* is part of what it *is*. @Santini2007 documented the consequences for classification: web pages display pervasive *genre hybridism* (multiple genres within a single page) and *individualization* (idiosyncratic instances fitting no established category), motivating flexible, multi-label, feature-based descriptions rather than discrete taxonomies, a position aligned with the register persepctive's gradient view of variation. @AskehaveNielsen2005 proposed a two-level model in which a Swalesian?? text (communicative purpose, moves, rhetorical strategies) is embedded in a digital medium with its own constitutuve?? properties (hyperlinks, navigation, multimodality), arguing that classical genre theory underestimates the medium's role. @GiltrowStein2009 edited volume posed the underlying question directly, whether genre theory survives its migration online, and answered it with qualified continuity: online genres are recongnisably descended from offline antecednednts, yet reshaped by medium affordances.

For news discourse specifically, @Bruce2010 analysed the *participatory news article* (the online news article together with its appended reader comments) as a hybrid genre in the cybergenre sense, characterising the comments as generally subjective, refering either to the host article or to previously posted comments, and following a general pattern in which a statement of view is followed by a justifiation (a fuller discussion follows in Section 4.1). The earliest genre-theoretic treatment of ONC already displays the tensions outlined above: the participatory article is hybrid, traceable to an offline antecedent - the letter to the editor - whose online descendants are immediately self-published and editorially unfiltered [@LandertJucker2011], and describable only with reference to medium affordances such as threading and moderation Where genre-based description risks rigidity, @Herring2007 facet-based scheme offers and alternative, classifying computer-mediated discourse by open-ended clusters of medium and social facets that function in practice as situational parameters akin to those of register analysis. Register-oriented work confirms that such hybridity is the norm: @EhretTaboada2020 found that online news comments pattern as a written, argumentative-evaluative register combining features of spoken informality with formal writing, and that online registers generally are less cleanly delineated than traditional ones. Digital text types thus require *both* theoretical lenses: a genre perspective for whole-text conventions and the comment's relation to its host article, and a register perspective for pervasive linguistic texture. Two consequences for the thesis follow. ONC can be viewed through the genre lens as part of a complete communicative event, the participatory news article [@Bruce2010], with its own staging and its constituitive?? relation to the host text; the same comments can be viewed through the register lens as a situationally conditioned variety whose pervasive linguistic texture can be measured and compared [@EhretTaboada2020; @EhretTaboada2021]. This thesis occupies precisely this intersection: it investigates the linguistic realisation of the genre *as* a register, while retaining genre-level awareness of the communicative event in which the comments participate. The register lens, being situationally defined and quantitatively tractable, invites corpus-based operaionalisation, the subject of the following section.

## 3. Corpus approaches to discourse: a framework for this chapter

Sections 1 and 2 established online news comments (ONC) as a site of computer-mediated communication (CMC) and surveyed genre and register theory; what remains is to specify the methodological lens for reviewing the empirical literature. As has been observed elsewhere, "corpus linguistics" is a misleading unitary label for a heterogeneous field [@EgbertLarssonBiber2020]: studies that all claim to be "corpus-based" differ radically in how they treat texts as data, how they annotate and quantify, and what their evidence can support. This chapter therefore adopts @Less2008 [pp. 86-99] four-way distinction of corpus-based approaches as its organising framework.

### 3.1 Lee (2008): corpus-informed, corpus-supported, corpuse-driven and corpus-induced approaches

The status of this framework requires clarification at the outset. @Lee2008 four categories are adopted here as an organising heuristic for comparing methodological emphases, not as an ontology of corpus reasearch: the studies reviewed in Sections 4 and 5 are assigned to the categories  retrospectively, and some sit between them. The justification for imposing the scheme nonetheless is that this thesis is interested in not merely what has been found, but in how different methodologica treatments of data make different kinds of claims possible.

@Lee2008 positions corpus-informed, corpus-supported and coprus-induced approaches as hyponms along a continuum for which "corpus-based" is the superordinate, discussed alongside the corpus-driven tradition originating with @TogniniBonelli2001. (The terminology here follows @Lee2008; the first variant is occasionally rendered "corpus-inspired" in secondary usage.) The continuum runs from maximal preservation of the text to maximal abstraction away from it.

In the **corpus-informed** approach, the corpus functions essentially as a strucuted collection of texts for qualitative analysis. Annotations are added manually, the text remains highly structured, typically at the level of the complete text or move, and methods such as genre analysis, systemic functional linguistics (SFL), conversation analysis or content analysis are applied to whole, co-textually intact documents [@Lee2008]. The **corpus-supported** approach combines qualitative interpretation and quantification: annotation may be manually coded according to a prior theoretical framework, but frequencies are the extracted and used to test hypotheses. The text remains structured, though at a finer grain: individual turns in conversation-analytic work, or the clause in SFL-based analysis [@Lee2008]. An illustrative CMC example is the manual Appraisal coding in corporate Facebook pages by @BurnsMatarazzoAbbamonte2014.

The **corpus-driven** approach shifts the balance towards the quantitative. Annotations are predominantly automatic (part-of-speech tagging and related markup), textual features are atomised at the word or phrase level and aggragated for quantification, and analysis is exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-testing, with a professed theoretical neutrality [@Lee2008; @TogniniBonelli2001]. The paradigm case is multi-dimensional analysis (MDA), in which factor analysis over counts of dozens of lexico-grammatical features yields dimensions of register variation [@Biber1988]; the additive variant, which scores new registers against an existing dimensional model rather than extracting new factors, has been applied to online comments data by @Gerhardt2021. Finally, the **corpus-induced** approach is almost wholly quantitative: annotation and classification are fully automatic, features are entirely atomised and aggregated, for instance into "bag-of-words" representations, with little researcher intervention or interpretation, and the purpose is typically a practical application, such as automatic genre classification or sentiment filtering at scale[@Lee2008; @Santini2007; @SharoffWuMarkert2010]. ### 3.2 Corpus design and representativeness 

The evidentiary value of any of these approaches depends on what the corpus can be taken to represent. @EgbertBiberGray2022 define representativeness as the extent to which a corpus reflects the full range of variability in the variety it is intended to represent, distinguishing **domain representativeness** (coverage of the situational and textual categories of the target domain) from **linguistic representativeness** (coverage of the resulting distribution of linguistic features). THe distinction matters acutely for ONC research: a convenience sample of comments scraped from a few outlets may suffice for corpus-informed reading, but cannot licence population-level claims about ONC as a register without an explicit account of its domain coverage [@EgbertBiberGray2022].

A second principle is that register characteristics emergemost clearly in contrast: the target corpus is compared against other registers or reference corpora, the comparative logic built into MDA from its inception [@Biber1988] and operationalised for the web inn the Corpus of Online Registers of English [@EgbertBiberDavies2015; @BiberEgbert2018]. Claims about what is distinctive of ONC therefore presupposed a principled choice of comparison data, not merely a large sample comments.

### 3.3 Stengths and limitations of the four approaches

Each variant trades depth of interpretation against scale and generalisability. Corpus-informed work retains co-text and discourse structure, keeping categories interpretable and theoretically meaningful, a decisive advantage for prosodic, disperesed phenomena such as Appraisal that resist item-counting [@MartinWhite2005;@Martin2017]. However, its small samples limit generalisability, and the reliability of manual annotation is a documented pressure point [@LarssonPaquotPlonsky2020]. Corpus-supported approaches achieve what @BakerEtAl2008 call a "methodological synergy": corpus evidence discliplines intuition while qualitative reading restores context, making this the natural home of much defensible discourse work on CMC. They nonetheless inherit both burdens: representativeness must still be justified [@EgbertBiberGray2022], and the quantitative arm can drift into statistical reporting at the expense of linguistic interpretation, a field-wide trend documented in a ten-year review of journal practice by @LarssonEgbertBiber2022.
Corpus-driven approaches offer reproducibilty, genuine exploratory power and cumulative comparability, the additive-MDA tradition allowing new registers to be situated against established dimensions [@Biber1988; @BerberSardinhaVeiranoPinto2019], but their claim to theoretical neutrality is contested, since statistical and functional design choices are themselves theory-laden, and their statistically derived dimensions require post hoc interpretation [CITATIONS]. Corpus-induced approaches, finally, are practically indispensible for harvesting and filtering web data at scale, yet their documented weaknesses are substantial: expert agreement on web-genre categories is low [@SharoffWuMarkert2010], and even state-of-the-art automatic register indentification remains well below ceiling. More fundamentally, bag-of-words and composite machine-derived measures are "linguistically opaque", severing the link between quantitative output and interpretable linguistic structure [@EgbertLarssonBiber2020; @LarssonBiber2024]. From a systemic-functional perspective, @Martin2017 argues that discourse-semantic meaning is relational, a matter of what a choice means against the alternatives it excludes, rather than countable; atomised quantitative analysis therefore risks what might be characterised as a flattening of the social dimensions of texts (this chapter's characterisation,building on @MartinWhite2005; @Martin2017). @Sharoff2018 demonstation that genre is orthogonal to topic reinforces the point: outputs of topic-oriented automatic methods cannot stand in for register analysis.

### 3.4 The framework as organising device

The four variants should not be considered as a ranking, but rather a mapping of methodological affordances, and the remainder of this chapter uses them as an organising device rather than a taxonomy to be policed. Section 4 applies the continuum to genre and register reseach on CMC and ONC, from qualitative genre analysis through MDA to automatic classification; Section 5 applies the same lens to research on stance and evaluation, from manual Appraisal analysis through stancetaking studies and corpus-driven work on evaluation, metadiscurse and lexical bundles to induced sentiment analysis.

### 4.1 Corpus-informed approaches: qualitative genre analysis of news texts and comment spaces

In the corpus-informed approach, the corpus is a collection of complete, structured texts from which the analyst selects examples of qualitative interpretation; annotation is manual with theoretially motivated categories, the method lending itself to genre analysis or ethnographic description rather than aggregate counting [@Lee2008].The canonical application of this approach in ONC is @Bruce2010 analysis of what he terms the "participatory news article": the composite digital text formed by an online news article together with its appended reader comments. Bruce argues that the comment space changes the communicative purpose and social structure of the host genre, producing a genuinely hybrid genre rather than a conventional article with an appended paratext. The study locates the distinctiveness of ONC at the level of the complete text (the stageing and social relations of the article-comment ensemble), precisely the analytical level that the register-based methods of Section 4.3 do not capture.

Genre-oriented work in this tradition also situates ONC genealogically, treating reader comments as the digital successor of the letters-to-the-editor page: the comparisons touched upon in Section 1.5 [@LandertJucker2011; @McCluskeyHmielowski2012] show that moving reader response online reconfigures the public-private boundary of audience participation, yielding comments that are more personal, more dialogic, less gatekep and more extreme than print letters. The changed participation regime is visible in the pagmatics of the texts themselves: @NeurauterKessels2011 documents a systematic repertoire of im/polite, face-threatening moves in reader responses on British online news sites, a profile the editorially filtered letter genre does not permit to surface,

The strengths of the corpus-informed approach are interpretive depth and fidelity to text structure: because texts remain whole, genre-level claims about moves, stageing an hybridity can be grouned in co-text. Its limitations are the mirror image:  @Bruce2010 account rests on a small, purposively selected set of articles and comment sections, offering limited domain representativeness [@EgbertEtAl2022], and no guarantee that the genre structure identified generalises across outlets, topics, moderation regimes or languages. Corpus-informed genre analysis therefore functions best as hypothesis-generating groundwork whose claims require testing at scale, the role taken up by the corpus-supported tradition.

 ### 4.2 Corpus-supported approaches: purpose-built annotated comment corpora

Corpus-supported research combines manual, theory-driven annotation with quantification, testing hypotheses formulated in advance [@Lee2008]; in ONC research it is dominated by projects that build annotated comment corpora as research infrastructure. Designed as a gold standard for automatic summarisation of mulit-party argumentative conversations [@BarkerGaizauskas2016], its layered annotation scheme embodies the corpus-supported logic: interpretive categories are imposed by trained humans, but the resulting annotations are countable and machine exploitable. ### 4.3 Corpus-driven approaches: multidimensional and register analyses

Corpus-driven research reverses the direction of inference: features are tagged automatically, atomised at the word or phrase level, aggregated, and interpreted only after quantification [@Lee2008]. The dominant corpus-driven method in register research is multi-dimensional analysis (MDA), the factor-analytic technique origninating with @Biber1988, in which co-occuring lexico-grammatical features are extracted as dimensions of variation on which registers are positioned. The following subsections trace the method's migration from the early speech-writing question about CMC through web registers generally to ONC, concluding with the choice between full and additive MDA.

#### 4.3.1 The early spoken-written cline revisited

The first generation of corpus-based CMC research asked a decptively simple question: does electronic language resemble speech or writing? @FerraraBrunnerWhittemore1991 characterised synchronous typed dialogue as "interactive written discourse", an emergent hybrid register; @Yates1996b gave the claim corpus-linguistic substance, finding that Open University computer conferencing and e-maile patterned with the written LOB corpus on textual measures (vocabulary range, lexical density) but with the spoken London-Lunnd corpus on interpersonal measures (first-person pronouns, modal verbs); and @CollotBelmore1996 imported @Biber1988 multidimensional framework, finiding public bulletin-board messages "replete with indicators of involvement" [@CollotBelmore1996, p. 22], closest overall to "public interviews and letters, personal as well as professional" \[p. 21\]. @Barron1998 synthesised these findings into the widely accepted position that electronic language is hybrid in a multidimensional sense. This early work established the expectation that comment language would be "conversation-like", later tested and largely overturned (Section 4.3.3), and demonstrated that the cline question is ill-posed without multiple independent dimensions, the lesson that propelled MDA into CMC research.

#### 4.3.2 MDA of CMC registers

Subsequent work extended MDA across the ecology of online registers. @Jonsoon2015 applied @Biber1988 dimensions additively to Internet Relay Chat and ICQ "supersynchronous" chat, showing that chat is speech-like across all five dimensions despite being graphically mediated. Corpus-driven MDA of web registers proper began with @BiberKurjian2007 and @GrieveEtAl2010, the latter demonstrating that blog registers are internally heterogeneous. @TitakRoberson2013 conducted the first exploratory MDA to include reader comments (from YouTube, ESPN, and Yahoo!): their four dimensions positioned comments as "involved, personal and opinonated" [@TitakRoberson2013, p. 255], nearer to email and blogs than to informational registers. Berber Sardinhas's paired studies supply the methodological poles of the tradition: an additive analysis projecting Internet registers onto @Biber1988 dimensions [@BerberSardinha2014], and a full MDA extracting new dimensions from the same corpus [@BerberSardinha2018], which recovered the involved-versus-informational dimension, but also two stance-based dimensions specific to social media, interactional evidentiality and interactional affect. At the largest scale, @BiberEgbert2016 extracted dimensions of variation across the Corpus of Online Registers of English (CORE), a programme consolidated in their book-length treatment [@BiberEgbert2018], which emphasises the pervasiveness of hybridm hard-to-classify online documents. Short-text adaptions followed: @ClarkeGrieve2017, [-@ClarkeGrieve2019] identified four functional styles on the Donald Trump Twitter account, while @FriginalWaughTitak2018 used an additive design on @TitakRoberson2013 dimensions to contrast Facebook and Twitter posts. Together, these studies mapped the dimensional cartography of online language and established that stance-related dimensions are central to social-media registers, a finding that returns with particular force for ONC.

#### 4.3.3 MDA and register analysis of ONC specifically

The first MDA studies dedicated to ONC are those of Ehret and Taboada. In the 2020 study, SOCC comments and their host opinion articles were compared with the spoken and written registers of the Canadian component of the International Corpus of English (ICE-Canada), using the Multidimensional Analysis Tagger (@Nini2019) and a full factor analysis. The result contradicts the folk-linguistic?? intuition inherited form early CMC research: online news comments are not like spontaneous conversation, but pattern?? closer to opinion articles and even examination scripts: a written register whose language is informally written, yet argumentative and evaluative[@EhretTaboada2020]. The 2021 study widened the comparison space to seventeen online registers in CORE, yielding three dimensions (involved-evaluative, informational-argumentative, and narrative descrition versus instructional) on which comments emerge as simultaneously involved-evaluative and informational but predominantly argumentative: informational-argumentative discourse with an involved-evaluative slant [@EhretTaboda2021]. @Gerhardt2021 provides the complementary additive analysis, scoring the comments sections of vegan food blogs onto @Biber1988 dimensions and concluding that these comments constitute a written genre that is involved, situation-dependent and non-abstract: dialogic and expressive, yet graphically coded, public and distal??. The convergence of the two studies on the written status of comments, reached by opposite methodologcial routes (full versus additive MDA) and on different comment ecologies (news versus blogs), strengthens the register-level claim, while their differences in involvement caution against treating ONC as a single uniform register: the MDA evidence shows systematic variation within the register across comment ecologies, not a family of established sub-registers (Cf SOMETHING).

Three further corpus-driven strands complement the MDA work. The first, @Marchand2013, is my one earlier study and a point of continuity with the present study: its BBC *Have Your Say* corpus (HYSOC), approximately 1.5 million words of messages, is a precussor of the reference corpus used here, and the study amounts to a pilot lexical-bundle analysis of the same commenting ecology. Comparing the extracted bundleswith spoken and written reference registers, it found the comments highly formulaic and patterning?? as a register distinct from both spontaneous speech and edited writing; because lexical bundles in comment data are heavily implicated in stance expression, this strand is taken up again in Section 5. @CollinsNerlich2015 applied the Wmatrix and the USAS semantic tagger to *Guardian* climate-change comments to derive key semantic domains, a design @Collins2019 extends in a bok-length treatment of online communication corpora, including comments on antimicrobial resistance. In a related critical-discourse design, @BakerMcGlashan2020 compare keywords and discourse prosody in *Daily Express* articles about Romanians with readers' comments on the same articles. These keyword and key-semantic-domain approaches characterise what comments are *about* and how they evaluate, forming a second bridge to Section 5.

 #### 4.3.4 Full versus additive MDA

The choice between the two MDA variants mentioned above deserves some extra attention. In a full MDA, a new factor analysis extracts dimensions from the target corpus itself; in an additive MDA, new registers are scored on the dimensions of a validated base study without conducting a new factor analysis [BerberSardinhaEtAl2019]. Full MDA is novel and its co-occurence structure is precisely what is in question, as for ONC, where @EhretTaboada2020, [-@EhretTaboada2021] could not assume that Biber's 1988 dimensions, derived from pre-Internet registers, would capture the variation internal to online comments. Additive MDA is more economical and yields scores directly comparable with the base study's register space, the property @Gerhardt2021 exploited for blog comments, but it inherits the base study's feature set, tagging assumptions and dimension structure, risking misfit when the new register differs systematically from those on which the base model was built [@BerberSardinhaEtAl2019]. A further consideration is common to both variants: register features become visible only through contrast, so what counts as a characteristic of ONC depends on the comparison corpus (ICE-Canada highlights comments' distance from conversation, CORE their position among online registers), and the composition and representativeness of the reference data [@EgbertBiberGray2022] are as consequential as the target corpus itself.

### 4.4 Corpus-induced approaches: automatic genre and register classification

At the corpus-induced end of @Lee2008 continuum, annotation and classification are fully automatic and the goals are practical: above all, automatic genre identification (AGI) on the web. @Santini2007 established the foundational problem: unlike the relatively stable genres of print, web pages exhibit genre hybridism and individualisation, so that a single document may instatiate several genres or none. @MehlerSharoffSantini2010 edited volume consolidated this research programme, while @SharoffWuMarkert2010 delivered its most sobering result: even genre experts agree poorly when classifying randomly extracted web texts, undermining the validity of the categories classifiers are trained to predict. Against this background, @EgbertBiberDavies2015 developed CORE through bootom-up, user-based procedure in which non-expert raters categorised documents from near-random web searches into a hierrarchial register scheme, with documented reliability, a repair of the single-expert annotation problem.@Sharoff2018 proposed Functional Text Dimensions, defining genre and register categories functionally and independently of topic, a useful pre-caution to note for ONC research, where topic variation across news stories must not be mistaken register variation. Most recently, @LaippaleEtAl2022 report register identification over unrestricted open web using CORE-derived training data, with state-of-the-art neural models reaching roughly 68 per cent F1 [explain this measure??], respectable for deployment, but from the ideal ceiling. The corpus-induced tradition is practically indispensable for ONC research, since harvesting comment data at scale requires exactly these classifiers; its limitations are equally relevant: category validity and inter-rater reliability remain weak [@SharoffWuMarkert2010], the representiveness of web-derived training corpora is uncertain [@EgbertBiberDavies2015], and the features exploited are linguitically opaque, so that the classification accuracy buys little linguistic explanation, and instance of the broader displacement of linguistic description by statistical reporting in corpus work. [@EgbertLarssonBiber2020; @LarssonEgbertBiber2022].

### 4.5 Section conclusion: register and genre revisited

Read together, the four tradtions yield complementary levels of description. At the genre level, the corpus-informed evidence indicates that comments particpate in a larger, hybrid textual unit, @Bruce2010 participatory news article, whose staged structure and social relations a feature-based register analysis alone cannot capture; register analysis, conversely, decribes the pervasive linguistic texture of comments, and the corpus-supported annotation programmes [@BarkerEtAl2016; @KolhatkarEtAl2020] supply the bridge between the two levels. One finding cuts across all four approaches and organises what follows: evaluation is a particularly salient characteristic of ONC, central to what commenting is for, a conclusion drawn independently from multidimensional analysis [@EhretTaboada2020] and from manual annotation showing that the large majority of evaluative spans?? in news comments are negative [@CavassoTaboada2021]. What this evaluative character implies for the emerging profile of ONC, and how stance and evaluation in comments have beem studied, is taken up in Section 5, organised by the same corpus approaches, and the fuller register characterisation is drawn together in Sections 5.6 and 6.1).

## 5. Stance and evaluation in CMC and ONC

Section 4 concluded that online news comments (ONC) can be characterised as an emerging, predominantly evaluative written register. The present section examines how the literature has theorised and operationalised this evaluative character, organised by @Lee2008 four variants of corpus-based methodology rather than chronologically, because each variant brings a distinct conception of what stance and evaluation are, and how they can be evidenced.

### 5.1 Stance, subjectivity, intersubjectivity, evaluation, appraisal: a terminological map

The literature on the expression of opinion and attitude is notorious for overlapping terminology, and a brief orientation is necessary before the approaches can be compared. *Stance* is used in the broadly Biberian sense of the linguistic marking of a speaker's or writer's "attitudes, feelings, judgements, or commitment concerning the message" [BiberFinegan1988, p. 1; cf. @GrayBiber2012]. *Subjectivity* concerns the imprint of the speaking self on utterance, while *intersubjectivity* captures the orientation of utterances towards other subjects whose alignment is solicited or resisted [@DuBois2007; @DuBoisKarkkainen2012]. *Evaluation* is the broadest cover term: the expression of speaker or writer opinion about the entities and propositions talked about [@Hunston2011], developed corpus-linguisically in news discourse by @Bednarek2006. *Appraisal*, by contrast, names a specific systematised taxonomy of evaluative resources developed within systemic functional linguistics (SFL) [@MartinWhite2005], while *metadiscourse* [@Hyland2005a] and *sentiment* [@PangLee2008] label the interactional and computational reconceptualisations of essentially the same phenomenon. These terms are not interchangeable: each embeds a different assumption about whether evaluation is a discourse-semantic system, an intersubjective act located in sequences, a phraseological pattern to be counted, or a polarity score to be computed [EXAMPLES after each OR TABLE?]

### 5.2 Corpus-informed approaches: the Appraisal framework

The Appraisal framework originates in the SFL tradition discussed in Section 2, developed predominantly within the Sydney School by Jim Martin, Peter White and colleagues [@Martin2000; @White2003; @MartinWhite2005]. Appraisal encompasses the linguistic resources through which emotions, judgements and aesthetic valuations are negotiatedd, amplified and related to alternative voices in the discourse [@Martin2000], modelled as a discourse-semantic system network comprising three primary sub-systems: Attitude, Engagement and Graduation [@MartinWhite2005].

Attitude is further differentiated into three sub-type: Affect covers emotional reactions (happiness, sadness, fear); Judgement, morally inflected assessments of human character and behaviour (ethical, deceptive, brave); and Appreciation, the valuation of entities, processes and natural phenomena (remarkable, elegant), as in the following attested examples from the present study's corpus (see Chapter 3):

> *I am sad to hear the news of this flood.* \[JOC_1707 - Affect\]

> *I think he is a very brave man.* \[JOC_0849 - Judgement\]

> *Japanese fall is very beautiful.* \[JOC_2802 - Appreciation\]

> *Brown did well to resist the temptation of popularity and only blow half of his \$ budget surplus.* \[HOC_LF - Judgement/Appreciation\]

Attempting to make these categories machine-tractable, @TaboadaGrieve2004 identified typical sentence structures associated with each Attitude subtype: Affect prototypically realised by patterns such as "I was sad", Judgement by "He was brave", Appreciation by "It was beautiful". Their semi-automatic classification of adjectives from review corpora is significant less for its accuracy than for demonstrating that Appraisal categories are lexico-grammatically patterned enough to be operationalised, an insight taken up in Sections 5.4 and 5.5. A further distinction separates *inscribed* (explicit) evalutation, as in "a bright kid", from *evoked* evaluation, where judgement is invited without being directly stated, as in "a kid who reads a lot" [@MartinWhite2005].

The Engagement system, inspired by Bakhtin's account of dialogism and heteroglossia [Bakhtin, 1981, as cited in @MartinWhite2005], distinguishes monoglossic utterances, which present propositions as bare fact, from heteroglossic utterances, which acknowledge or engage alternative viewpoints; heteroglossic resources are divided into those that *expand* the dialogic space (entertain, attribute) and those that *contract* it (disclaim, proclaim) [@White2003; @MartinWhite2005]. @White2003 analysis of "reporter", "correspondent" and "commentator" voices in news discourse shows how journalistic genres calibrate the range of admissable evaluative positions, a calibration against which the freer evaluative regime of reader comments can be measured. Finally, the Graduation system modulates the strength and precision of evaluations: Force scales the intensity of gradable meanings ("very interesting" versus "a little sad"), while Focus sharpens or softens prototypicality ("a true friend" versus "a kind of friend") [@MartinWhite2005].

Appraisal has been applied across genres including news discourse [@White1998; @CoffinOHalloran2006], children's narratives [@Martin1996; @Painter2003], online discussions of literary texts [@Love2006] and everyday conversation [@EgginsSlade1997]. Within CMC research, the most influential corpus-informed extension is @Zappavigna2012 analysis of a 100 million word Twitter corpus, coupling Appraisal with the concept of "ambient affiliation": users align around shared evaluative bonds without directly addressing one another, a configuration closely resembling the loosely threaded interaction of comment sections, where commenters respond to an article and an imagined audience rather than a single interlocutor. The strengths of corpus-informed Appraisal work are delicacy and attention to co-text; its limitations are the mirror image; manual annotation is labour-intensive, results hard to generalise, and reliability uneven: in the ONC study discussed below, all categories except Graduation reached acceptable inter-annotator agreement [@CavassoTaboada2021].

### 5.3 Corpus-supported approaches: Appraisal with quantification and stancetaking

Corpus-supported work retains the theoretically motivated annotation schemes of the corpus-informed tradition, but subjects the annotated categories to systematic quantification, trading some delicacy for testability. A key study for the present thesis is @CavassoTaboada2021, who manually annotated 1,043 comments on opinion pieces in *The Globe and Mail*, a subcorpus of the SFU Opinion and Comments Corpus [SOCC; @KolhatarEtAl2020], using the full Appraisal system, with two annotators, a curator and two reliability studies. Their principal finding is clear: "The defining characteristic of the register of online news comments is their evaluative nature" [@CavassoTaboada2021, p.1]. Quantitatively, 73.5% of all Appraisal spans were negative, and 79% of comments were mostly or entirely negative; commenters also favoured Judgement and Appreciation over Affect, which accounted for only 3.4% of spans. Rather than *I hate the candidate*, commenters write *The candidate is incompetent* or *The candidate's policies are bad*: highly opinionated content packaged as objective assessment rather than emotional outburst [@CavassoTaboada2021]. This preference for apparently depersonalised evauation is a register-level that qualitative reading alone could not establish; the authors further show that evaluative patterns interact with constructiveness and toxicity, with implications for automatic moderation.

@TrnavacTaboada2023 extend the analysis cross-linguistically, annotating comparable samples of *Globe and Mail* (English) and RT (Russian) comments for the the Engagement system: constructive comments were more explicitly subjective, English ones foregrounding the reliability of knowledge claims, and Russian ones relying more on necessity modality. Appraisal has also served as a SOMETHING scheme elsewhere in CMC: @BurnsMatarazzoAbbamonte2014 use Appraisal categories to retrieve emotion and stance from posts and comments on Samsung's and Apple's Facebook pages: an early demonstration, albeit in a commercial rather than news context, that Appraisasl can drive quantitative comparison of online communities.

A parallel corpus-supported tradition approaches the same phenomena not through a taxonomy of evaluative meanings, but through the pragmatics of the stance itself. @DuBois2007 defines stance as "a public act by a social actor, achieved dialogically through overt communicative means, of simultaneously evaluating objects, positioning subjects (self and others), and aligning with other subjects, with respect to any salient dimension of the sociocultural field" (p. 163). The resulting "stance triangle" models every stance as as three simultaneous sub-acts: a first subject evaluates a shared stance object, therby positioning him- or herself, and in doing so aligns (or disaligns) with a second subject oriented to the same object. Because each stance act invites a responsive act of alignment, stance is inherently intersubjetive and sequential rather than a private psychological state, a point reinforced by findings that even routine epistemic markers such as *I think* function interactionally [@Karkkainen2003, @Karkkainen2006; @Englebreson2007] and that stancetaking enregisters social identities and dialects [@Johnstone2007]. The triangle maps almost isomorphically onto the comment-thread situation, in which a commenter evaluates the news article, positions themselves, and invites alignment from subsequent commenters. Research on digital discourse confimrs the model's transferability: @GiaxoglouJohansson2020 show that networked practices of epistemic, affective and narrative stancetaking shape the participation in social media responses to events unfolding in the media, and @Johansson2017 documents the vernacular?? opinion work of news discussion forums. Stancetaking research thus supplies what taxonomic Apprasial work SOMETHING, an account of why evaluation in ONC is fundamentally other-directed, while remaining largely qualitative in its evidential base.

### 5.4 Corpus-driven approaches: evaluation, semantic prosody, metadiscourse, grammatical stance, lexical bundles

Corpus-driven approaches invert the logic of the previous two traditions: rather than applying a prior taxonomy, they induce evaluative patterning?? from recurrent lexico-grammatical forms, on the basis that evaluative meaning is often invisible to intuition, and recoverable only through copus recurrence. @Louw1993 coined *semantic prosody* for the aura?? of favourable or unfavourable meaning that items acquire through their typical collocates, arguing that insincerity and irony can be diagnosed where prosodies clash; @Hunston2007 shows that semantic prosodies are pervasive, frequently attidunal, and central to how evaluation is signalled obliquely. Hunston's [-@Hunston2011] monograph generalises the point into a programme: evaluation is fundamentally phraseological, accruing?? to recurrent patterns rathen than isolated words, and is best studied through grammar patterns and *local grammars* of evaluation [@HunstonSinclair2000], which parse evaluative utterances into functional elements such as the evaluated entity, the evalautive category, and the evaluative response.

The single most consequential corpus-driven study for present purposes is @MillarHunston2015, whose data, free-text comments submitted to the website RateMyProfessors.com, are structurally homologous to ONC: short, unsolicited, evaluative user contributions responding to a shared object. Starting not from a taxonomy but from the adjectives commenters use to describe people, clustered with Principal Components Analysis, they derive seven dimensions of evaluative meaning along which professors are assessed, and three distinct evaluative "voices" the community deploys (the "novice intellectual", the "consumer", the "subordinate"). Three implications follow: adjectives emerge as the lexico-grammatical locus at which evalautive meaning concentrates in comment-style data; evaluative taxonomies are demonstrably community-specific, so parameters induced from one commenting community cannot be assumed for another; and bottom-up statistical induction can effectively precede, and problematise, top-down category systems such as Appraisal. The study thus provides both an empirical bridge from generic evaluation research to online comment data, and a methodologcial licence for inducing ONC-specific evaluative categories.

@Hyland2005a model of *metadiscourse* likewise distinguishes interactive resources, which guide the reader through the text, from interactional resources, namely stance (hedges, boosters, attitude markers, self-mention) and engagement (reader pronouns, directives, questions), through which writers negotiate with imagined readers [see alse @Hyland2005b; @HylandTse2004]. Although developed primarily for academic writing, the model captures a design feature of ONC that the Appraisal approach underplays: commenters habitually address the article's author, other commenters and a wider imagined readership, and the markers of that address are themselves recurrent, countable items.

The most thoroughly quantified strand of corpus-driven stance research, however, is the grammatical-stance tradition associated with Biber and colleagues. Chapter 12 of the *Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English* [@BiberEtAl1999] provides the standard inventory of grammatical stance devices: stance adverbials (marking certainty, likelihood, attitude, or style); complement-clause constructions whose controlling element is a verb (including mental verbs such as *think* and *suasive* verbs such as *demand*, *suggest* and *insist*), and adjective (*it is clear that...*) or a noun; and modal and semi-modal verbs. @GrayBiber2012 review and delimit these conceptions, and @Biber2006 demonstrates, in a multi-register study of university language, that such stance markers cluster densely in particular registers and tend to occur not as isolated items, but as parts of recurrent multi-word combinations.

That last observation connects grammatical stance to the lexical bundle programme. @BiberConradCortes2004 extracted recurrent four-word sequences from university teaching and textbooks, and proposed the now-standard taxonomy distinguishing *stance bundles* (epistemic; attitudinal/modal - desire, obligation/directive, intention/prediction, ability) from discourse organisers and referential expressions; subsequent work showed that bundles are structurally incomplete, functionally specialised and register-differentiated, with stance bundles predominating in conversation-like registers [@BiberBarbieri2007], and that bundle inventories are sensitive to the discourse community, so that register specific inventories must be derived empirically rather than imported [@Hyland2008]. Crucially for CMC, @Barbieri2018 extracted bundles from a 2.2 million corpus of American blogs, and found that roughly half are stance bundles, typically verb-phrase-based and encapsulating first-person reference (*I don't want to*, *don't get me wrong*), a window onto the subjectivity and intersubjectivity of blog writing.  At the level of ONC specifically, Marchand's [-@Marchand2013] *Have Your Say* bundle analysis (Section 4.3) found comment language highly formulaic, patterning as a register distinct from both spontaneous speech and edited writing [SOMETHING ABOUT STANCE]. Beyond fixed n-grams, the colgram approach implemented in Concgram retrieve non-contiguous, variably ordered lexical co-occurences exhaustively [@Greaves2009; @GreavesWarren2010]. Taken together, these studies establish that stance is phraseologically patterned, register and community specific, and that blog and comment data in particular are saturated with reccurent stance formulae.

 ### 5.5 Corpus-induced approaches: sentiment analysis of comments

At the corpus-induced end of the continuum, evalaution is operationalised for practical ends (classification, ranking, moderation) with minimal researcher interpretation. Sentiment analysis (or opinion mining) is comprehensively surveyed by PangLee2008 and @Liu2012, who document its core tasks: polarity classficiation, subjectivity detection, and aspect-based opinion extraction. Methodologically, two families are relevant here. Lexicon-based methods aggregate the prior sentiment values of words, adjusted by valence shifters such as negation, intensification and irrealis [EXAMPLES OF EACH?]; the SO-CAL system of @TaboadaEtAl2011, a direct computational descendent of @TaboadaGrieve2004 automation of Appraisal, is the reference implementation. Its authors show that it is stubbornly domain-dependent: dictionaries built on one genre underperform on another. Related resources include the NRC word-emotion association lexicon, crowdsourced by @MohammadTurney2013, which extends binary polarity into eight emotion categories. SentStrenght [@ThelwallEtAl2010] exemplified machine-learning approaches for short informal web text, assigning separate positive and negative strength scores to comments, tweets and similar data.

Sentiment and quality analysis of news comments specifically has become a substantial subfield, built on the YNACC and SOCC corpora described in Section 4.2 \[see also Table 4\]. @NapolesEtAl2017 used YNACC's manual annotations of sentiment, persuasiveness, tone, agreement, sarcasm and conversation quality as the basis for automatically identifying "good conversations", demonstrating that comment quality is sufficiently learnable to support ranking and moderation applications. Work on SOCC pursued the complementary goals of constructiveness and toxicity classification: @KolhatkarEtAl2017a piloted the annotation and supervised classification of constructive language in *Globe and Mail* comments, and @KolhatkarEtAl2020 report classification experiments over the full corpus's constructiveness and toxicity annotations. ### 5.6 Section conclusion

Read together, the four traditions converge on a single characterisation of ONC. Corpus-informed and corpus-supported Appraisal studies show that evaluation is a particularly salient characteristic of the register, central to what commenting is for, and that commenters prefer apparently objective Judgement and Appreciation over emotive Affect [@CavassoTaboada2021; @TrnavacTaboada2023]. Stancetaking research explains why: a comment is a public act of evaluating a shared object that positions its author and solicits alignment from others, and intersubjective logic that transfers directly to asynchronous written CMC [@DuBois2007; @GiaxoglouJohansson2020]. Corpus-driven work adds that such an evaluative positioning is phraseologically patterned and community-specific: evaluative meaning accrues?? to recurrent word sequences [@Hunston2011], and adjective-centred evaluative taxonomies differ between commenting communities [@MillarHunston2015; @Barbieri2018]. Even corpus-induced sentiment research, for all its loss of delicacy, presupposes that evaluation permeates the register. What none of these traditions has provided is a register-specific inventory of the recurrent lexico-grammatical formulae through which stance is taken in ONC.

These findings converge on the present study's central construct through a chain of four steps. First, ONC are evaluative: evaluation is central to the communicative function of commenting. Second, evalution is realised linguistically through the resources of stance, the linguistic marking of attitudes, judgements and commitment [@BiberFinegan1988; @GrayBiber2012]. Third, at least some of these stance resources take the form of recurrent phraseological patterns rather than isolated items [@Biber2006; @Hunston2011]. Fourth, such patterns can be extracted automatically and inventoried for a register. Stance bundles therefore operationalise one important linguistic realisation of ONC's evaluative character: they are recurrent multi-word sequences identified through recurrent tagged stance features, namely evaluative adjectives, stance adverbs and suasive verbs. The choice of these three feature categories is motivated by the grammatical-stance framework of @BiberEtAl1999, which establishes them as core grammatical categories of stance marking: adjectives are the primary evaluative word class [@MillarHunston2015], adverbs function as stance adverbials, and suasive verbs mark directive stance in complement-clause constructions. Complement-clause constructions and stance adverbials thus supply the grammatical framework within which the features are interpreted, while the bundles themselves are assembled from the tagged adjectives, adverbs and suasive verbs. Therefore the method inherits the grammatical-stance grounding of the SOMETHING framework, while remaining extractable through automatic tagging. Chapter 4 develops this operationalisation for the ONC corpora of this study.

## 6. Conclusion: toward the present study

### 6.1 The argument of the chapter

Taken together, the literature reviewed in this chapter establishes three propositions about online news comments (ONC). The first is situational: ONC constitute a coherent discoursal configuration within computer-mediated communication (CMC), namely asynchronous, persistent, pseudonymous, one-to-many messages appended to professionally produced news texts [@Herring2007], descended SOMETHING from the letter to the editor, but reconfigured by that migration into something more personal, more dialogic and less gatekept [@LandertJucker2011]. The second concerns register: read through the genre-versus-register distinction of Section 2 and the corpus typology of Section 3 [@BiberConrad2009; @Lee2008], comments are an emerging written register rather than the typed conversation early CMC research expected, patterning?? closest to opinion journalism and other persuasive written registers [@EhretTaboada2020, @EhretTaboada2021; cf. @Marchand2013; @Gerhardt2021], while at the genre level they participate in the larger hybrid unit of the participatory news article [@Bruce2010]. The third proposition is functional: evaluation is a particularly salient characteristic of the register, central to what commenting is for. Evaluative language saturates comments, skews strongly negative, and prefers apparently objective Judgement and Appreciation over emotive Affect [@CavassoTaboada2021], and stancetaking theory explains why: a comment is a public act of evaluating a shared object that simultaneously positions its author and solicits alignment from co-commenters [@DuBois2007]. In short, the field informs us what ONC are, where they sit among registers, and that evaluation is their main concern.

### 6.2 What the literature cannot explain: the double gap

What this body of work cannot explain is how stance is performed, phraseologically, in the register. Each of @Lee2008 four approaches stops short of an inventory??: corpus-informed genre analysis works at the level of whole texts [@Bruce2010]; corpus-supported annotation quantifies evaluative meanings rather than their recurrent wording [@KolhatkarEtAl2020; @CavassoTaboada2021]; corpus-induced sentiment methods achieve scale at the cost of linguistic interpretability; and even the corpus-drive tradition, which has shown stance to be phraseologically patterned?? and community-specific in adjacent comment data [@Barbieri2018; @MillarHunston2015], has produced no such inventory for news comments, the closest precendent being the exploratory bundle counts of @Marchand2013. The first gap, then, would appear to be that the field lacks a register-specific inventory of the recurrent lexico-grammatical formulae of stance in ONC. The second gap can also be stated provisionally for now: the literature reviewed here has not provided an account of how language learners deploy the register's characteristic stance resources; whether and to what extent learner corpus research has addressed the corresponding learner dimension will be taken up in Chapter 3.

The double gap matters because register claims are only as strong as their comparisons: register features become visible only through contrast, and the evidentiary value of any corpus claim depends on demonstrable representativeness [@BiberConrad2009; @EgbertBiberGray2022]. The thesis therefore employs an early-2000s BBC *Have Your Say* corpus as a register benchmark rather than a perfectly matched control: it captures the first generation of moderated, public service, below-the-line commenting [@Harrison2010; @Herring2004b], so that learner comments written over ten to fifteen years later can be located against an established native-speaker comment register with genre held broadly constant [@HerringPaolillo2006], while the residual differences of writer population and period between the corpora are treated as objects of interpretation rather than SOMETHING away.

### 6.3 The design of the present study

The design developed in Chapter 4 follows directly from this argument, combining the two traditions best placed to close the first gap. Register description proceeds by additive multi-dimensional analysis, scoring the learner and reference comments onto @Biber1988 dimensions: the additive variant is econoomical and yields scores directly comparable with the base study, the property @Gerhardt2021 exploited for blog comments, while its known risk of dimensional misfit is mitigated by the convergence of Ehret and Taboada's [-@EhretTaboada2021] full MDA results on the writtern, argumentative-evaluative profile. Stance analysis proceeds by stance feature bundles: recurrent multi-word sequences identified through recurrent tagged stance features, namely evaluative adjectives, stance adverbs and suasive verbs. As Section 5.6 detailed, these are the core grammatical categories of stance marking established by @BiberEtAl1999: adjectives are the primary evaluative word class [@MillarHunston2015], adverbs function as stance adverbials, and suasive verbs mark directive stance in complement-clause constructions; the stance-adverbial and complement-clause framework supplies the grammatical grounding [@Biber2006], comment-style data confirm these categories as the locus of evaluative meaning [@Barbieri2018], and the method remains extractable through automatic tagging. This second strand deliberately bridges the corpus-driven and corpus-supported approaches: extraction is inductive and automatic, but the feature bundles are classified against the established functional categories and interpreted in co-text, retaining the interpretability that purely induced measures sacrifice. Chapter 4 then turns to the data itself, and the mechanics of SOMETHING.
