# Chapter 2

## Introduction: Online news comments as computer-mediated communication

### 1.1 Online news comments as a form of CMC: why study language below the line

The present thesis analyses a learner corpus of online news comments (ONC) written by Japanese university students of English across six cohorts, against a reference corpus of comments from the BBC's *Have Your Say* platform dating from the early 2000s. The object of study first requires situating: ONC are a form of computer-mediated communication (CMC), and CMC research supplies the context for a linguistic analysis of news commenting.

@Herring1996 \[p.1\] canonical definition describes CMC as "communication that takes place between human beings via the intrumentality of computers". @Herring2001 later distinguished *computer-mediated communication*, the socio-technical phenomenon, from *computer-mediated discourse* (CMD), the language produced through it, and it is the later term that is adopted here. Within the ecology of CMD, ONC occupy a distinctive niche: asynchronous, text-based, persistent, one-to-many, typically pseudonymous and editorially moderated messages append to professionally produced news texts [@Herring2001; @Herring2007].

The case for studying the discourse rests partly on scale: within a decade of its introduction, commenting became one of the most widespread forms of public writing in English, characterised as a new "opinion pipeline" running alongside, and sometimes against, official editorial opinion [@Santana2-11]. Yet news comments have been studied far more intensively by journalism and communication scholars than by linguists [see, for example, CITATIONS]: little is know about their register characteristics, genre structure, or stance patterning. This asymeetry motivates the two traditions reviewed in Section 2.2.

The chapter's ultimate problem can be stated explicitly at the outset: existing research establishes ONC as an evaluative written register, but it has not adequately described the recurrent lexico-grammatical phraseology through which stance is realised in this register. The chapter therefore proceeds as an accumulation of evidence toward that problem, assembling the theoretical and methodological resources that its resolution requires.

### 1.2 Two research tradtions: communication studies and linguistic approaches

Research on news commenting has proceeded along two largely paralle tracks. The dominant tradition, rooted in journalism and communication studies, treats comments as participartory media practice, investigated through surveys, newsroom ethnographies and content analysis [CITATION of a general review]. Early studies documented the uneasy integration of user-generated content into professional frameworks [@HermidaThurman2008; @Thurman2008; @DomingoEtAl2008]: commenting was embraced rhetorically but managed as a moderation burden, a "clash of cultures*, and participatory spaces remained structurally peripheral. A second wave of work coded large samples for civility and deliberative merit: anonymous comment boards are substantially more uncivil than identified ones [@Santana2014]; incivility is platform and design dependent rather than inherent to online talk [@Rowe2015]; and active journalist engagement in threads improves deliberative norms [@StroudEtAl2015]. @DiakopoulosNaaman2011, working computationally, analysed over a million *New York Times* comments to identify markers of high-quality discourse and inform moderation design. @Reader2012 counters that the industry's "civility" framing itself constrains free expression, and @KsiazekSpringer2018 synthesise this literature around civility, deliberation, and platform governance.

The linguistic tradition, by contrast, treats comments as discourse. Its methodological anchor is @Herring2004a computer-mediated discourse analysis (CMDA), a "language-focused content analysis" that adapts the concerns of discourse analysis (structure, meaning, interation management, social phenomena) to online language. Linguists have approached ONC through pragmatics, notably working on impoliteness in reader responses to British online news [@NeurauterKessels2011; see also @RababahAlali2020], and increasingly through corpus methods: KehoeGee2019 reveal stable topic-based communities in 6.2 million *Guardian* comments, @CollinsNerlich2015 apply corpus-driven analysis to climate-change threads, and @TrnavacTaboada2023 compare engagement markers in English and Russian comments. The two taditions are complementary: the first asks who comments, why, and with what democratic consequences; the second, what comments are like as language. They have nonetheless rarely been integrated. This thesis aligns with the linguistic tradition while drawing on the first for institutional context.
### 1.3 From hybrid register to new variety: early spoken-written cline studies

The first wave of linguistic CMC research askedd where electronic language sits on the continuum between speech and writing [@Baron1984], addressed empirically through @Biber1988 multi-dimensional model of spoken and written variation. @FerraraBrunnerWhittemore1991 described real-time computer conferencing as an "emergent register" blending speech and writing; @CollotBelmore1996 read "electornic langauge" against Biber's dimensions as a single new variety; @Ytes1996b placed computer conferencing between spoken and written norms; and @Crystal2001 went furthest: Internet language is "something fundamentally different from both writing and speaking" [@Crystal2006, p. 24].

These studies established that CMD is linguitically patterned and measurable, amd their comparative designs remain methodological models. Yet they presupposed a relatively homogenous object; variation *within* CMC soon proved to exceed variation between CMC and offline modes [CITATION]. CMC,in short, is not linguistically homogenous, and the analysis of any online variety must begin from its particular situation: that is the move made by @Herring2007 faceted scheme.

### 1.4 Herring's (2007) faceted classification: locating ONC

@Herring2007 faceted classification scheme responds directly to this heterogeneity. Rather than treating each technology as a "genre of the medium", it profiles any instance of CMS along *medium facets* (technological affordances; Table 1) and *social facets* (situational context: participation structure, participant characteristics, purpose, topic, tone, norms). A single technology can host many genres: what distinguishes a news comment from a blog post is a configuration of facet values, not a platform label.

For ONC, the social facets are equally diagnostic: participation open and self-selected, purpose predominantly evaluative, topic inherited from the host article, and norms enforced by moderation. @HerringAndroutsopoulos2015 extend the scheme into the Web 2.0 era; @Rowe2015 confirms empirically that facet-level deign differences shape discourse. For the present thesis, the scheme specifies the *situational configuration* that constitutes ONC as a coherent object of register analysis, while the SOMETHING for analysing the linguistic variation that this situation produces comes from the Biberian tradition / Flagstadd school? reviewed in Section 2: Herring's scheme answers what the communicative situation is, register theory what linguistic variety the situation engenders. The facets also articulate the contextual differences between the learner corpus (a classroom context) and the BBC reference corpus (a live, moderated public forum).

### 1.5 Genre evolution: from letters to the editor to below-the-line comments

A parallel line of research addresses the genesis of digital genres. @YatesOrlikowski1992 structural account posits?construes? genres as typified communicative actions that migrate across media:  communities enact a *genre repetoire* in which a new medium reconfigures existing genres [@OrlilowskiYates1994], distinguishing reproduced genres from emergent genres adapted to the Web [@CrowstonWilliams2000]. HerringEtAl2005 characterised the weblog as a "bridging genre", at once derivative and novel [see also @GiltrwoStein2009].

ONC fit this account neatly. Their functional ancestor is the letter to the editor: online commentaries are more personal, more dialogic, and less gatekept than printed letters [@LanertJucker2011; @Landert2014], and anonymous comments expressed more extreme and diverse opinions than moderated letters during the 2008 US immigration debate [@McCluskeyHmielowski2012]. ONC thus reproduce the letters-to-the-editor function with new immediacy, interactively, pseudonymity, and scale. Yet apparent demographic differences in blog language are largely mediated by sub-genre [@HerringPaolillo2006], so corpus comparisons, learner and native alike, are valid only when genre is held constant.

### 1.6 A short history of below-the-line commenting: the early 2000s ecology

The early 2000s constitute a distinct era of commenting, which underwrites the choice of the BBC *Have Your Say* corpus as the reference baseline. BBC News Online launched in November 1997; *Have Your Say* was among the first institutional spaces for large-scale reader discussion, consolidated in 2005 within the BBC's user-generated content hub under public-service gatekeeping [@Harrison2010; @WardleWilliams2010]. Commercial commenting proliferated slightly later: the *Guardian*'s *Comment is Free* and five other UK sites in 2006 [@KehoeGee2019; @GrahamWright2015], the *Washington Post* in 2006 and the *New York Times* in 2007 [@Santana2011; see also @HermidaThurman2008].

@Herring2004b supplies the periodisation logic: CMC was then " slouching toward the ordinary". An early-2000s *Have Your Say* corpus thus captures the first generation of moderated, public-service, below-the-line commenting. It functions here as a genre and register benchmark rather than a perfectly matched control: the learner comments differ in production context, period, and writer status, difference that are themselves part of the study's interest, as Chapters 3 and 4 develop.

### 1.7 Chapter roadmap

The remainder of the chapter builds the scaffolding for the analysis: Section 2 reviews the theories of genre and register, Section 3 surveys the four approaches to corpus analysis, Section 4 turns to genre and register studies of CMC and ONC, and Section 5 reviews research on stance and evaluation; Section 6 foreshadows the thesis's combined methodology of multi-dimensional analyis and stance bundles. Two further pivots organise what follows beyond this chapter. Chapter 3 is a second literature review, of learner corpus research (LCR), necessary to contextualise the dynamics of L2 English writing within which the learner comments must be interpreted. Only then does Chapter 4 operationalise the methodology, introducing the corpora of interest and the analytical design.

## Corpus approaches to discourse: a framework for this chapter

## Conclusion: toward the present study

### The argument of the chapter
* Situational status: distinct CMD configuration derived from print letters [@Herring2007; @LandertJucker2011]
* Register status: emerging written, argumentative-evaluative variety [@BiberConrad2009; @Lee2008; @EhretTaboada2020, @EhretTaboada2021; @Bruce2010; @Marchand2013; @Gerhardt2021]
* Functional status: heavily negative, depersonalized evaluation for public alignment [@CavassoTaboada2021; @DuBois2007]

### What the literature cannot explain: the double gap
* Gap 1: Absence of a register-specific inventory of recurrent stance phraseology in ONC [@Bruce2010; @KolhatkarEtAl2020; @Barbieri2018; @Marchand2013]
* Gap 2: Lack of empirical accounts regarding L2 learner production of ONC stance resources
* Methodological requirement for representativeness and benchmark comparison [@BiberConrad2009; @EgbertBiberGray2022; @Harrison2010; @Herring2004b; @HerringPaolillo2006]
