2  Chapter 2

2.1 Introduction: Online news comments as computer-mediated communication

2.1.1 Online news comments as a form of CMC: why study language below the line

  • Learner ONC analysis vs. early-2000s BBC Have Your Say reference corpus
  • CMC (socio-technical phenomenon) vs. CMD (linguistic production) distinction (Herring 1996; Herring 2001)
  • Situational niche: asynchronous, text-based, persistent, pseudonymous, moderated (Herring 2001, 2007)
  • Scale and role as an alternative public “opinion pipeline” (Santana 2011)
  • Gap in register, genre, and stance descriptions in linguistics

2.1.2 Two research traditions: communication studies and linguistic approaches

  • Communication/journalism studies: participation, civility, moderation, deliberative quality (Hermida and Thurman 2008; Thurman 2008; Domingo et al. 2008; Santana 2014; Rowe 2015; Stroud et al. 2015; Diakopoulos and Naaman 2011; Reader 2012; Ksiazek and Springer 2018)
  • Linguistic tradition: CMDA framework (Herring 2004a), impoliteness pragmatics (Neurauter-Kessels 2011; Rabab’ah and Alali 2020), corpus-based discourse studies (Kehoe and Gee 2019; Collins and Nerlich 2015; Trnavac and Taboada 2023)
  • Complementarity and historical lack of integration between traditions

2.1.3 From hybrid register to new variety: early spoken–written cline studies

  • Electronic language on the speech–writing continuum (Baron 1984)
  • Early multi-dimensional and corpus comparisons (Biber 1988; Ferrara et al. 1991; Collot and Belmore 1996; Yates 1996)
  • Internet language framed as a distinct variety (Crystal 2001, 2006)
  • Internal variation exceeding medium-wide generalizations

2.1.4 Herring’s (2007) faceted classification: locating ONC

  • Rejection of monolithic “medium as genre” assumptions
  • Classification across technical medium facets and social facets (Herring 2007; Herring and Androutsopoulos 2015; Rowe 2015)
  • Profiling ONC situational parameters for register analysis
  • Framework for comparing learner classroom data with public reference data

2.1.5 Genre evolution: from letters to the editor to below-the-line comments

  • Structurational genre repertoires and cybergenre emergence (Yates and Orlikowski 1992; Orlikowski and Yates 1994; Crowston and Williams 2000)
  • Weblogs and comments as bridging genres (Herring et al. 2005; Giltrow and Stein 2009)
  • Letters to the editor as offline functional ancestor (Landert and Jucker 2011; Landert 2014; McCluskey and Hmielowski 2012)
  • Requirement to control for sub-genre in corpus comparisons (Herring and Paolillo 2006)

2.1.6 A short history of below-the-line commenting: the early-2000s ecology

  • Launch of BBC News Online (1997) and Have Your Say UGC hub (2005) (Harrison 2010; Wardle and Williams 2010)
  • Emergence of UK and US commercial commenting platforms (Kehoe and Gee 2019; Graham and Wright 2015; Santana 2011; Hermida and Thurman 2008)
  • Early-2000s baseline as the initial era of normalized CMC (Herring 2004b)

2.1.7 Chapter roadmap

  • Section 2: Genre and register theoretical frameworks

  • Section 4: Genre and register studies of CMC and ONC
  • Section 5: Stance and evaluation frameworks

2.2 Genre and register: theoretical foundations

2.2.1 Three traditions of genre theory

  • 1980s contextual reaction against formal text description (Hyon 1996)
  • Variable positioning of textual form vs. social context across schools

2.2.1.1 The SFL tradition: register as field, tenor and mode; genre as staged, goal-oriented social process

  • Language as social semiotic (Halliday 1978)
  • Context of situation: field, tenor, and mode mapping to metafunctions (Halliday and Hasan 1989; Halliday and Matthiessen 2004)
  • Sydney School model: genre stratified above register (Martin 1984, 1992; Martin and Rose 2008)

2.2.1.2 The ESP tradition: communicative purpose, move analysis and the complete text

  • Discourse communities and shared communicative purpose (Swales 1990)
  • Move and step analysis of whole texts (Bhatia 1993, 2004)
  • Limitations of single communicative purpose in multi-party texts (Askehave and Swales 2001)

2.2.1.3 The New Rhetoric tradition: genre as social action

  • Typified rhetorical action in recurring situations (Miller 1984)
  • Mutual constitution of genres and activity systems (Bazerman 1988, 1994)
  • Socio-cognitive genre schemas and disciplinary power (Berkenkotter and Huckin 1995)

2.2.1.4 Convergences and tensions between the three traditions

  • Shared foundations: situated, recurring, purposeful communication (Hyon 1996)
  • Methodological divergences: semiotic stratification, move analysis, and ethnography (Martin 1992; Swales 1990; Berkenkotter and Huckin 1995)
  • Incompatible definitions and scope of “register”

2.2.2 Register and genre in the text-linguistic tradition

  • Multi-Dimensional Analysis (MDA) and factor analysis of co-occurring features (Biber 1988)
  • External, situational definition of register vs. formal text types
  • Tripartite distinction: register, genre, and style perspectives (Biber and Conrad 2009, 2019)
  • Quantitative scaling to web-scale document populations (Biber and Egbert 2018)

2.2.3 Genre and register in digital environments

  • Cybergenre evolution: replication, variant, and novel stages (Shepherd and Watters 1998)
  • Pervasive genre hybridism and individualization online (Santini 2007; Askehave and Nielsen 2005; Giltrow and Stein 2009)
  • Participatory news article as a composite digital genre (Bruce 2010; Landert and Jucker 2011)
  • ONC as a hybrid, argumentative-evaluative written register (Ehret and Taboada 2020, 2021)

2.3 Corpus approaches to discourse: a framework for this chapter

2.3.1 Lee (2008): corpus-informed, corpus-supported, corpus-driven and corpus-induced approaches

  • Continuum of text preservation vs. abstraction (Lee 2008; Tognini-Bonelli 2001)
  • Corpus-informed: manual qualitative analysis of whole, structured texts
  • Corpus-supported: manual theory-driven coding combined with quantification (Burns et al. 2014)
  • Corpus-driven: automatic feature tagging and inductive quantification (Biber 1988; Gerhardt 2021)
  • Corpus-induced: fully automated bag-of-words processing and machine learning (Santini 2007; Sharoff et al. 2010)

2.3.2 Corpus design and representativeness

  • Domain representativeness vs. linguistic representativeness (Egbert et al. 2022)
  • Contrastive requirement: register features visible through reference comparisons (Biber 1988; Egbert et al. 2015; Biber and Egbert 2018)

2.3.3 Strengths and limits of the four approaches

  • Trade-offs between interpretive delicacy and sample scale
  • Methodological synergy in corpus-assisted discourse studies (Baker et al. 2008; Larsson et al. 2020, 2022)
  • Inductive power vs. theoretical assumptions in MDA (Berber Sardinha and Veirano Pinto 2019)
  • Linguistic opacity and lack of interpretability in induced models (Sharoff et al. 2010; Egbert et al. 2020; Larsson and Biber 2024; Martin 2017; Sharoff 2018)

2.3.4 The framework as organising device

  • Heuristic structure for reviewing CMC/ONC genre, register, and stance literature

2.4 Genre and register analysis of CMC and ONC

2.4.1 Corpus-informed approaches: qualitative genre analysis of news texts and comment spaces

  • Move analysis of the participatory news article ensemble (Bruce 2010)
  • Evolution of audience participation from print letters to online comments (Landert and Jucker 2011; McCluskey and Hmielowski 2012)
  • Pragmatic repertoires of face-threatening acts online (Neurauter-Kessels 2011)
  • Limitations in domain representativeness (Egbert et al. 2022)

2.4.2 Corpus-supported approaches: purpose-built annotated comment corpora

  • SENSEI corpus: multi-stage manual annotation for conversation summarization (Barker et al. 2016; Barker and Gaizauskas 2016)
  • YNACC: taxonomy coding for conversation quality (ERICs) (Napoles et al. 2017)
  • SOCC: reliability-tested annotation of constructiveness and toxicity Kolhatkar and Taboada (2017b)
  • Manual Appraisal schemes on commercial social media (Burns et al. 2014)

2.4.3 Corpus-driven approaches: multidimensional and register analyses

2.4.3.1 The early spoken–written cline revisited

  • Synchronous chat as interactive written discourse (Ferrara et al. 1991)
  • Corpus measures of early conferencing and email (Yates 1996)
  • MD analysis of bulletin boards and hybridity models (Collot and Belmore 1996; Baron 1998)

2.4.3.2 MDA of CMC registers

  • Multi-dimensional scoring of IRC and ICQ chat (Jonsson 2015)
  • Exploratory MDA of web pages, blogs, and comments (Biber and Kurjian 2007; Grieve et al. 2010; Titak and Roberson 2013)
  • Additive vs. full MDA of Internet varieties (Berber Sardinha 2014, 2018)
  • Large-scale web registers (CORE) and micro-blogging styles Clarke and Grieve (2019)

2.4.3.3 MDA and register analysis of ONC specifically

  • SOCC vs. ICE-Canada: ONC as an argumentative-evaluative written register (Ehret and Taboada 2020)
  • SOCC vs. CORE: position among online registers (Ehret and Taboada 2021)
  • Additive MDA of blog comments on Biber’s 1988 dimensions (Gerhardt 2021)
  • Formulaicity and lexical bundles in BBC Have Your Say (Marchand 2013)
  • Semantic domains, keywords, and discourse prosody in comments (Collins and Nerlich 2015; Collins 2019; Baker and McGlashan 2020)

2.4.3.4 Full versus additive MDA

  • Dimensions extracted de novo vs. projection onto base models (Berber Sardinha et al. 2019)
  • Suitability for emerging comment registers Ehret and Taboada (2021)
  • Dependence of register findings on comparison corpus composition (Egbert et al. 2022)

2.4.4 Corpus-induced approaches: automatic genre and register classification

  • Genre hybridity and individualization in web classification (Santini 2007; Mehler et al. 2010)
  • Reliability issues in expert web-genre annotation (Sharoff et al. 2010; Egbert et al. 2015)
  • Functional Text Dimensions independent of topic (Sharoff 2018)
  • Neural register classification over open web data (Laippala et al. 2022)
  • High classification accuracy vs. linguistic opacity (Egbert et al. 2020; Larsson et al. 2022)

2.4.5 Section conclusion: register and genre revisited

  • Dual lens: macro-textual genre context vs. pervasive register features (Bruce 2010; Ehret and Taboada 2020)
  • Salience of evaluation across multiple methodological traditions (Cavasso and Taboada 2021)

2.5 Stance and evaluation in CMC and ONC

2.5.1 Stance, subjectivity, intersubjectivity, evaluation, appraisal: a terminological map

  • Stance as linguistic marking of attitude and epistemic status (Biber and Finegan 1988; Gray and Biber 2012)
  • Subjectivity and dialogic intersubjectivity (Du Bois 2007; Du Bois and Kärkkäinen 2012)
  • Evaluation as broad cover term in discourse (Bednarek 2006; Hunston 2011)
  • SFL Appraisal system taxonomy (Martin and White 2005)
  • Metadiscourse and computational sentiment frameworks (Hyland 2005a; Pang and Lee 2008)

2.5.2 Corpus-informed approaches: the Appraisal framework

  • Tripartite system: Attitude, Engagement, Graduation (Martin 2000; White 2003; Martin and White 2005)
  • Attitude subtypes: Affect (emotion), Judgement (ethics/behavior), Appreciation (aesthetic/value)
  • Syntactic patterns associated with Attitude categories (Taboada and Grieve 2004)
  • Inscribed (explicit) vs. evoked (implicit) evaluation
  • Engagement: monoglossic vs. heteroglossic (expanding vs. contracting) (White 2003)
  • Graduation: Force (scaling) and Focus (prototypicality)
  • Cross-genre and CMC applications (White 1998; Coffin and O’Halloran 2006; Martin 1996; Painter 2003; Love 2006; Eggins and Slade 1997; Zappavigna 2012)

2.5.3 Corpus-supported approaches: Appraisal with quantification and stancetaking

  • Quantified Appraisal analysis of SOCC comments (Cavasso and Taboada 2021)
  • Overwhelming prevalence of negative evaluation and preference for Judgement/Appreciation over Affect
  • Cross-linguistic Engagement patterns in news comments (Trnavac and Taboada 2023)
  • Retrieval of stance and affect on social platforms (Burns et al. 2014)
  • Stance triangle: evaluation, positioning, alignment (Du Bois 2007)
  • Interactional epistemic stance and networked positioning Kärkkäinen (2006)

2.5.4 Corpus-driven approaches: evaluation, semantic prosody, metadiscourse, grammatical stance, lexical bundles

  • Semantic prosody and evaluative collocation (Louw 1993; Hunston 2007)
  • Phraseology and local grammars of evaluation (Hunston and Sinclair 2000; Hunston 2011)
  • Inductive evaluation dimensions and community voices on RateMyProfessors (Millar and Hunston 2015)
  • Metadiscourse models of stance and engagement Hyland (2005b)
  • Grammatical stance devices: adverbials, complement clauses, modals (Biber et al. 1999; Biber 2006; Gray and Biber 2012)
  • Stance bundles across spoken, academic, and blog registers (Biber et al. 2004; Biber and Barbieri 2007; Hyland 2008; Barbieri 2018)
  • Bundle analysis of BBC Have Your Say and non-contiguous concgrams (Marchand 2013; Greaves 2009; Greaves and Warren 2010)

2.5.5 Corpus-induced approaches: sentiment analysis of comments

  • Sentiment analysis tasks: polarity, subjectivity, aspect extraction (Pang and Lee 2008; Liu 2012)
  • Lexicon-based methods with valence shifters: SO-CAL, NRC Emotion Lexicon (Taboada and Grieve 2004; Taboada et al. 2011; Mohammad and Turney 2013)
  • Machine learning scoring of informal digital texts: SentiStrength (Thelwall et al. 2010)
  • Automated prediction of quality, constructiveness, and toxicity in ONC (Napoles et al. 2017; Kolhatkar and Taboada 2017a; Kolhatkar et al. 2020)
  • Limitations: polarity scores without lexico-grammatical description (Larsson et al. 2022)

2.5.6 Section conclusion

  • Synthesis: ONC as an inherently evaluative, other-directed register (Cavasso and Taboada 2021; Du Bois 2007)
  • Phraseological, community-specific realization of evaluative meaning (Hunston 2011; Millar and Hunston 2015; Barbieri 2018)
  • Operationalizing stance via recurrent phraseological bundles (Biber et al. 1999; Biber 2006)

2.6 Conclusion: toward the present study

2.6.1 The argument of the chapter

  • Situational status: distinct CMD configuration derived from print letters (Herring 2007; Landert and Jucker 2011)
  • Register status: emerging written, argumentative-evaluative variety Ehret and Taboada (2021)
  • Functional status: heavily negative, depersonalized evaluation for public alignment (Cavasso and Taboada 2021; Du Bois 2007)

2.6.2 What the literature cannot explain: the double gap

  • Gap 1: Absence of a register-specific inventory of recurrent stance phraseology in ONC (Bruce 2010; Kolhatkar et al. 2020; Barbieri 2018; Marchand 2013)
  • Gap 2: Lack of empirical accounts regarding L2 learner production of ONC stance resources
  • Methodological requirement for representativeness and benchmark comparison (Biber and Conrad 2009; Egbert et al. 2022; Harrison 2010; Herring 2004b; Herring and Paolillo 2006)

2.6.3 The design of the present study

  • Additive MDA on Biber’s (1988) dimensions for register comparison Ehret and Taboada (2021)
  • Automatic extraction and functional categorization of stance bundles (Biber et al. 1999; Biber 2006; Barbieri 2018; Millar and Hunston 2015)
  • Methodological bridge between corpus-driven extraction and corpus-supported functional interpretation
  • Road ahead: Chapter 2 (LCR context) and Chapter 3 (Corpus methodology and design)
