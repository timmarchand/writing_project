2  Online news comments as computer-mediated communication

2.1 Online news comments as a form of CMC: why study language below the line

The present thesis analyses a learner corpus of online news comments (ONC) written by Japanese university students of English across six cohorts, against a reference corpus of comments from the BBC’s Have Your Say platform dating from the early 2000s. The object of study first requires situating: ONC are a form of computer-mediated communication (CMC), and CMC research supplies the context for a linguistic analysis of news commenting. Herring’s (1996, 1) canonical definition describes CMC as “communication that takes place between human beings via the instrumentality of computers”. Herring (2001) later distinguished computer-mediated communication, the socio-technical phenomenon, from computer-mediated discourse (CMD), the language produced through it; the latter term is used here when the focus is on language itself. Within the ecology of CMD, ONC occupy a distinctive niche: asynchronous, text-based, persistent, one-to-many, typically pseudonymous and editorially moderated messages appended to professionally produced news texts (Herring 2001, 2007). The case for studying the discourse rests partly on scale: within a decade of its introduction, commenting became one of the most widespread forms of public writing in English, characterised as a new “opinion pipeline” running alongside, and sometimes against, official editorial opinion (Santana 2011). Yet news comments have been studied far more intensively by journalism and communication scholars than by linguists (see, for example, Santana 2014; Stroud et al. 2015; Ksiazek and Springer 2018): little is known about their register characteristics, genre structure, or stance patterning. This asymmetry motivates the two traditions reviewed in Section 1.2. The chapter’s ultimate problem can be stated explicitly at the outset: existing research establishes ONC as an evaluative written register, but it has not adequately described the recurrent lexico-grammatical phraseology through which stance is realised in this register. The chapter therefore proceeds as an accumulation of evidence toward that problem, assembling the theoretical and methodological resources that its resolution requires.

2.2 Two research tradtions: communication studies and linguistic approaches

Research on news commenting has proceeded along two largely paralle tracks. The dominant tradition, rooted in journalism and communication studies, treats comments as participatory media practice, investigated through surveys, newsroom ethnographies and content analysis (for a review, see Ksiazek and Springer 2018). Early studies documented the uneasy integration of user-generated content into professional frameworks (Hermida and Thurman 2008; Thurman 2008; Domingo et al. 2008): commenting was embraced rhetorically but managed as a moderation burden, a “clash of cultures”, and participatory spaces remained structurally peripheral. A second wave of work coded large samples for civility and deliberative merit: anonymous comment boards are substantially more uncivil than identified ones (Santana 2014); incivility depends on platform and design rather than being inherent to online talk (Rowe 2015); and active journalist engagement in threads improves deliberative norms (Stroud et al. 2015). Diakopoulos and Naaman (2011), working computationally, analysed over a million New York Times comments to identify markers of high-quality discourse and inform moderation design. Reader (2012) counters that the industry’s “civility” framing itself constrains free expression, and Ksiazek and Springer (2018) synthesise this literature around civility, deliberation, and platform governance. The linguistic tradition, by contrast, treats comments as discourse. Its methodological anchor is Herring’s (2004a) computer-mediated discourse analysis (CMDA), a “language-focused content analysis” that adapts the concerns of discourse analysis (structure, meaning, interaction management, social phenomena) to online language. Linguists have approached ONC through pragmatics, notably working on impoliteness in reader responses to British online news (Neurauter-Kessels 2011; see also Rabab’ah and Alali 2020), and increasingly through corpus methods: Kehoe and Gee (2019) reveal stable topic-based communities in 6.2 million Guardian comments, Collins and Nerlich (2015) apply corpus-driven analysis to climate-change threads, and Trnavac and Taboada (2023) compare engagement markers in English and Russian comments. The two taditions are complementary: the first asks who comments, why, and with what democratic consequences; the second, what comments are like as language. They have nonetheless rarely been integrated. This thesis aligns with the linguistic tradition while drawing on the first for institutional context.

2.3 From hybrid register to new variety: early spoken-written cline studies

The first wave of linguistic CMC research asked where electronic language sits on the continuum between speech and writing (Baron 1984), a question addressed empirically through Biber’s (1988) multi-dimensional model of spoken and written variation. Ferrara et al. (1991) described real-time computer conferencing as an “emergent register” blending speech and writing; Collot and Belmore (1996) read “electornic langauge” against Biber’s dimensions as a single new variety; Yates (1996) placed computer conferencing between spoken and written norms; and Crystal (2001) went furthest: Internet language is “something fundamentally different from both writing and speaking” (Crystal 2006, 24). These studies established that CMD is linguitically patterned and measurable, amd their comparative designs remain methodological models. Yet they presupposed a relatively homogeneous object; variation within CMC soon proved to exceed variation between CMC and offline modes (Herring and Paolillo 2006; Herring 2007; Grieve et al. 2010). CMC, in short, is not linguistically homogeneous, and the analysis of any online variety must begin from its particular situation: that is the move made by Herring’s (2007) faceted scheme.

2.4 Herring’s (2007) faceted classification: locating ONC

Herring’s (2007) faceted classification scheme responds directly to this heterogeneity. Rather than treating each technology as a “genre of the medium”, it profiles any instance of CMD along medium facets (technological affordances; Table 1) and social facets (situational context: participation structure, participant characteristics, purpose, topic, tone, norms). A single technology can host many genres: what distinguishes a news comment from a blog post is a configuration of facet values, not a platform label. Table 1 profiles ONC along Herring’s medium facets. For ONC, the social facets are equally diagnostic: participation open and self-selected, purpose predominantly evaluative, topic inherited from the host article, and norms enforced by moderation. Herring and Androutsopoulos (2015) extend the scheme into the Web 2.0 era; Rowe (2015) confirms empirically that differences in platform design at the level of individual facets shape discourse. For the present thesis, the scheme specifies the situational configuration that constitutes ONC as a coherent object of register analysis, while the framework for analysing the linguistic variation that this situation produces comes from the text-linguistic register tradition associated with Biber and colleagues, reviewed in Section 2: Herring’s scheme answers what the communicative situation is, register theory what linguistic variety the situation engenders. The facets also articulate the contextual differences between the learner corpus (a classroom context) and the BBC reference corpus (a live, moderated public forum).

2.5 Genre evolution: from letters to the editor to below-the-line comments

A parallel line of research addresses the genesis of digital genres. Yates and Orlikowski (1992), in their structurational account, conceptualise genres as typified communicative actions that migrate across media: communities enact a genre repertoire in which a new medium reconfigures existing genres (Orlikowski and Yates 1994), and Crowston and Williams (2000) later distinguished genres reproduced on the Web from emergent genres adapted to it. Herring et al. (2005) characterised the weblog as a “bridging genre”, at once derivative and novel (see also Giltrow and Stein 2009). ONC fit this account neatly. Their functional ancestor is the letter to the editor: online commentaries are more personal, more dialogic, and less gatekept than printed letters (Landert and Jucker 2011; Landert 2014), and anonymous comments expressed more extreme and diverse opinions than moderated letters during the 2008 US immigration debate (McCluskey and Hmielowski 2012). ONC thus reproduce the letters-to-the-editor function with new immediacy, interactivity, pseudonymity, and scale. Yet apparent demographic differences in blog language turn out to be largely confounded with sub-genre (Herring and Paolillo 2006), so corpus comparisons, learner and native-speaker alike, are valid only when genre is held constant.

2.6 A short history of below-the-line commenting: the early 2000s ecology

The early 2000s constitute a distinct era of commenting, and this underwrites my choice of the BBC Have Your Say data as the reference corpus. BBC News Online launched in November 1997; Have Your Say was among the first institutional spaces for large-scale reader discussion, consolidated in 2005 within the BBC’s user-generated content hub under public-service gatekeeping (Harrison 2010; Wardle and Williams 2010). Commercial commenting proliferated slightly later: the Guardian’s Comment is Free and five other UK sites in 2006 (Kehoe and Gee 2019; Graham and Wright 2015), the Washington Post in 2006 and the New York Times in 2007 (Santana 2011; see also Hermida and Thurman 2008). Herring (2004b) supplies the periodisation logic: CMC was then ” slouching toward the ordinary”. An early-2000s Have Your Say corpus thus captures the first generation of moderated, public-service, below-the-line commenting. It functions here as a genre and register benchmark rather than a strictly matched comparison corpus: the learner comments differ in production context, period, and writer status, differences that are themselves part of the study’s interest, as Chapters 3 and 4 develop.

3 Genre and register: theoretical foundations

Section 1 situated online news comments (ONC) within computer-mediated communication (CMC) and traced the emergence of commenting as a recognisable discursive practice. Before the empirical literature on ONC can be reviewed, however, the two contstructs on which most of the literature silently relies, genre and register, must be disentangled; the terms are used inconsistently across disciplines, and in one influential tradition they are effectively inverted. This section reviews the three major traditions of genre theory (Section 2.1), contrasts them with the text-linguistic account of register developed by Biber and colleagues (Section 2.2), and considers how both constructs have been extended to digital environments (Section 2.3), where the hybrid, fast-evolving character of online texts puts classical definitions under particular strain.

3.1 Three traditions of genre theory

Genre theory consolidated in the 1980s as a reaction against formalist approaches that described texts without reference to their contexts of use. Hyon’s (1996) comparative review distinguished three schools that have structured the field since: English for specific purposes (ESP), North American New Rhetoric, and Australian systemic functional linguistics (SFL). All three treat genre as socially situated communication, but locate it differently and assign different weight to textual form and social context (Hyon 1996).

3.1.1 The SFL tradition: register as field, tenor and mode; genre as staged, goal-oriented social process

Systemic functional linguistics grounds genre and register in a theory of language as social semiotic, in which situation an linguistic form are systematically, not accidentally, related (Halliday 1978). Halliday and Hasan (1989) gave this insight its canonical shape: context of situation is described through field (what is happening), tenor (who is taking part), and mode (what role language is playing), each variable resonating with one metafunction, and register is a functionall variety of language determined by situation (Halliday and Matthiessen 2004). Martin (1984) introduced the departure that defines the Sydney School: register is recontrued as the context of situtation itself, while genre is located at the higher level of context of culture as a staged, goal-oriented social process (Martin 1992), the genres of a culture mapping into families of stories, histories, reports, explanations and procedures (Martin and Rose 2008). The contribution is double: a metafunctionally grounded account of register, and a stratified model in which genre sits above register as the contextual plane that orchestrates it.

3.1.2 The ESP tradition: communicative purpose, move analysis and the complete text

The ESP tradition, developed for the teaching of academic and professional English, anchors genre in the communicative purposes of discourse communities. Swales (1990, 58) supplied the tradition’s canonical definition:

“A genre comprises a class of communicative events, the members of which share some set of communicative purposes. These purposes are recognized by the expert members of the parent discourse community and thereby constitute a rationale for the genre… In addition to purpose, exemplars of a genre exhibit various patterns of similarity in terms of structure, style, content and intended audience.”

Communicative purpose is explicitly “a privileged criterion” (Swales 1990, 58): genres are identified by what they are for, and their schematic structure, the ordered series of moves and steps, follows from that rationale. Move analysis thus made genre operational for complete texts rather than decontextualised linguistic features. Bhatia (1993, 75) extended the framework to professional settings, each move subservient to the genre’s overall communicative purpose; Bhatia (2004) later recast genre as a socio-cognitive construct whose interdiscursivity and generic integrity account for genre hybridisation across professional worlds. Askehave and Swales (2001) cautioned from within that communicative purpose cannot be the sole criterion, since purposes are often layered, contested or attributed post hoc, a caution relevant when a single purpose is later assigned to a multi-party genre such as the online news comment.

3.1.3 The New Rhetoric tradition: genre as social action

North American New Rhetoric shifted attention from the text to the action it performs. Miller (1984, 159) defined genres as “typified rhetorical actions based in recurrent situations”: genre knowledge is acquired through participation rather than formal instruction, and classification is legitimate only once genre has been understood as action (Miller 1984). Bazerman (1988) traced the emergence of the experimental research article, showing genre form and scientific activity to be mutually constitutive, and later developed the concept of genre systems, interlocking sets of genres organising complex social activity, such as the applications, examiners’ reports and granted patents of the patent system (Bazerman 1994). Berkenkotter and Huckin (1995) added a socio-cognitive dimension, construing genre knowledge as simultaneously cognitive (schemas built from prior genre experience) and social (embedded in disciplinary culture and power relations).

3.1.4 Convergences and tensions between the three traditions

The three traditions converge on fundamentals: genres are recurrent, socially recognised, purposeful, and inseparable from social context, and recurring situations leave systematic traces in discourse (Hyon 1996). They diverge in emphasis and method: the Sydney School offers the most elaborate stratified model of language and context (Martin 1992; Martin and Rose 2008); ESP is the most textually operational (though see Askehave and Swales 2001); and New Rhetoric’s ethnographic methods resist generalisation across large text collections (Berkenkotter and Huckin 1995). 3.2 Register and genre in the text-linguistic tradition

The corpus-linguistic tradition associated with Douglas Biber approaches the same territory from the opposite direction. Biber (1988) established the multidimensional (MD) approach, subjecting 67 lexico-grammatical features across 23 spoken and written genres of English to factor analysis and extracting seven factors, six of which he interpreted as dimensions of variation, including Involved versus Informational Production and Narrative versus Non-Narrative Concerns. Two principles are key. First, text varieties are defined by external, situational criteria (who produces the text, for whom, in what channel, for what purpose) rather than by linguistic similarity, with Biber (1988, 70) explicitly distinguishing such externally defined categories, which he then termed genres, from “text types”, defined by linguistic form. Second, registers are distinguished less by categorical register markers, features restricted to a particular variety, than by the relative frequencies of pervasive register features that occur throughout texts and co-occur in functionally interpretable ways (Biber 1988; Biber and Conrad 2009). Biber and Conrad (2009) systematised this position into three analytical perspectives on text varieties: the register perspective combines pervasive linguistic features with the variety’s situation of use; the genre perspective shares the situational analysis, but focuses on conventional features of complete texts (openings, closings, move-like elements) that may occur only once yet recur in all texts of the type; and the style perspective attends to features not functionally motivated by situational context (Biber and Conrad 2009, 2). A register, accordingly, is “a variety associated with a particular situation of use (including particular communicative purposes)” (Biber and Conrad 2009, 6). Register analysis presupposes comparison: because register features are matters of frequency and co-occurrence, they are identifiable as characteristic only by contrasting one variety against others (Biber and Conrad 2009). The framework also accomodates the everyday sense of register as formality, and regards register variation itself as a linguistic universal (Biber and Conrad 2009); the second edition updates the treatment of electronic registers and adds an analysis of hybrid registers combining features of distinct varieties (Biber and Conrad 2019), a development of obvious relevance to online discourse. This now puts us in a position to clarify the terminological relationship between the text-linguistic tradition and the genre traditions presented in Section 2.1. What Biber and Conrad call the genre perspective corresponds roughly to the text-structural focus of SFL and ESP genre analysis (the conventional staging of complete texts), while their register perspective covers what the Sydney School distributes across register and genre together. The frameworks answer different analytical questions rather than competing over the same one, and for that reason offer complementary perspectives for the present study: genre analysis captures the macro-structure and social purpose of whole texts, register analysis the pervasive linguistic texture that situational factors engender. This complementarity is claimed for the study’s purposes without adjudicating the genuine theoretical differences between genres construed as context of culture, as communicateive event, and as rhetorical action. Crucially, the register framework also extends to very large text populations: Biber and Egbert (2018) sampled documents from the searchable web, coded their situational characteristics, and demonstrated through MD analysis that register categories survive online, albeit in new hybrid combinations. Because register is situationally defined and characterised by pervasive, countable features, it is the perspective most readily operationalised by corpus methods, a connection taken up in Section 3.

3.3 Genre and register in digital environments

Digital environments test both constructs. Shepherd and Watters (1998) coined the term cybergenre, proposing that web-based genres evolve from offline antecedents through replication and variant stages towards novel genres unique to the medium, and that genre identification online must consider functionality alongside content and form: what a digital artefact does is part of what it is. Santini (2007) documented the consequences for classification: web pages display pervasive genre hybridism (multiple genres within a single page) and individualization (idiosyncratic instances fitting no established category), motivating flexible, multi-label, feature-based descriptions rather than discrete taxonomies, a position aligned with the register persepctive’s gradient view of variation. Askehave and Nielsen (2005) proposed a two-dimensional model in which a text described in Swalesian terms (communicative purpose, moves, rhetorical strategies) is embedded in a digital medium with its own distinctive properties (hyperlinks, navigation, multimodality), so that users alternate between a reading mode and a navigating mode; they argue that classical genre theory underestimates the medium’s role. Giltrow and Stein’s (2009) edited volume posed the underlying question directly, whether genre theory survives its migration online, and answered it with qualified continuity: online genres are recognisably descended from offline antecedents, yet reshaped by medium affordances. For news discourse specifically, Bruce (2010) analysed the participatory news article (the online news article together with its appended reader comments) as a hybrid genre in the cybergenre sense, characterising the comments as generally subjective, refering either to the host article or to previously posted comments, and following a general pattern in which a statement of view is followed by a justifiation (a fuller discussion follows in Section 4.1). The earliest genre-theoretic treatment of ONC already displays the tensions outlined above: the participatory article is hybrid, traceable to an offline antecedent, the letter to the editor, whose online descendants are immediately self-published and editorially unfiltered (Landert and Jucker 2011), and describable only with reference to medium affordances such as threading and moderation. Where genre-based description risks rigidity, Herring’s (2007) facet-based scheme offers an alternative, classifying computer-mediated discourse by open-ended clusters of medium and social facets that function in practice as situational parameters akin to those of register analysis. Register-oriented work confirms that such hybridity is the norm: Ehret and Taboada (2020) found that online news comments pattern as a written, argumentative-evaluative register combining features of spoken informality with formal writing, and that online registers generally are less cleanly delineated than traditional ones. Digital genres and registers thus require both theoretical lenses: a genre perspective for whole-text conventions and the comment’s relation to its host article, and a register perspective for pervasive linguistic texture. Two consequences for the thesis follow. ONC can be viewed through the genre lens as part of a complete communicative event, the participatory news article (Bruce 2010), with its own staging and its intertextual dependence on the host text; the same comments can be viewed through the register lens as a situationally conditioned variety whose pervasive linguistic texture can be measured and compared (Ehret and Taboada 2020, 2021). This thesis occupies precisely this intersection: it investigates the linguistic realisation of the genre as a register, while retaining genre-level awareness of the communicative event in which the comments participate. The register lens, being situationally defined and quantifiable, invites corpus-based operationalisation, the subject of the following section.

4 Corpus approaches to discourse: a framework for this chapter

Sections 1 and 2 established online news comments (ONC) as a site of computer-mediated communication (CMC) and surveyed genre and register theory; what remains is to specify the methodological lens for reviewing the empirical literature. As has been observed elsewhere, “corpus linguistics” is a misleading unitary label for a heterogeneous field (Egbert et al. 2020): studies that all claim to be “corpus-based” differ radically in how they treat texts as data, how they annotate and quantify, and what their evidence can support. This chapter therefore adopts Lee’s (2008, 86–99) four-way distinction of corpus-based approaches as its organising framework.

4.1 Lee (2008): corpus-informed, corpus-supported, corpus-driven and corpus-induced approaches

The status of this framework requires clarification at the outset. I adopt Lee’s (2008) four categories as an organising heuristic for comparing methodological emphases, not as a fixed taxonomy of corpus research: I assign the studies reviewed in Sections 4 and 5 to the categories retrospectively, and some sit between them. The justification for imposing the scheme nonetheless is that this thesis is interested in not merely what has been found, but in how different methodologica treatments of data make different kinds of claims possible. Lee (2008) positions corpus-informed, corpus-supported and corpus-induced approaches as points along a continuum for which “corpus-based” is the superordinate category, discussed alongside the corpus-driven tradition originating with Tognini-Bonelli (2001). (The terminology here follows Lee (2008); the first variant is occasionally rendered “corpus-inspired” in secondary usage.) The continuum runs from maximal preservation of the text to maximal abstraction away from it. In the corpus-informed approach, the corpus functions essentially as a strucuted collection of texts for qualitative analysis. Annotations are added manually, the text remains highly structured, typically at the level of the complete text or move, and methods such as genre analysis, systemic functional linguistics (SFL), conversation analysis or content analysis are applied to whole, co-textually intact documents (Lee 2008). The corpus-supported approach combines qualitative interpretation and quantification: texts may be manually coded according to a prior theoretical framework, but frequencies are then extracted and used to test hypotheses. The text remains structured, though at a finer grain: individual turns in conversation-analytic work, or the clause in SFL-based analysis (Lee 2008). An illustrative CMC example is the manual Appraisal coding in corporate Facebook pages by Burns et al. (2014). The corpus-driven approach shifts the balance towards the quantitative. Annotations are predominantly automatic (part-of-speech tagging and related markup), textual features are atomised at the word or phrase level and aggragated for quantification, and analysis is exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-testing, with a professed theoretical neutrality (Lee 2008; Tognini-Bonelli 2001). The paradigm case is multi-dimensional analysis (MDA), in which factor analysis over counts of dozens of lexico-grammatical features yields dimensions of register variation (Biber 1988); the additive variant, which scores new registers against an existing dimensional model rather than extracting new factors, has been applied to online comments data by Gerhardt (2021). Finally, the corpus-induced approach is almost wholly quantitative: annotation and classification are fully automatic, features are entirely atomised and aggregated, for instance into “bag-of-words” representations, with little researcher intervention or interpretation, and the purpose is typically a practical application, such as automatic genre classification or sentiment filtering of large web collections (Lee 2008; Santini 2007; Sharoff et al. 2010). 4.2 Corpus design and representativeness

The evidentiary value of any of these approaches depends on what the corpus can be taken to represent. Egbert et al. (2022) define representativeness as the extent to which a corpus reflects the full range of variability in the variety it is intended to represent, distinguishing domain representativeness (coverage of the situational and textual categories of the target domain) from linguistic representativeness (coverage of the resulting distribution of linguistic features). The distinction matters acutely for ONC research: a convenience sample of comments scraped from a few outlets may suffice for corpus-informed reading, but cannot license population-level claims about ONC as a register without an explicit account of its domain coverage (Egbert et al. 2022). A second principle is that register characteristics emergemost clearly in contrast: the target corpus is compared against other registers or reference corpora, the comparative logic built into MDA from its inception (Biber 1988) and operationalised for the web inn the Corpus of Online Registers of English (Egbert et al. 2015; Biber and Egbert 2018). Claims about what is distinctive of ONC therefore presuppose a principled choice of comparison data, not merely a large sample of comments.

4.3 Strengths and limitations of the four approaches

Each variant trades depth of interpretation against scale and generalisability. Corpus-informed work retains co-text and discourse structure, keeping categories interpretable and theoretically meaningful, a decisive advantage for prosodic, dispersed phenomena such as Appraisal that resist the counting of individual items (Martin and White 2005; Martin 2017). However, its small samples limit generalisability, and the reliability of manual annotation is a documented pressure point (Larsson et al. 2020). Corpus-supported approaches achieve what Baker et al. (2008) call a “methodological synergy”: corpus evidence discliplines intuition while qualitative reading restores context, making this the natural home of much defensible discourse work on CMC. They nonetheless inherit both burdens: representativeness must still be justified (Egbert et al. 2022), and the quantitative arm can drift into statistical reporting at the expense of linguistic interpretation, a field-wide trend documented in a ten-year review of journal practice by Larsson et al. (2022). Corpus-driven approaches offer reproducibility, genuine exploratory power and cumulative comparability, the additive MDA tradition allowing new registers to be situated against established dimensions (Biber 1988; Berber Sardinha and Veirano Pinto 2019), but their claim to theoretical neutrality is contested, since the choice of features, extraction and rotation methods is itself theory-laden, and statistically derived dimensions require post hoc functional interpretation (Diwersy et al. 2014; Egbert et al. 2020). Corpus-induced approaches, finally, are practically indispensable for harvesting and filtering web data in bulk, yet their documented weaknesses are substantial: expert agreement on web genre categories is low (Sharoff et al. 2010), and even state-of-the-art automatic register identification remains well below ceiling. More fundamentally, bag-of-words and composite machine-derived measures are “linguistically opaque”, severing the link between quantitative output and interpretable linguistic structure (Egbert et al. 2020; Larsson and Biber 2024). From a systemic-functional perspective, Martin (2017) argues that discourse-semantic meaning is relational, a matter of what a choice means against the alternatives it excludes, rather than countable; atomised quantitative analysis therefore risks what I would characterise as a flattening of the social meaning of texts (cf. Martin and White 2005; Martin 2017). Sharoff’s (2018) argument that genre varies independently of topic reinforces the point: outputs of topic-oriented automatic methods cannot stand in for register analysis.

4.4 The framework as organising device

The four variants should not be considered as a ranking, but rather a mapping of methodological affordances, and the remainder of this chapter uses them as an organising device rather than a taxonomy to be policed. Section 4 applies the continuum to genre and register reseach on CMC and ONC, from qualitative genre analysis through MDA to automatic classification; Section 5 applies the same lens to research on stance and evaluation, from manual Appraisal analysis through stancetaking studies and corpus-driven work on evaluation, metadiscurse and lexical bundles to induced sentiment analysis.

5 Genre and register analysis of CMC and ONC

The preceding sections established the conceptual apparatus: Section 2 distinguished genre (the staged configuration of a complete text) from register (the pervasive lexico-grammatical character of a situationally defined text variety), and Section 3 introduced Lee’s (2008) four corpus approaches together with the contrastive logic of register analysis and the criterion of representativeness (Egbert et al. 2022). This section applies that framework to the literature on the genre and register of computer-mediated communication (CMC) in general and online news comments (ONC) in particular. Two caveats apply: the four-way classification is an analytical grid imposed retrospectively, since few studies label themselves in Lee’s terms; and the field’s centre of gravity has shifted from qualitative genre description towards the quantitative, multi-dimensional description of register variation. 5.1 Corpus-informed approaches: qualitative genre analysis of news texts and comment spaces

In the corpus-informed approach, the corpus is a collection of complete, structured texts from which the analyst selects examples for qualitative interpretation; annotation is manual with theoretically motivated categories, the method lending itself to genre analysis or ethnographic description rather than aggregate counting (Lee 2008). The canonical application of this approach in ONC is Bruce’s (2010) analysis of what he terms the “participatory news article”: the composite digital text formed by an online news article together with its appended reader comments. Bruce argues that the comment space changes the communicative purpose and social structure of the host genre, producing a genuinely hybrid genre rather than a conventional article with an appended paratext. The study locates the distinctiveness of ONC at the level of the complete text (the stageing and social relations of the article-comment ensemble), precisely the analytical level that the register-based methods of Section 4.3 do not capture. Genre-oriented work in this tradition also situates ONC genealogically, treating reader comments as the digital successor of the letters-to-the-editor page: the comparisons touched upon in Section 1.5 (Landert and Jucker 2011; McCluskey and Hmielowski 2012) show that moving reader response online reconfigures the public-private boundary of audience participation, yielding comments that are more personal, more dialogic, less gatekep and more extreme than print letters. The changed participation regime is visible in the pragmatics of the texts themselves: Neurauter-Kessels (2011) documents a systematic repertoire of im/polite, face-threatening moves in reader responses on British online news sites, a profile the editorially filtered letter genre does not permit to surface. The strengths of the corpus-informed approach are interpretive depth and fidelity to text structure: because texts remain whole, genre-level claims about moves, staging and hybridity can be grounded in co-text. Its limitations are the mirror image: Bruce’s (2010) account rests on a small, purposively selected set of articles and comment sections, offering limited domain representativeness (Egbert et al. 2022), and no guarantee that the genre structure identified generalises across outlets, topics, moderation regimes or languages. Corpus-informed genre analysis therefore functions best as hypothesis-generating groundwork whose claims require testing on larger and more representative samples, the role taken up by the corpus-supported tradition.

5.2 Corpus-supported approaches: purpose-built annotated comment corpora

Corpus-supported research combines manual, theory-driven annotation with quantification, testing hypotheses formulated in advance (Lee 2008); in ONC research it is dominated by projects that build annotated comment corpora as shared research resources. An early example is the SENSEI annotated corpus (Barker et al. 2016): 1,845 reader comments on 18 Guardian articles, annotated through a four-stage manual procedure in which annotators labelled each comment’s main points, grouped comments by theme and stance, wrote unconstrained and contrained summaries of each conversation, and linked summary sentences back to supporting comment groups. Designed as a gold standard for automatic summarisation of multi-party argumentative conversations (Barker and Gaizauskas 2016), its layered annotation scheme embodies the corpus-supported logic: interpretive categories are imposed by trained humans, but the resulting annotations are countable and machine-readable. A similar design at a larger scale is the Yahoo News Annotated Comments Corpus (Napoles et al. 2017): 522,000 comments from 140,000 threads, with a subset of 9,200 comments and 2,400 threads manually annotated for comment-level properties (sentiment, persuasiveness, tone, agreement, sarcasm) and thread-level conversation quality, operationalised as “ERICs” (engaging, respectful and/or informative conversations). The most fully documented corpus of this kind is the SFU Opinion and Comments Corpus [SOCC; Kolhatkar et al. (2020)], pairing 10,339 opinion articles, editorials and op-eds from The Globe and Mail (2012-2016) with all 663,173 comments posted in response. The SOCC programme proceeded incrementally: Kolhatkar and Taboada (2017a) piloted a four-point constructiveness annotation scheme on a Globe and Mail sample, while Kolhatkar and Taboada (2017b) used the New York Times “Picks” as a proxy for annotation of constructiveness; the definitive corpus description reports reliability-tested annotation of constructiveness and toxicity followed by supervised classification experiments. On the wider CMC stage, Burns et al. (2014) offer a comparable design outside the news domain, using Apppraisal categories to retrieve emotion and stance from comments on Samsung’s and Apple’s Facebook pages. The corpus-supported tradition has delivered ONC research’s most reusable resources, and its synergy of human interpretation and quantification (Baker et al. 2008) addresses the cherry-picking critique levelled at purely qualitative work. Its constraints are equally clear: manual annotation is expensive, so annotated subsets are small relative to their source populations (9,200 of 522,000 comments in the Yahoo case), and annotation schemes import their designers’ theoretical commitments: constructiveness, for instance, is an editorially inflected construct rather than a neutral descriptive category. Both the sampling ratios and the scheme-dependence of these corpora bear directly on the representativeness framework of Section 3 (Egbert et al. 2022).

5.3 Corpus-driven approaches: multidimensional and register analyses

Corpus-driven research reverses the direction of inference: features are tagged automatically, atomised at the word or phrase level, aggregated, and interpreted only after quantification (Lee 2008). The dominant corpus-driven method in register research is multi-dimensional analysis (MDA), the factor-analytic technique originating with Biber (1988), in which patterns of co-occurrence among lexico-grammatical features are identified through factor analysis and interpreted as dimensions of variation along which registers are positioned. The following subsections trace the method’s migration from the early speech-writing question about CMC through web registers generally to ONC, concluding with the choice between full and additive MDA.

5.3.1 The early spoken-written cline revisited

The first generation of corpus-based CMC research asked a decptively simple question: does electronic language resemble speech or writing? Ferrara et al. (1991) characterised synchronous typed dialogue as “interactive written discourse”, an emergent hybrid register; Yates (1996) gave the claim corpus-linguistic substance, finding that Open University computer conferencing and email patterned with the written LOB corpus on textual measures (vocabulary range, lexical density) but with the spoken London-Lund corpus on interpersonal measures (first-person pronouns, modal verbs); and Collot and Belmore (1996) imported Biber’s (1988) multidimensional framework, finding public bulletin-board messages “replete with indicators of involvement” (Collot and Belmore 1996, 22), closest overall to “public interviews and letters, personal as well as professional” [p. 21]. Baron (1998) drew these findings together in the widely cited characterisation of email as a hybrid, or ‘creole’, of speech and writing. This early work established the expectation that comment language would be “conversation-like”, later tested and largely overturned (Section 4.3.3), and demonstrated that the cline question is ill-posed without multiple independent dimensions, the lesson that propelled MDA into CMC research.

5.3.2 MDA of CMC registers

Subsequent work extended MDA across the ecology of online registers. Jonsson (2015) applied Biber’s (1988) dimensions additively to Internet Relay Chat and ICQ “supersynchronous” chat, showing that chat is speech-like across all five dimensions despite being written. Corpus-driven MDA of web registers proper began with Biber and Kurjian (2007) and Grieve et al. (2010), the latter demonstrating that blog registers are internally heterogeneous. Titak and Roberson (2013) conducted the first exploratory MDA to include reader comments (from YouTube, ESPN, and Yahoo!): their four dimensions positioned comments as “involved, personal and opinonated” (Titak and Roberson 2013, 255), nearer to email and blogs than to informational registers. Berber Sardinha’s paired studies supply the methodological poles of the tradition: an additive analysis scoring Internet registers on Biber’s (1988) dimensions (Berber Sardinha 2014), and a full MDA extracting new dimensions from the same corpus (Berber Sardinha 2018), which again yielded an involved-versus-informational dimension, but also two stance-based dimensions specific to social media, interactional evidentiality and interactional affect. At the largest scale, Biber and Egbert (2016) extracted dimensions of variation across the Corpus of Online Registers of English (CORE), a programme consolidated in their book-length treatment (Biber and Egbert 2018), which emphasises the pervasiveness of hybrid, hard-to-classify online documents. Short-text adaptations followed: Clarke and Grieve (2017, 2019) identified four functional styles on the Donald Trump Twitter account, while Friginal et al. (2018) used an additive design on Titak and Roberson’s (2013) dimensions to contrast Facebook and Twitter posts. Together, these studies established the multi-dimensional profile of online language and showed that stance-related dimensions are central to social-media registers, a finding that returns with particular force for ONC.

5.3.3 MDA and register analysis of ONC specifically

The first MDA studies dedicated to ONC are those of Ehret and Taboada. In the 2020 study, SOCC comments and their host opinion articles were compared with the spoken and written registers of the Canadian component of the International Corpus of English (ICE-Canada), using the Multidimensional Analysis Tagger (Nini 2019) and a full factor analysis. The result contradicts the expectation, inherited from early CMC research, that comments would be conversation-like: online news comments pattern closer to opinion articles and even examination scripts, as a written register whose language is informal yet argumentative and evaluative (Ehret and Taboada 2020). The 2021 study widened the comparison space to seventeen online registers in CORE, yielding three dimensions (involved-evaluative, informational-argumentative, and narrative descrition versus instructional) on which comments emerge as simultaneously involved-evaluative and informational but predominantly argumentative: informational-argumentative discourse with an involved-evaluative slant (Ehret and Taboada 2021). Gerhardt (2021) provides the complementary additive analysis, scoring the comments sections of vegan food blogs on Biber’s (1988) dimensions and concluding that these comments constitute a written genre that is involved, situation-dependent and non-abstract: dialogic and expressive, yet medially written and public. The convergence of the two studies on the written status of comments, reached by opposite methodological routes (full versus additive MDA) and on different comment ecologies (news versus blogs), strengthens the register-level claim, while their differences in involvement caution against treating ONC as a single uniform register: the MDA evidence shows systematic variation within the register across comment ecologies, not a family of established sub-registers (cf. Biber et al. 2020). Three further corpus-driven strands complement the MDA work. The first, Marchand (2013), is my own earlier study and a point of continuity with the present study: its BBC Have Your Say corpus (HYSOC), approximately 1.5 million words of messages, is a precursor of the reference corpus used here, and the study amounts to a pilot lexical-bundle analysis of the same commenting ecology. Comparing the extracted bundles with those of spoken and written reference corpora, I found the comments highly formulaic and distinct as a register from both spontaneous speech and edited writing; because lexical bundles in comment data are heavily implicated in stance expression, I return to this strand in Section 5. Collins and Nerlich (2015) used Wmatrix and its USAS semantic tagger to identify key semantic domains in Guardian climate-change comments, a design Collins (2019) extends in a book-length treatment of online communication corpora, including comments on antimicrobial resistance. In a related critical discourse study, Baker and McGlashan (2020) compare keywords and discourse prosody in Daily Express articles about Romanians with readers’ comments on the same articles. These keyword and key semantic domain approaches characterise what comments are about and how they evaluate, forming a second bridge to Section 5.

5.3.4 Full versus additive MDA

The choice between the two MDA variants mentioned above deserves some extra attention. In a full MDA, a new factor analysis extracts dimensions from the target corpus itself; in an additive MDA, new registers are scored on the dimensions of a validated base study without conducting a new factor analysis (Berber Sardinha et al. 2019). Full MDA is preferable when the target register is novel and its co-occurrence structure is precisely what is in question, as for ONC, where Ehret and Taboada (2020), (2021) could not assume that Biber’s 1988 dimensions, derived from pre-Internet registers, would capture the variation internal to online comments. Additive MDA is more economical and yields scores directly comparable with the base study’s register space, the property that Gerhardt (2021) made use of for blog comments, but it inherits the base study’s feature set, tagging assumptions and dimension structure, risking misfit when the new register differs systematically from those on which the base model was built (Berber Sardinha et al. 2019). A further consideration is common to both variants: register features become visible only through contrast, so what counts as a characteristic of ONC depends on the comparison corpus (ICE-Canada highlights comments’ distance from conversation, CORE their position among online registers), and the composition and representativeness of the reference data (Egbert et al. 2022) are as consequential as the target corpus itself.

5.4 Corpus-induced approaches: automatic genre and register classification

At the corpus-induced end of Lee’s (2008) continuum, annotation and classification are fully automatic and the goals are practical: above all, automatic genre identification (AGI) on the web. Santini (2007) established the foundational problem: unlike the relatively stable genres of print, web pages exhibit genre hybridism and individualisation, so that a single document may belong to several genres or to none. Mehler, Sharoff and Santini’s (2010) edited volume consolidated this research programme, while Sharoff et al. (2010) delivered its most sobering result: even genre experts agree poorly when classifying randomly extracted web texts, undermining the validity of the categories classifiers are trained to predict. Against this background, Egbert et al. (2015) developed CORE through a bottom-up, user-based procedure in which non-expert raters categorised documents from near-random web searches into a hierarchical register scheme, with documented reliability, a remedy for the single-expert annotation problem. Sharoff (2018) proposed Functional Text Dimensions, defining genre and register categories functionally and independently of topic, a useful precaution for ONC research, where topic variation across news stories must not be mistaken for register variation. Most recently, Laippala et al. (2022) report register identification across the unrestricted open web using CORE-derived training data, with state-of-the-art neural models reaching an F1 score of roughly 68 per cent (F1 being the harmonic mean of precision and recall), a respectable result for practical applications but still far from the ideal. The corpus-induced tradition is practically indispensable for ONC research, since harvesting comment data in bulk requires exactly these classifiers; its limitations are equally relevant: category validity and inter-rater reliability remain weak (Sharoff et al. 2010), the representativeness of web-derived training corpora is uncertain (Egbert et al. 2015), and the features used are linguistically opaque, so that classification accuracy buys little linguistic explanation, an instance of the broader displacement of linguistic description by statistical reporting in corpus work (Egbert et al. 2020; Larsson et al. 2022).

5.5 Section conclusion: register and genre revisited

Read together, the four tradtions yield complementary levels of description. At the genre level, the corpus-informed evidence indicates that comments participate in a larger, hybrid textual unit, Bruce’s (2010) participatory news article, whose staged structure and social relations a feature-based register analysis alone cannot capture; register analysis, conversely, describes the pervasive linguistic texture of comments, and the corpus-supported annotation programmes (Barker et al. 2016; Kolhatkar et al. 2020) supply the bridge between the two levels. One finding cuts across all four approaches and organises what follows: evaluation is a particularly salient characteristic of ONC, central to what commenting is for, a conclusion drawn independently from multidimensional analysis (Ehret and Taboada 2020) and from manual annotation showing that the large majority of annotated Appraisal spans in news comments are negative (Cavasso and Taboada 2021). What this evaluative character implies for the emerging profile of ONC, and how stance and evaluation in comments have been studied, is taken up in Section 5, organised by the same corpus approaches, and the fuller register characterisation is drawn together in Sections 5.6 and 6.1.

6 Stance and evaluation in CMC and ONC

Section 4 concluded that online news comments (ONC) can be characterised as an emerging, predominantly evaluative written register. The present section examines how the literature has theorised and operationalised this evaluative character, organised by Lee’s (2008) four variants of corpus-based methodology rather than chronologically, because each variant brings a distinct conception of what stance and evaluation are, and how they can be evidenced.

6.1 Stance, subjectivity, intersubjectivity, evaluation, appraisal: a terminological map

The literature on the expression of opinion and attitude is notorious for overlapping terminology, and a brief orientation is necessary before the approaches can be compared. Stance is used in the broadly Biberian sense of the linguistic marking of a speaker’s or writer’s “attitudes, feelings, judgements, or commitment concerning the message” (Biber and Finegan 1988, 1; cf. Gray and Biber 2012). Subjectivity concerns the imprint of the speaking self on utterance, while intersubjectivity captures the orientation of utterances towards other subjects whose alignment is solicited or resisted (Du Bois 2007; Du Bois and Kärkkäinen 2012). Evaluation is the broadest cover term: the expression of speaker or writer opinion about the entities and propositions talked about (Thompson and Hunston 2000; Hunston 2011), developed corpus-linguistically in news discourse by Bednarek (2006). Appraisal, by contrast, names a specific systematised taxonomy of evaluative resources developed within systemic functional linguistics (SFL) (Martin and White 2005), while metadiscourse (Hyland 2005a) and sentiment (Pang and Lee 2008) label, respectively, an interactional and a computational reconceptualisation of overlapping phenomena. These terms are not interchangeable: each embeds a different assumption about whether evaluation is a discourse-semantic system, an intersubjective act located in sequences, a phraseological pattern to be counted, or a polarity score to be computed (Table 5).

6.2 Corpus-informed approaches: the Appraisal framework

The Appraisal framework originates in the SFL tradition discussed in Section 2, developed predominantly within the Sydney School by Jim Martin, Peter White and colleagues (Martin 2000; White 2003; Martin and White 2005). Appraisal encompasses the linguistic resources through which emotions, judgements and aesthetic valuations are negotiatedd, amplified and related to alternative voices in the discourse (Martin 2000), modelled as a discourse-semantic system network comprising three primary sub-systems: Attitude, Engagement and Graduation (Martin and White 2005). Attitude is further differentiated into three subtypes: Affect covers emotional reactions (happiness, sadness, fear); Judgement, morally inflected assessments of human character and behaviour (ethical, deceptive, brave); and Appreciation, the valuation of entities, processes and natural phenomena (remarkable, elegant), as in the following attested examples from the corpora used in this study (see Chapter 4):

I am sad to hear the news of this flood. [JOC_1707 - Affect]

I think he is a very brave man. [JOC_0849 - Judgement]

Japanese fall is very beautiful. [JOC_2802 - Appreciation]

Brown did well to resist the temptation of popularity and only blow half of his $ budget surplus. [HOC_LF - Judgement/Appreciation]

Attempting to make these categories machine-tractable, Taboada and Grieve (2004) identified typical sentence structures associated with each Attitude subtype: Affect prototypically realised by patterns such as “I was sad”, Judgement by “He was brave”, Appreciation by “It was beautiful”. Their semi-automatic classification of adjectives from review corpora is significant less for its accuracy than for demonstrating that Appraisal categories are lexico-grammatically patterned enough to be operationalised, an insight taken up in Sections 5.4 and 5.5. A further distinction separates inscribed (explicit) evaluation, as in “a bright kid”, from invoked evaluation, where judgement is invited without being directly stated, as in “a kid who reads a lot” (Martin and White 2005). The Engagement system, inspired by Bakhtin’s account of dialogism and heteroglossia (Bakhtin, 1981, as cited in Martin and White 2005), distinguishes monoglossic utterances, which present propositions as bare fact, from heteroglossic utterances, which acknowledge or engage alternative viewpoints; heteroglossic resources are divided into those that expand the dialogic space (entertain, attribute) and those that contract it (disclaim, proclaim) (White 2003; Martin and White 2005). White’s (2003) analysis of “reporter”, “correspondent” and “commentator” voices in news discourse shows how journalistic genres calibrate the range of admissible evaluative positions, a calibration against which the freer evaluative regime of reader comments can be measured. Finally, the Graduation system modulates the strength and precision of evaluations: Force scales the intensity of gradable meanings (“very interesting” versus “a little sad”), while Focus sharpens or softens prototypicality (“a true friend” versus “a kind of friend”) (Martin and White 2005). Appraisal has been applied across genres including news discourse (White 1998; Coffin and O’Halloran 2006), children’s narratives (Martin 1996; Painter 2003), online discussions of literary texts (Love 2006) and everyday conversation (Eggins and Slade 1997). Within CMC research, the most influential corpus-informed extension is Zappavigna’s (2012) analysis of a 100-million-word Twitter corpus, coupling Appraisal with the concept of “ambient affiliation”: users align around shared evaluative bonds without directly addressing one another, a configuration closely resembling the loosely threaded interaction of comment sections, where commenters respond to an article and an imagined audience rather than a single interlocutor. The strengths of corpus-informed Appraisal work are delicacy and attention to co-text; its limitations are the mirror image; manual annotation is labour-intensive, results hard to generalise, and reliability uneven: in the ONC study discussed below, all categories except Graduation reached acceptable inter-annotator agreement (Cavasso and Taboada 2021).

6.3 Corpus-supported approaches: Appraisal with quantification and stancetaking

Corpus-supported work retains the theoretically motivated annotation schemes of the corpus-informed tradition, but subjects the annotated categories to systematic quantification, trading some delicacy for testability. A key study for the present thesis is Cavasso and Taboada (2021), who manually annotated 1,043 comments on opinion pieces in The Globe and Mail, a subcorpus of the SFU Opinion and Comments Corpus [SOCC; Kolhatkar et al. (2020)], using the full Appraisal system, with two annotators, a curator and two reliability studies. Their principal finding is clear: “The defining characteristic of the register of online news comments is their evaluative nature” (Cavasso and Taboada 2021, 1). Quantitatively, 73.5% of all Appraisal spans were negative, and 79% of comments were mostly or entirely negative; commenters also favoured Judgement and Appreciation over Affect, which accounted for only 3.4% of spans. Rather than I hate the candidate, commenters write The candidate is incompetent or The candidate’s policies are bad: highly opinionated content packaged as objective assessment rather than emotional outburst (Cavasso and Taboada 2021). This preference for apparently depersonalised evaluation is a register-level pattern that qualitative reading alone could not establish; the authors further show that evaluative patterns interact with constructiveness and toxicity, with implications for automatic moderation. Trnavac and Taboada (2023) extend the analysis cross-linguistically, annotating comparable samples of Globe and Mail (English) and RT (Russian) comments for the the Engagement system: constructive comments were more explicitly subjective, English ones foregrounding the reliability of knowledge claims, and Russian ones relying more on necessity modality. Appraisal has also served as an annotation scheme elsewhere in CMC: Burns et al. (2014) use Appraisal categories to retrieve emotion and stance from posts and comments on Samsung’s and Apple’s Facebook pages, an early demonstration, albeit in a commercial rather than news context, that Appraisal can drive quantitative comparison of online communities. A parallel corpus-supported tradition approaches the same phenomena not through a taxonomy of evaluative meanings, but through the pragmatics of the stance itself. Du Bois (2007) defines stance as “a public act by a social actor, achieved dialogically through overt communicative means, of simultaneously evaluating objects, positioning subjects (self and others), and aligning with other subjects, with respect to any salient dimension of the sociocultural field” (p. 163). The resulting “stance triangle” models every stance as as three simultaneous sub-acts: a first subject evaluates a shared stance object, therby positioning him- or herself, and in doing so aligns (or disaligns) with a second subject oriented to the same object. Because each stance act invites a responsive act of alignment, stance is inherently intersubjective and sequential rather than a private psychological state, a point reinforced by findings that even routine epistemic markers such as I think function interactionally (Kärkkäinen 2003, 2006; Englebretson 2007) and that stancetaking enregisters social identities and dialects (Johnstone 2007). The triangle maps closely onto the comment-thread situation, in which a commenter evaluates the news article, positions themselves, and invites alignment from subsequent commenters. Research on digital discourse confirms the model’s transferability: Giaxoglou and Johansson (2020) show that networked practices of epistemic, affective and narrative stancetaking shape participation in social media responses to events unfolding in the media, and Johansson (2017) documents the everyday opinion work of news discussion forums as public vernacular discourse. Stancetaking research thus supplies what taxonomic Appraisal work lacks, an account of why evaluation in ONC is fundamentally other-directed, while remaining largely qualitative in its evidential base.

6.4 Corpus-driven approaches: evaluation, semantic prosody, metadiscourse, grammatical stance, lexical bundles

Corpus-driven approaches invert the logic of the previous two traditions: rather than applying a prior taxonomy, they identify evaluative meaning from recurrent lexico-grammatical patterning, on the basis that such meaning is often inaccessible to intuition and becomes visible only through repeated occurrence across a corpus. Louw (1993) popularised the term semantic prosody, which he attributes to Sinclair, for the “consistent aura of meaning” that items acquire through their typical collocates, arguing that insincerity and irony can be diagnosed where prosodies clash; Hunston (2007) shows that semantic prosodies are pervasive, frequently attitudinal, and central to how evaluation is signalled obliquely. Hunston’s (2011) monograph generalises the point into a programme: evaluation is fundamentally phraseological, residing in recurrent patterns rather than isolated words, and is best studied through grammar patterns and local grammars of evaluation (Hunston and Sinclair 2000), which parse evaluative utterances into functional elements such as the thing evaluated and the evaluative category. The single most consequential corpus-driven study for present purposes is Millar and Hunston (2015), whose data, free-text comments submitted to the website RateMyProfessors.com, are structurally homologous to ONC: short, unsolicited, evaluative user contributions responding to a shared object. Starting not from a taxonomy but from the adjectives commenters use to describe people, clustered with Principal Components Analysis, they derive seven dimensions of evaluative meaning along which professors are assessed, and three distinct evaluative “voices” the community deploys (the “novice intellectual”, the “consumer”, the “subordinate”). Three implications follow: adjectives emerge as the lexico-grammatical locus at which evaluative meaning concentrates in comment-style data; evaluative taxonomies are demonstrably community-specific, so categories derived from one commenting community cannot be assumed for another; and bottom-up analysis of corpus data can effectively precede, and problematise, top-down category systems such as Appraisal. The study thus provides both an empirical bridge from generic evaluation research to online comment data, and a methodological rationale for deriving ONC-specific evaluative categories from the data. Hyland’s (2005a) model of metadiscourse likewise distinguishes interactive resources, which guide the reader through the text, from interactional resources, namely stance (hedges, boosters, attitude markers, self-mention) and engagement (reader pronouns, directives, questions), through which writers negotiate with imagined readers (see also Hyland 2005b; Hyland and Tse 2004). Although developed primarily for academic writing, the model captures a defining feature of ONC that the Appraisal approach underplays: commenters habitually address the article’s author, other commenters and a wider imagined readership, and the markers of that address are themselves recurrent, countable items. The most thoroughly quantified strand of corpus-driven stance research, however, is the grammatical-stance tradition associated with Biber and colleagues. Chapter 12 of the Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English (Biber et al. 1999) provides the standard inventory of grammatical stance devices: stance adverbials (marking certainty, likelihood, attitude, or style); complement-clause constructions whose controlling element is a verb (including mental verbs such as think and suasive verbs such as demand, suggest and insist), and adjective (it is clear that…) or a noun; and modal and semi-modal verbs. Gray and Biber (2012) review and delimit these conceptions, and Biber (2006) demonstrates, in a multi-register study of university language, that such stance markers cluster densely in particular registers and tend to occur not as isolated items, but as parts of recurrent multi-word combinations. That last observation connects grammatical stance to the lexical bundle programme. Biber et al. (2004) extracted recurrent four-word sequences from university teaching and textbooks, and proposed the now-standard taxonomy distinguishing stance bundles (epistemic; attitudinal/modal: desire, obligation/directive, intention/prediction, ability) from discourse organisers and referential expressions; subsequent work showed that bundles are structurally incomplete, functionally specialised and register-differentiated, with stance bundles predominating in conversation-like registers (Biber and Barbieri 2007), and that bundle inventories are sensitive to the discourse community, so that register-specific inventories must be derived empirically rather than imported (Hyland 2008). Crucially for CMC, Barbieri (2018) extracted bundles from a 2.2-million-word corpus of American blogs, and found that roughly half are stance bundles, typically verb-phrase-based and encapsulating first-person reference (I don’t want to, don’t get me wrong), a window onto the subjectivity and intersubjectivity of blog writing. At the level of ONC specifically, Marchand’s (2013) Have Your Say bundle analysis (Section 4.3.3) found comment language highly formulaic, patterning as a register distinct from both spontaneous speech and edited writing, with stance bundles accounting for [PROPORTION] of bundle types, led by [TWO OR THREE EXAMPLES]. Beyond contiguous n-grams, the concgram approach implemented in ConcGram retrieves non-contiguous, variably ordered lexical co-occurrences exhaustively (Greaves 2009; Greaves and Warren 2010). Taken together, these studies establish that stance is phraseologically patterned, register and community specific, and that blog and comment data in particular are saturated with reccurent stance formulae.

6.5 Corpus-induced approaches: sentiment analysis of comments

At the corpus-induced end of the continuum, evaluation is operationalised for practical ends (classification, ranking, moderation) with minimal researcher interpretation. Sentiment analysis (or opinion mining) is comprehensively surveyed by Pang and Lee (2008) and Liu (2012), who document its core tasks: polarity classification, subjectivity detection, and aspect-based opinion extraction. Methodologically, two families are relevant here. Lexicon-based methods aggregate the prior sentiment values of words, adjusted by valence shifters such as negation (not good), intensification (very good) and irrealis markers (would have been good); the SO-CAL system of Taboada et al. (2011), a direct computational descendant of Taboada and Grieve’s (2004) automation of Appraisal, is a prominent example. Its authors argue that such lexicon-based methods are more robust across domains than supervised classifiers, which tend to underperform on genres other than those on which they were trained. Related resources include the NRC word-emotion association lexicon, crowdsourced by Mohammad and Turney (2013), which extends binary polarity into eight emotion categories. SentiStrength (Thelwall et al. 2010), designed for short informal web text, assigns separate positive and negative strength scores to comments, tweets and similar data. Sentiment and quality analysis of news comments specifically has become a substantial subfield, built on the YNACC and SOCC corpora described in Section 4.2 [see also Table 4]. Napoles et al. (2017) used YNACC’s manual annotations of sentiment, persuasiveness, tone, agreement, sarcasm and conversation quality as the basis for automatically identifying “good conversations”, demonstrating that comment quality is sufficiently learnable to support ranking and moderation applications. Work on SOCC pursued the complementary goals of constructiveness and toxicity classification: Kolhatkar and Taboada (2017a) piloted the annotation and supervised classification of constructive language in Globe and Mail comments, and Kolhatkar et al. (2020) report classification experiments over the full corpus’s constructiveness and toxicity annotations. Table 6 summarises these corpus-induced comment studies.

These approaches achieve the scale that corpus-informed and corpus-supported work cannot, and have made comment data a standard benchmark in computational linguistics. However, as argued in Section 3, their gains come at the cost of linguistic granularity: texts are reduced to feature aggregates whose scores are “linguistically opaque”, quantitatively tractable but uninformative about the specific lexico-grammatical resources through which evaluation is performed (Larsson et al. 2022). A polarity score can establish that a comment is negative; it cannot show how negativity is constructed, hedged, intensified or aligned with, the questions to which the present thesis is directed.

6.6 Section conclusion

Read together, the four traditions converge on a single characterisation of ONC. Corpus-informed and corpus-supported Appraisal studies show that evaluation is a particularly salient characteristic of the register, central to what commenting is for, and that commenters prefer apparently objective Judgement and Appreciation over emotive Affect (Cavasso and Taboada 2021; Trnavac and Taboada 2023). Stancetaking research explains why: a comment is a public act of evaluating a shared object that positions its author and solicits alignment from others, an intersubjective logic that transfers directly to asynchronous written CMC (Du Bois 2007; Giaxoglou and Johansson 2020). Corpus-driven work adds that such evaluative positioning is phraseologically patterned and community-specific: evaluative meaning resides in recurrent word sequences (Hunston 2011), and adjective-centred evaluative taxonomies differ between commenting communities (Millar and Hunston 2015; Barbieri 2018). Even corpus-induced sentiment research, for all its loss of delicacy, presupposes that evaluation permeates the register. What none of these traditions has provided is a register-specific inventory of the recurrent lexico-grammatical formulae through which stance is taken in ONC. These findings converge on the present study’s central construct through a chain of four steps. First, ONC are evaluative: evaluation is central to the communicative function of commenting. Second, evalution is realised linguistically through the resources of stance, the linguistic marking of attitudes, judgements and commitment (Biber and Finegan 1988; Gray and Biber 2012). Third, at least some of these stance resources take the form of recurrent phraseological patterns rather than isolated items (Biber 2006; Hunston 2011). Fourth, such patterns can be extracted automatically and inventoried for a register. Stance-related phraseological patterns therefore operationalise one important linguistic realisation of ONC’s evaluative character: they are recurrent sequences defined over tagged stance features, such as evaluative adjectives, stance adverbs, private and suasive verbs, and modal verbs, whose attested lexical realisations are the bundles analysed in later chapters. The choice of these feature categories is motivated by the grammatical-stance framework of Biber et al. (1999), which establishes them as core grammatical categories of stance marking: adjectives are the primary evaluative word class (Millar and Hunston 2015), adverbs function as stance adverbials, private and suasive verbs control stance complement clauses, and modal verbs mark epistemic and deontic stance. Complement-clause constructions and stance adverbials thus supply the grammatical framework within which the features are interpreted, while the patterns themselves are assembled from the tagged adjectives, adverbs, verbs and modals. The method therefore inherits the grammatical-stance grounding of the Longman Grammar framework (Biber et al. 1999), while remaining extractable through automatic tagging. Chapter 4 develops this operationalisation for the ONC corpora of this study.

7 Conclusion: toward the present study

7.1 The argument of the chapter

Taken together, the literature reviewed in this chapter establishes three propositions about online news comments (ONC). The first is situational: ONC constitute a coherent discoursal configuration within computer-mediated communication (CMC), namely asynchronous, persistent, pseudonymous, one-to-many messages appended to professionally produced news texts (Herring 2007), functionally descended from the letter to the editor, but reconfigured by that migration into something more personal, more dialogic and less gatekept (Landert and Jucker 2011). The second concerns register: read through the genre-versus-register distinction of Section 2 and the corpus typology of Section 3 (Biber and Conrad 2009; Lee 2008), comments are an emerging written register rather than the typed conversation early CMC research expected, patterning closest to opinion journalism and other persuasive written registers (Ehret and Taboada 2020, 2021; cf. Marchand 2013; Gerhardt 2021), while at the genre level they participate in the larger hybrid unit of the participatory news article (Bruce 2010). The third proposition is functional: evaluation is a particularly salient characteristic of the register, central to what commenting is for. Evaluative language saturates comments, skews strongly negative, and prefers apparently objective Judgement and Appreciation over emotive Affect (Cavasso and Taboada 2021), and stancetaking theory explains why: a comment is a public act of evaluating a shared object that simultaneously positions its author and solicits alignment from co-commenters (Du Bois 2007). In short, the field informs us what ONC are, where they sit among registers, and that evaluation is their main concern.

7.2 What the literature cannot explain: the double gap

What this body of work cannot explain is how stance is performed, phraseologically, in the register. Each of Lee’s (2008) four approaches stops short of a register-specific phraseological inventory: corpus-informed genre analysis works at the level of whole texts (Bruce 2010); corpus-supported annotation quantifies evaluative meanings rather than their recurrent wording (Kolhatkar et al. 2020; Cavasso and Taboada 2021); corpus-induced sentiment methods achieve scale at the cost of linguistic interpretability; and even the corpus-driven tradition, which has shown stance to be phraseologically patterned and community-specific in adjacent comment data (Barbieri 2018; Millar and Hunston 2015), has produced no such inventory for news comments, the closest precedent being the exploratory bundle counts of Marchand (2013). The first gap, then, would appear to be that the field lacks a register-specific inventory of the recurrent lexico-grammatical formulae of stance in ONC. The second gap can also be stated provisionally for now: the literature reviewed here has not provided an account of how language learners deploy the register’s characteristic stance resources; whether and to what extent learner corpus research has addressed the corresponding learner dimension will be taken up in Chapter 3. The double gap matters because register claims are only as strong as their comparisons: register features become visible only through contrast, and the evidentiary value of any corpus claim depends on demonstrable representativeness (Biber and Conrad 2009; Egbert et al. 2022). I therefore use an early-2000s BBC Have Your Say corpus as a register benchmark rather than a strictly matched comparison corpus: it captures the first generation of moderated, public-service, below-the-line commenting (Harrison 2010; Herring 2004b), so that learner comments written more than a decade later can be located against an established native-speaker comment register with genre held broadly constant (Herring and Paolillo 2006), and I treat the residual differences of writer population and period between the corpora as objects of interpretation rather than explaining them away.

7.3 The design of the present study

The design developed in Chapter 4 follows directly from this argument, combining the two traditions best placed to close the first gap. Register description proceeds by additive multi-dimensional analysis, scoring the learner and reference comments on Biber’s (1988) dimensions: the additive variant is economical and yields scores directly comparable with the base study, the property that Gerhardt (2021) made use of for blog comments, while its known risk of dimensional misfit is mitigated by the convergence of Ehret and Taboada’s (2021) full MDA results on the written, argumentative-evaluative profile. Stance analysis proceeds through stance-related phraseological patterns: recurrent sequences defined over tagged stance features, such as evaluative adjectives, stance adverbs, private and suasive verbs, and modal verbs, together with their lexical realisations as bundles. As Section 5.6 detailed, these are core grammatical categories of stance marking established by Biber et al. (1999): adjectives are the primary evaluative word class (Millar and Hunston 2015), adverbs function as stance adverbials, private and suasive verbs control stance complement clauses, and modal verbs mark epistemic and deontic stance; the stance-adverbial and complement-clause framework supplies the grammatical grounding (Biber 2006), comment-style data confirm these categories as the locus of evaluative meaning (Barbieri 2018), and the method remains extractable through automatic tagging. This second strand deliberately bridges the corpus-driven and corpus-supported approaches: extraction is inductive and automatic, but the patterns are classified against established functional categories and interpreted in co-text, retaining the interpretability that purely induced measures sacrifice. Chapter 3 first takes up the provisional learner gap through a second literature review, of learner corpus research (LCR), situating the L2 English writing of Japanese learners in particular. Chapter 4 then turns to the data itself, and to the construction of the corpora, the tagging procedure and the modelling strategy.
