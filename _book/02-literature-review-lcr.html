3  The learner dimension of the ONC problem

3.1 From Chapter 2 to the learner corpus

As outlined in Chapter 2, research on computer-mediated communication (CMC) and online discourse provides the basis, through register theory, for treating online news commenting as a distinct communicative environment. The central conclusion of that chapter was that online news comments (ONC) would appear to constitute a distinctive evaluative written register: publice, asynchronous texts in which lay readers react to news events, characterised by involved, stance-heavy features that set them apart from both the news reports they respond to, and from casual conversation (Biber and Conrad 2009; Biber and Egbert 2018; Ehret and Taboada 2021). Chapter 2 also identified the first of the two gaps motivating this thesis: although the register may be defined by evaluation, it would seem that no register-specific inventory of its recurrent stance phraseology has yet been compiled, that is, of the stance-related phraseological patterns, defined over tagged stance features such as evaluative adjectives, stance adverbs and suasive verbs, through which evaluation is conventionally expressed in comment discourse. The present chapter turns to the second dimension of the study: learner language. In reviewing Learner Corpus Research (LCR), this chapter seeks to establish how differences between learner and native-speaker writing have traditionally been investigated and, crucially, why such comparisons require attention to register. The corpus as the heart of this thesis introduces a complication that Chapter 2 left undeveloped: the data under study are learner-produced, comprising six cohorts of online news comments written by Japanese university students learning Engish as a foreign language (EFL). With learner texts, the descriptive question of Chapter 2 can no longer be separated from an interpretive one. Learner language is known to diverge systematically from native-speaker usage even when no error is present, though the quantitative overuse and underuse of perfectly well-formed items (Granger 1998b, 2003); the interpretation of any such divergence, however, depends on what is known about the learner language, about the register, and about the learner population in question.

Given this complication, my task in this chapter is to establish a framework for interpreting differences in stance phraseology between the learner comments and the reference corpus, identifying the competing explanations that must be addressed before a difference may be attributed to learner status, to first language (L1), or to register knowledge. In doing so, the chapter establishes the thesis’s second gap: research on second language (L2) stance and phraseology, including the substantial body of work on Japanese learners, has been conducted almost entirely on academic or otherwise controlled writing, and the intersection of learner language, stance, phraseology and informal online evaluation appears to remain insufficiently understood.

3.2 Why comparing learner and native-speaker writing is not straightforward

When learner language differs from native-speaker usage, the question of what is responsible is not a straightforward one. Such differences may reflect the learners’ L1, through the transfer of rhetorical or linguistic habits; their proficiency or developmental stage; the task conditions under which the texts were produced; the genre or register sampled; the topic; the period of data collection; or the learners’ educational background and prior instruction. Granger (1996) insisted from the outset of Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis (CIA) that such external variables should be controlled, so that comparisons are made “like with like”, and SOMETHING has since proved consequential: proficiency is “best described as a fuzzzy variable” in learner corpora (Carlsen 2012), task and prompt conditions measurably alter learner output (Alexopoulou et al. 2017), and register effects in learner writing would appear to rival or exceed those of writer status or L1 (Larsson et al. 2021).

The argument I develop in this chapter rests on four propositions. First, Learner Corpus Research establishes the value of systematically comparing learner and native-speaker production (Section 2): comparing large collections of authentic learner writing with native-speaker data makes visible the patterns of overuse and underuse that error analysis could not detect (Granger 1996, 2003, 2015). Secondly, such comparisons require an appropriate reference corpus and sustained attention to competing sources of variation (Sections 2.3 to 2.5): the native-speaker corpus functions as a reference population rather than a prescriptive norm, and L1 composition, proficiency and task conditions must be documented or controlled before differences are interpreted (Connor and Moreno 2005; Gilquin 2022; Granger 2015). Thirdly, register, genre and task substantially affect learner language, so comparisons of learner and native-speaker writing must take register into account (Section 3): linguistic choice varies systematically with situational context for all language users (Biber 1988; Biber and Conrad 2009), learner production would appear to be no exception, and comparisons across mismatched registers risk attributing register effects to learner status (Larsson et al. 2021). Finally, existing L2 stance and phraseology research, including research on Japanese learners, has focused overwhelmingly on academic or controlled writing (Sections 4 and 5) (Chen and Baker 2010, 2016; Hyland and Milton 1997; Ädel and Erman 2012), and the major Japanese learner corpora are likewise built on essays and elicited speech (Granger et al. 2009; Ishikawa 2013; Tono 2007). The intersection of learner language, stance, phraseology, and informal online evaluation remains correspondingly under-described.

The central claim I make in this chapter follows directly from these propositions: register is a necessary condition for interpreting differences between learner and native-speaker writing, since every alternative explanation for such differences (L1, proficiency, development, task, topic and time) operates within, and interacts with, the register in which the language was produced.

Section 6 draws these strands together, setting out the double gap that emerges when the learner-side gap established here is combined with the register gap identified in Chapter 2, and the methodological requirements that Chapter 4 must meet.

3.3 Learner Corpus Research and Comparative Logic

This thesis compares a corpus of online news comments written by Japanese learners of English as a foreign language (EFL) with a reference corpus of native-speaker comments from the BBC Have Your Say forum. Any such comparison requires a methodological framework that specifies what may legitimately be compared, against which reference data, and under what interpretive constraints. This section therefore reviews Learner Corpus Research (LCR), the branch of corpus linguistics concerned with the systematic study of learner language, and its signature comparative method, Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis (CIA). This section traces the emergence of LCR (2.1), presents the two configurations of CIA and their relation to the register-analytic framework of Chapter 2 (2.2), examines how differences between learner and native-speaker writing should be interpreted (2.3), reviews the design variables that condition comparability (2.4), and situates the present study within the development of major learner corpora, from ICLE and LOCNESS to ICNALE (2.5).

3.4 The emergence of Learner Corpus Research

Before the advent of learner corpora, empirical evidence about second language (L2) production came largely from three sources, each with recognised limitations: introspection and elicitation, which reveal what learners can do under experimental conditionsa rather than what they do naturally; error analyis (EA), which reconstructed the learner’s developing system from observable errors, revauled as evidence of transitional competence (Corder 1967); and small, hand-collected datasets whose “considerable variation in the numbers of subjects, in the backgrounds of the subjects, and in the empirical data” (Odlin, 1989, p. 151, as cited in Granger 2003, 541) made cumulative generalisation difficult.

Error analysis, hoowever, was structurally blind to quantitative divergence. Schachter (1974) demonstrated that learners may avoid structure they find difficult, so that an error count captures only what learners attempt and fail, not what they decline to attempt at all. A learner might use a linguistic feature correctly but far more often, or far less often, than a native speaker would, and this phenomenon, later termed overuse and underuse, lay outside EA’s descriptive reach (Granger 1998b, 2002).

Learner Corpus Research emerged in the early 1990s as a systematic, corpus-based answer to these data problems. A computer learner corpus is defined as “an electronic collection of authentic texts produced by foreign or second language learners”, assembled, like all corpora, “according to explicit design criteria”, but requiring “extra care” in data collection “given the large number of variables affecting the learning/acquisition process” (Granger 2003, 538; cf. Granger 2002; Nesselhauf 2004). The founding resource was the International Corpus of Learner English, first presented by Granger (1993) as a cross-l1 corpus of advanced learners’ English essays in which the learner’s first language (L1) was a central design variable (see also Granger 2024), and the edited volume Learner English on Computer (Granger 1998a) supplied early demonstrations of cross-linguistic lexical profiling and overuse/underuse analysis (Granger and Rayson 1998; Ringbom 1998; Lorenz 1999).

CIA should be distinguished from both error analysis, including its computer-aided descendant (Dagneaux et al. 1998), which remains focused on the classification of non-target-like forms, and from corpus-based seccond language acquisition (SLA) research, which uses learner corpora to test acquisition hypotheses (Myles 2015; Tracy-Ventura et al. 2021). It is a specifically comparative methodology defined by its systematic confrontation of learner data with native-speaker data, r with other learner data, and it supplies the comparative logic of the present study.

3.5 Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis

Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis was introduced by Granger (1996) as the corpus-based counterpart of Contrastive Analysis: whereas Contrastive Analysis compared different languages, CIA was designed to establish comparisons “between native and interlanguage varieties (NL vs IL) and between interlanguage varieties (IL vs IL)” (Granger 2024, 247; see also Granger 2003, 541).

The first configuration, learner versus native-speaker comparison, uncovers “the patterns of use distinguishing learner data from native data”, which “fall into two categories: qualitative differences (misuse) and quantitative differences (over- and underuse)” (Granger 2003, 541). A text may thus contain few overt errors yet still produce a distinctly non-native impression because the frequency distribution of perfectly well-formed items diverges from native norms (Granger 1998b), as early studies of connector usage (Granger and Tyson 1996) and adjective intensification (Lorenz 1999) demonstrated.

The second configuration, learner versus learner comparison, assesses the effects of the L1 (the classic transfer question),, proficiency, age, learning setting, medium and task type. Granger (1996) already underlined the importance of controlling such external variables, insisting that researchers compare “like with like” (Granger, 1996, as discussed in Granger 2002). Because IL versus IL comparisons isolate these variables, they are indispensable before any observed pattern is attributed to learnerhood in general (Granger 2002). The present study adopts the first configuration (Japanese EFL learners versus native speakers) while holding constant, as far as corpus design permits, the variables that the second configuration has shown to matter.

3.5.1 CIA and the logic of Chapter 2

The parallel between CIA and the register-analytic framework established in Chapter 2 is worth making explicit, since the thesis combines the two. Register analysis, in the tradition of Biber and Conrad (2009), compares a target register against a reference register to identify lingusitic features functionally characteristic of a situation of use; CIA compares a learner corpus against an expert or native-speaker corpus to identify the features characteristic of learner production. Both are fundamentally comparative in design, and both are uninterpretable without an approrpiate comparison population: a register feature is only “characteristic” relative to other registers, just as a learner feature is only “overused” relative to some reference variety.

The two design are nevertheless not equivalent, and conflating them is a methodological risk best to be avoided. Register analysis targets situational variation: its axis of comparison is the situational context (purpose, channel, participants, topic9, and its populations may both consist of expert native users. CIA, by contrast, targets variation associated with learner status: its axis of comparison is whether the writer or speaker is a learner or a native user, and its situations are ideally held constant. The distinction has a direct consequence for th design of the present study: if the learner corpus and the native-speaker reference corpus differ not only in learner status, but also in register, then any observed differences are ambiguous between the two axes. Chapter 2’s characterisation of online news comments (ONC) as a distinctive evaluative written register therefore plays a double role in this thesis: it motivates the choice of the BBC Have Your Say data as a reference corpus, and it frames the interpretation of any difference between the learner and reference corpora that remains once register has been matched.

3.6 Interpreting differences between learner and native-speaker data

Comparability of design does not by itself settle the prior question of what such a difference means. This subsection reviews the debate over the native norm that will inform the interpretive stance of this thesis.

3.6.1 The native corpus as reference population, not as idealised target

Early CIA studies were sometimes read, and occaisionally writtern, as if the native corpus represented an idealised target, and that every quantitative divergence from it a deficiency. This reading has been progressively abandoned within LCR itself. Granger’s (2015) reappraisal of the method she introduced two decades earlier is decisive here: the revised model, CIA2, replaces the single idealised native norm with multiple possible reference language varieties, and reconceives the two comparison types as informing each other bidirectionally. Gilquin (2022) systematises the point: while learner corpous studies “can dispense with a native norm, they usually rely on one”, and choosing the most approporiate among the avaialble native and non-native norms is of crucial importance. The native corpus is thus best understood as a referenc population, a sample of what a defined community of users actually does in a defined situation, rather than as a standard of correctness.

This interpretation also reflects a fact about native reference corpora that is often underplayed: they are themselves samples from particular populations. LOCNESSS, the reference copus of the ICLE tradition, consist of student rather tham professional writing [Granger (1998a); see 2.5.2 below]; treating it as a prescriptive model would both overvalue native production and misrepresent the particular native population sampled.

3.6.2 The comparative fallacy

The strongest caution against target-relative analysis predates learner corpora. Bley-Vroman (1983) indentified what he termed the “comparative fallacy”, “the mistake of studyimg the systematic character of one language by comparing it to another” (p. 6), arguing that constructs defined relative to the target language, such as error, omission and substitution, risk obscuring the internal logic of the interlanguage system. Taken as methodlogical caution rather than a prohibition, the point constrains interpretation rather than design; comparison with native data remains legitimate as a discovery procedure, provided that interlanguage is not thereby defined as failed nativeness and that quantitative divergence is not automatically read as deficit (Granger 2015).

3.6.3 Implication for this study

These considerations determine the status of the BBC Have Your Say corpus in this thesis. It does not function as a prescriptive model of how the Japanese learners ought to write, and still less as a matched control in an experimental sense: the two corpora differ in period, collection circumstances and the communities sampled, and no attempt is made to equate them beyond register. This is precisely the “benchmark, not matched control” framing established in Chapter 1, now given its LCR justificatins. Consistent with CIA2 (Granger 2015) and Gilquin’s (2022) account of corpus-derived norms, I interpret any difference between the learner corpus and this benchmark as evidence about how the learners’ production responds to register, and not as an error count or a measure of distance from an ideal.

3.7 Learner corpus design and comparability

If a comparison of learner and native-speaker corpora is to support inferences about learner status, the corpora must differ, as far as possible, in learner status alone. This subsection reviews the principal desgin variables that condition such comnparability, L1 composition, proficiency operationalisation, and task and prompt conditions, and closes with the comparability principle that governs interpretation in later chapters.

The first variable is L1 composition. Multi-L1 corpora such as ICLE support cross-linguistic comparison and the identification of patterns shared across learner populations (Granger 1993, 2003); but when a single L1 group is the object of the study, a multi-L1 design confounds with institutional context, since each national subcorpus is collected in a difference educational system under different local conditions [CITATION?]. The case for a learner sample that is homogeneous in L1, as the present corpus largely is (approximately 98 per cent of contributors have Japanese as their L1), is correspondingly strong: it isolates the population of interest and aligns with existing Japanese learner resources such as the JEFLL Corpus (Tono 2007) and the Japanese component of ICNALE (Ishikawa 2013). Recent large-scale evidence also counsels humility about L1 effects: cluster analysis of over a million words of ICNALE data from more than 2,300 Asian learners suggest that L1-based similarity may be considerably lower than generally believed once other variables are controlled (Ishikawa 2025), strengthening the case for controlling, rather than assuming, L1-related variation.

The second variable is proficiency operationalisation. In the ICLE tradition, proficiency was assigned by institutional status, with CEFR-based rating added only later (Granger et al. 2009). This reliance on institutional status is not an idiosyncrasy of ICLE but the dominant practice in SLA research generally, whose operationalisations range from impressionistic judgement and institutional status to standardised tests (Thomas 1994). Carlsen (2012) concludes that proficiency is “best described as a fuzzy variable in computer learner corpora, representing a potential source of error” in LCR. More recent designs respond with explicit measurement: ICNALE combines a standard vocabulary size test with self reported standardised test scores, and classifies all writers into CEFR-linked bands (Ishikawa 2013, 2018). For the present thesis, proficiency is treated as a variable controlled and SOMETHING ABOUT Chapter 4.

The third variable is the task. Prompts, timing, length constraints and access to reference tools systematically shape the vocabulary, grammar, stance and phraseology of learner output. In a large-scale analysis of the EFCAMDAT corpus, Alexopoulou et al. (2017) show that prompt and task requirements have measurable effects on linguistic complexity and accuracy. Corpus documentation acknowledges the point: ICLE’s task conditions (timing, dictionary use, prompt) were not uniform across contributing teams and are recorded as metadata (Granger et al. 2009), and JEFLL’ documentation likewise notes task effects on writing quality (Tono 2007).

It is at this point that the analytical distinction between task, genre and register, maintained throughout this thesis following Biber and Conrad (2009), becomes a crucial methodological consideration. A task is a concrete elicitation event (a prompt, a time limit, rules on the use of reference tools); a genre is a culturally recognised, conventionally structured text category; a register is a situationally defined language variety characterised by pervasive functional linguistic patterns. Learner essays, for example, constitute a task and a genre but also, at the situational level, a register whose communicative purpose (displaying assessed academic argumentation [CITATION?) differs sharply from that of public evaluative commentary. A comparison of learner and native-speaker writing that confounds these levels risks attributing to learner status what in fact belongs to the elicitation task or to the situational variety sampled [CITATIONS]. These considerations converge on what Connor and Moreno (2005) call a common platform of comparison, which I formulate in this thesis as the comparability principle: a difference between learner and native-speaker writing is attributable to learner status only to the extent that other major sources of variation, register and genre above all, but also task conditions, proficiency and L1 composition, have been controlled or accounted for (Connor and Moreno 2005; Moreno 2008).

Connor and Moreno (2005) argue that a common platform is required “at all levels of the research”, from corpus compilation to the identification of the linguistic resources compared; Moreno (2008) specifies the relevant similarity constraints, genre, subject matter, writers’ expertise and mode; and Gablasova, Brezina, and McEnery (2017) address the statistical side of comparing frequencies across corpora [GRIES THREE LEVELS OF CORPUS COMPARISON??].

3.8.1 ICLE and the learner-essay tradition

The International Corpus of Learner English established the template of large-scale, systematic learner writing: approximately 3.7 million words of mostly argumentative essays by advanced EFL and ESL learners from sixteen mother-tongue backgrounds, among them Japanese (Granger et al. 2009). The ICLE design made it possible for the first time to compare learners across L1 backgrounds and to analyse overuse and underuse in large samples. Its limitations for present purposes follow directly from 2.4: the tradition is centred on academic and argumentative writing produced under conditions that varied across contributing teams, and its population is uniformly advanced and adult. As Granger (2024) notes, the early corpora were constrainedin age (adults), proficiency (advanced), medium (written) and text type (argumentative essays), a profile that overlaps only partially with the informal, public, evaluative register examined in this thesis.

3.8.2 LOCNESS as native-speaker reference

Because CIA’s first configuration requires native comparison data, the Louvain team collected the Louvain Corpus of Native English Essays, “the mirror of the ICLE” (Granger et al. 2009, 42): 324,304 words of mostly argumentative essays by British A-level pupils and British and American university students (Granger 1998a). Two design features carry the wider lesson. The comparison is meaningful only because the datasets are comparable in purpose and genre, so that differences could plausibly be attributed to learner status rather than text type; and LOCNESS deliberately samples novice writers, students rather than professionals, which forecloses any interpretation of it as idealised prose (Granger 1998a). Both features prefigure the treatment of the BBC corpus in this thesis: it is matched to the learner data for register, it represents one specific population, and it is not treated as prescriptive.

3.9 Why ICNALE was developed

The International Corpus Network of Asian Learners of English was developed explicitly in response to the uncontrolled variation documented above. Reviewing earlier learner corpora, Ishikawa (2011) observes that differences in writing conditions may influence the language of essays, concluding that “a more controlled learner corpus is needed for a more reliable cross-interlanguage study [pp. 3-4]. In the Written Essays module, writing conditions are”controlled more strictly compared with other major learner corpora” (Ishikawa 2013): two fixed prompts, twenty to forty minutes, 200-300 words, no reference tools, and proficiency operationalised through a vocabulary size test and/or standarised test scores mapped onto CEFR-linked bands (Ishikawa 2011, 2013, 2018). The JEFLL Corpus (Tono 2007) had already demonstrated the value of tight task control within a single L1 Japanese population, 10,038 secondary school students writing twenty-minute compositions on assigned topics without assistance, but at lower proficiency and without an integrated native component; ICNALE combined this degree of control with adult, cross-regional coverage and native comparison data (Ishikawa 2018, 2023).

3.9.1 ICNALE as methodological precedent

ICNALE constitutes the methodological precedent closest to the present study: it shows that comparisons of Asian, and specifically Japanese, learner English with native-speaker data and across L1 groups are most reliable when L1 composition, proficiency, topic and task are controlled at the point of data collection, rather than adjusted for post hoc (Ishikawa 2013, 2023). The present thesis adopts the same logic but shifts its object: where ICNALE controls the task by fixing it, this study controls the register by matching it, comparing learner and native texts produced in the same situational variety, the online news comment, rather than under the same elicitation task. This shift raises the question that the remainder of this chapter addresses, and it is here that the limits of existing learner corpora become apparent: even the most highly controlled learner corpora remain corpora of predominantly argumentative, academic writing. The patterns documented in them, overuse and underuse of stance devices, distinctive phraseological profiles, sensitivity to proficiency, were established in a register of assessed academic display. Do those patterns extend to an informal, public, evaluative online register whose situational characteristics, and hence whose functional linguistic demands, differ systematically from the learner essay? Register theory (Biber and Conrad 2009) predicts that they should not transfer unchanged; the following sections of this chapter provide the framework within which the thesis tests that prediction.

3.10 Register as a necessary variable in learner corpus research

This section advances the chapter’s central argument: that differences between learner and native-speaker writing cannot be meaningfully interpreted independently of register. A learner who produces an informal, speech-like argumentative essay may not be displaying a deficient interlanguage so much as a mismatch between the situational demands of the task and the linguistic choices the writer has (or has not) learned to make. If register effects are large, then comparisons drawn from a single regiser, or from registers mismatched across writer groups, risk attributing to “learner status” what is in fact variation in communivative context. The section moves from the general principle that all language varies by register (3.1) and the multidimensional evidence on learner writing (3.2 and 3.3) to learners’ register awareness (3.4), the academic bias of learner corpora (3.5) and the scarcity of research on informal online learner writing (3.6), before drawing out the consequences for stance (3.7).

3.10.1 Learner language varies by register and task

The foundational insight of register research is that no speaker or writer produces a single invariant variety. This was discussed in the last chapter (section 2.X), but here I will offer a brief recap. Linguistic choice varies systematically with the situational characteristics of communicative events: their purpose, audience, topic, mode and degree of interactivity. Biber’s (1988) factor-analytic study of twenty-three spoken and written registers deomnstrated that this variation is organised into a small number of underlying dimensions, such as involved versus informational production, each defined by clusters of co-occuring lexico-grammatical features with shared cmmunicative functions. The cross-linguistic extension of this work showed that analogous dimensions structure register variation across typologically unrelated languages, suggesting that register is a cross-linguistically general determinant of linguistic form (Biber 1995). Biber and Conrad (2009) subsequently codified the analystical apparatus, distinguishing register (a variery defined by its situation of use), genre (a variety defined by its conventional textual structure), and style (a variety defined by aesthetic or individual preference). The distinction matters for learner corpus research because task, genre and register are often conflated in corpus design, even though they are analytically separable and can have indepenednet effects on output.

If register variation is a basic property of language use among mature speakers, there is no principled reason to expect learner language to be exepmt from it. Learners, too, adjust their linguistic choices to purpose, audience and mode, although the extent and appropriateness of that adjustment is itself a developmental question (see 3.4). The implications for corpus design were recognised early; Granger (1996) stressed that external variables, including proficiency and task type, must be controlled so that comparison are made “like with like”. The register-functional pespective adds a pedagogical motivation: because feature distributions differ across registers, frequency information differentiated by register, as opposed to intuitions blind to it, should inform what learners are taught and how their production is assessed (Biber and Reppen 2002). What changed over the following two decades was not the principle but the availability of methods and corpora capable of quantifying register effects in large bodies of learner data. The multidimensional analysis of learner writing, reviewed next, is the most direct expression of this development.

3.10.2 Multidimensional analysis and learner writing: four questions

Multidimensional analysis (MDA), the methodlogical framework introduced by Biber (1988), identifies dimensions of variation from the statistical co-occurence of lexico-grammatical features across texts and registers. Applied to learner data, it allows the comparison with native-speaker writing to be reframed: instead of asking whether learner writing differs from native-speaker writing overall, one can ask on which dimensions, under which situational conditions, and relative to which comparison varieties the differences arise. The literature can usefully be organised around four questions: (1) Do learner and native writing differ multidimensionally? (2) Do such differences vary by proficiency? (3) Do they vary by task or register? (4) Does register account for variation that would otherwise be attributed to learner status or influence of first languages?

3.10.3 Question 1: Do learner and native writing differ multidimensionally?

The earliest multidimensional analyses of learner corpora answered this question affirmatively, but with important qualifications. Working with a corpus of Tswana Learner English, Rooy (2008) and Rooy and Terblanche (2009) compared learner writing with L1 student writing and published writing on Biber (1988) dimensions, finding learner essays to be more involved and speech-like than the comparison varieties. Friginal and Weigle (2014) and WeigleFriginal2015 extended the approach to timed impromptu test essays, finding that they differed markedly on several dimensions from authentic disciplinary writing in the Michigan Corpus of Upper-level Student Papers (MICUSP), so that the exam register proved a poor proxy for the writing that students must actually produce at university. These studies established that L1-L2 differences are real and multidimensional, but that also contained the seed of a problem: the comparison varieties differed from the learner data in register as well as writer status, leaving open how much of the observed differences was attributable to which factor.

3.10.4 Question 2: Do mutidimensional differences vary by proficiency?

Developmental work shows that observed differences are strongly conditioned by proficiency, but in ways that are contingent on register. Biber et al. (2011) argued that the clausal subordnination indices long used to measure writing development are characteristic of conversation rather than academic prose, and proposed instead a phrasal-commplexity hypthesis: development in academic writing consists largely in the increasing use of phrasal modifiers within noun phrases. Corpus evidence broadly supports the register-functional account. In the Britishh Academic Written English (BAWE) corpus, phrasal commplexity increased and clausal complexity decreased across levels of study, with strong additional effects of discipline and genre family (Staples et al. 2016), and longitudinal L2 disciplinary writing develops over time towards register norms (Biber, Reppen, et al. 2020). Crucially, proficiency effects interact with task: Biber et al. (2016) showed that both proficiency level and TOEFL iBT task type predicted lexico-grammatical complexity profiles, and Biber, Gray, et al. (2020) cautioned that complexity measures conflate description with prediction unless register is explicitly modelled. Proficiency, in short, is not register neutral.

3.10.5 Question 3: Do differences vary by task and register?

Here the evidence is unusually consistent across research groups and corpus types. Within L1 student writing, Hardy and Römer (2013) identified four dimensions of variation across MICUSP’s disciplines and paper types, demonstrating substantial register and genre differentiation even among successful writers, and Gardner et al. (2019), in a new MDA of BAWE, argued that situational parameters (discipline, level of study, genre family) account for more of the variation in student writing than writer background does. Within learner writing, task effects of comparable magnitude have been documented from large-scale data: Alexopoulou et al. (2017) showed that linguistic complexity and accuracy in EF Cambridge Open Langugage Database (EFCAMDAT) varied systematically across narrative, descriptive and professional task types, and interacted with proficiency; Qin and Uccelli (2016) found Chinese EFL learners’ writing performance to differ across genres, serving different communicative functions; and Yoon and Polio (2016) found that longitudinal L2 development itself followed different trajectories in narrative and argumentative genres. Staples and Reppen (2016), analysing first-year L2 university writing across L1 backgrounds, genres and language ratings, found genre effects as strong as or stronger thatn L1 and rating effects. Perhaps most consequentially for assessment-driven research, Staples et al. (2018) demonstrated through situational and linguistic comparison that the register of TOEFL iBT writing tasks is mismatched with authentic disciplinary writing, undermining generalisations from exam essays to academic writing ability. 3.12 Register development and register awareness

If register shapes the differences observed between learner and native-speaker writing, a further question arises: do learners develop the ability to adapt their linguistic choices to communicative context? Addressing this requires distinguishing development from increasing native-likeness. Development need not mean converging on a single native norm; it may equally mean an increasing ability to differentiate one’s linguistic choices by context, deploying more informational packaging where the situation demands it, and more involved, interpersonal features where it does not.

Evidence for such register flexibilty, though still limited, is suggestive. Qin and Uccelli (2020) had 263 EFL learners from three L1 backgrounds (Chinese, French and Spanish) each produce two texts on the same topic: a personal email to a close friend, and an academic report to an educational authority. Learners adjusted lexical, syntactic and discourse-organisational features across the two contexts, and higher proficiency was associated with greater differentiation of complex noun phrases between the academic and colloquial conditions, although the relationship between proficiency and flexibilty was not consistent across all linguistic levels and L1 groups. Larsson and Kaatari (2020) compared learner writing against four BNC registers ranked on a formality cline (academic prose, popular science, news, fiction) and found that phrasal complexity tracked the cline: the learners “generally exhibited appropriate register awareness”, differing from published writers chiefly in their use of complex nominals (Larsson and Kaatari 2020, Abstract). Perception studies add a caveat: novice writers’ and EAP teachers’ judgements of formality across registers align only partially with corpus-derived formality measures (Dixon et al. 2023; Larsson et al. 2023), so register awareness as learners and teachers perceive it is not the same thing as the register sensitivity that corpus measures detect.

Set against this partial sensitivity is the well-documented leakage of informal, speech-like features into learners’ academic prose. Gilquin and Paquot (2008) analysis of ICLE argumentative essays against L1 student writing showed learners overusing features typical of conversation (questions, imperatives, second-person pronouns, vague nouns), a pattern they interpreted not as simple difficiency, but as register confusion conditioned by input and teaching materials dominated by speech. Larsson (2019) showed that learners’ overuse of grammatcial stance markers varies by register and argued that formality is better treated as a cline rather than a dichotomy, since both learners and native speakers modulate stance marking acrosss registers of graded formality. Taken together, this literature supports a developmental reading: register awareness in L2 writers is real but incomplete, and it apparent absence in single register studies may say more about the eliciting task than about the learners. The developmental programme around grammatical complexity (Biber et al. 2011; Staples et al. 2016; Biber, Reppen, et al. 2020) makes the same point from another direction: what develops is the capacity to meet register specific functional demands, not an abstract increase in “complexity”.

3.13 The academic register bias of learner corpus research

3.15 Synthesis: register as a necessary variable

The literature reviewed in this section converges on a single conclusion. Learner language cannot be interpreted independently of communicative context. Register and task produce substantial, systematic variation within learner populations, variation that is routinely of the same order as and sometimes larger than, the variation attributed to proficiency or L1 background (Alexopoulou et al. 2017; Staples and Reppen 2016; Gardner et al. 2019; Larsson et al. 2021). Proficiency itself manifests differently in different registers, since what counts as development depends on the functional demands of the target variety (Biber et al. 2011; Yoon and Polio 2016). Comparisons across mismatched registers confound these effects, and the field’s historical concentration on academic essays has meant that many well-known “learner features” are entangled with the register in which they were observed (Gilquin and Paquot 2008; Gilquin 2015; Larsson et al. 2021).

These conclusions are especially consequential for the study of stance, the focus of Section 4. Stance marking is among the linguistic phenomena most sensitive to situational variation: the expression of attitudes, evaluations and epistemic commitments responds directly to a text’s purpose, its imagined audience and the interpersonal relatonship between writer and reader (Biber 1988; Biber and Conrad 2009; Larsson 2019). 4 Stance and phraseological realisation in L2 writing

4.1 Stance in L2 writing:from concept to empirical question

Chapter 2 set out the conception of stance adopted in this thesis: the writer’s textual projection of attitude, evaluation and epistemic commitment. The construct’s classic articulation is Du Bois’s (2007) stance triangle, in which a single stance act simultaneously evaluates an object, positions a subject and aligns with other subjects; the terminological variety of the construct across research traditions is surveyed by Gray and Biber (2012). Perhaps the most influential operationalisation of this came through Hyland’s (2005) model of stance and engagement, in which hedges, boosters, attitude markers and self-mention constitute the writer-oriented resources of academic interaction. While Chapter 2 establised the functional and theoretical concept of stance (which will not be repeated here), the present section reviews what L2 research has found about learners’ realisation of stance. What it asks is an empirical question: what does L2 writing research show about how learners deploy these resources, and through what linguistic means are learner-native differences realised? The answer, developed across the subsections that follow, is that L2 writers differ from native writers not so much in the gross quantity of stance marking as in its calibration??: the range of devices they command, the precicion with which they grade commitment, and the fit between their rhetorical habits and the norms of the target register (Hyland and Milton 1997; Ädel 2006; BaxNakatsusharaWaller2019?). Crucially for the argument of this chapter, almost all of this evidence comes from institutional, academic or examination genres, a limitation taken up in Sections 4.4 and 4.8.

4.2 Epistemic stance: hedging and boosting

The epistemic dimension of stance, the expression of doubt and certainty, has attracted the largest share of L2 research attention, partly because its pedagogical stakes were identified early. Hyland (1994) demonstrated that hedging is a defining characteristic of academic writing, allowing writers to express uncertainty and deference to readers, yet found that textbooks widely used English for Academic Purposes (EAP) classes displayed “an ignorance of empirical usage”, neglecting precisely the forms whose misuse produces pragmatic failure for L2 science students. His book-length treatment of hedging in scientific research articles framed hedging and boosting as the negotiation of knowledge claims within disciplinary commmunities rather than as optional stylisitic ornament (Hyland (1998)], setting the agenda for the learner corpus work that followed.

The canonical emipirical study remains Hyland and Milton (1997). Drawing on a corpus of approximately one million words, they compared the expression of doubt and certainty in examination scritps by 900 Cantonese-speaking Hong Kong school leavers and 770 British students of similar age and level; because the scripts had been graded by the examination authority, the design also permitted a comparison by proficiency, with higher-graded scripts exhibiting patterns closer to the native benchmark. They also found that the L2 writers differed significantly from the native speakers “in relying on a more limited range of items, offering stronger commitments, and exhibiting greater problems in conveying a precise degree of certainty” (Hyland and Milton 1997, 183). The differences, in other words, concern the calibration of epistemic commitment rather than the amount. Miscalibration can run in either direction: writing may appear “too direct, runnning the risk of being considered as either brusque or dogmatic, or as too tentative, and therefore seem as equivocal, different or naive” (Hyland and Milton 1997, 186). While these learners were from a different L1 background to the present study, Hyland and Milton (1997) provide SOMETHING about L2 epistemic stance in general.

Subsequent work complicated any simple “learners hedge less” narrative further. Hinkel (1997), analysing 150 argumentative placement essays by native speakers and by learners from Chinese, Korean, Japanese and Indonesian background, found that L2 writers used vagueness and ambiguity markers more frequently than native writers, while not differing in conventional lexical hedges, cautioning against equating indiretness with hedging. Her larger-scale comparisons converged on a restricted and skewed epistemic repertoire, with L2 essays over-relying on amplified intensifiers and strong modal obligations while underusing conventional hedging forms (Hinkel 2002, 2005). The complementary overuse side of the picture was established by Ädel (2006), whose comparison of argumentative essays by advanced Swedish learners (the Swedish ICLE component) with native British and American student essays found that the learners’ general overuse of metadiscourse, particularly of writer-visible devices, “strongly marks them as lacking in communicative competence.” That advanced learners can simultaneously over-mark stance and under-differentiate it is the strongest available evidence that the difference between learner and native-speaker stance lies in calibration to genre and audience rather than in quantity. This interpretation has since been corroborated in an assessment context: Bax et al. (2019), examining some 900 examination scripts rated at CEFR B2 and C2, found that higher-level writers used fewer metadiscourse markers overall, but a significantly wider range across most marker classes, echoing, across proficiency levels, Hyland and Milton’s (1997) account of range and calibration. Quality, moreover, cuts across nativeness: Lee and Deakin (2016) report that successful argumentative essays, whether written by L1 or L2 undergraduates, deployed more hedges and engagement markers than less successful ones.

4.3 The development of stance

A second strand of SOMETHING treats stance not as a static L1-L2 contrast, but as a developmental accomplishment. The most comprehensive corpus evidence here is Aull and Lancaster (2014), who compared the stance expressions across more than 4,000 argumentative essays by incoming first-year students, upper-level undergraduate writing from the Michigan Corpus of Upper-level Student Papers (MICUSP) and published prose. Although first-year essays shared many stance markers regardless of students’ educational context, the clearest distinctions separated first-year writers from all more advanced groups, and the features indexing the developmental trajectory were specific: approximate hedges and boosters, code glosses, and adversative or contrast connectors. First-year writers underused these delimiting resources while overusing intensifying boosters such as definitely and extremely. Aull and Lancaster (2014) interpret advanced academic stance as “a process of delimiting one’s stance in a way that accounts for the view of others” (abstract), a formulation that aligns development with the calibration theme of Section 4.2: maturing writers learn not to assert more or less, but to position assertions more precisely in relation to alternative voices. Two caveats govern the use of this study in the present chapter. First, it is a novice-expert comparison of predominantly L1 writers and is therefore developmental evidence along an academic register, not an L1-L2 contrast. Second, proficiency remains a live alternative explanation whenever developmental stage and language background are confounded, a point to which Section 4.7 returns.

Genuinely longitudinal L2 evidence, though scarcer, points in the same direction. Crosthwaite and Jiang (2017) tracked Hyland’s four stance categories in a 205,682-word corpus of Hong Kong university students’ writing collected across a semester of EAP instruction, finding that hedging rose while boosting and self-mention fell, leaving students with “a more careful, narrower, less polarising and less personal range of expressions with which to convey their stance over time” (Crosthwaite and Jiang 2017, 92). Taken together with the cross-sectional findings of Bax et al. (2019) and Lee and Deakin (2016), the developmental strand suggests that stance development in L2 writing involves the progressive differentiation and disciplining of the epistemic repertoire rather than a simple, one-directional increase or decrease in stance marking.

4.4 Stance and register

Section 3 of this chapter concluded that register is consequential for the interpretation of learner language, and the stance literature illustrates why. Academic writing, the register from which nearly all the evidence reviewed above derives, conventionally rewards cautious, citation-based and disciplinary qualified commitment [WORDY??]. Hyland’s (1998) scientists hedge because knowledge claims must be negotiated with a sceptical peer community; Hyland (2005) eght-discipline comparison shows that even this generalisation is internal to a particular register, since the soft disciplines take “far more explicitly involved and personal positions” (p. 187) than the hard disciplines. The norm against which learners are judged deviant, in other words, is itself a register-specific and discipline-specifc norm.

Online news commenting (ONC) can be expected to operate under quite different conditions. Public commenting on news stories places a premium on direct evaluation, affect, assertion, agreement and disagreement, and on orientation toward a general readership rather than an assessor or disciplinary peer; there is no institutional incentive to hedge, and considerable interactional incentive to take a position. Register analysis of commenting bears out the interactional character while complicating the affective one: Cavasso and Taboada (2021), in an appraisal-based analysis of evaluation in online news comments, found that evaluation in the register is predominantly depersonalised, directed at propositions, entities and states of affairs rather than expressed as the commenter’s own feeling, with affect accounting for only 3.4 per cent of evaluative spans. Two implications follow. First, the “deficit” patterns reported for L2 acacemic writing, such as over-reliance on string commitment, restricted hedging range and excessive writer visibility, may partly reflect a mismatch between learners’ rhetorical habits and the specific norms of academic registers, rather than a general inability to calibrate stance. Second, the empirical base for any claim about L2 stance in ONC is thin in both directions: the flagship L1-2 stance studies all use argumentative or examination genres (Hyland and Milton 1997; Hinkel 1997, 2005; Lee and Deakin 2016), and where L2 epistemic stance has been examined outside institutional writing, it has proved systematically sensitive to situational factors. Gablasova, Brezina, McEnery, and Boyd (2017), analysing spoken production by 132 advanced L2 speakers across four tasks, found “systematic variation in L2 speakers’ stance-taking choices across the four tasks”, the largest contrast opposing monologic and dialogic conditions; Gablasova et al. (2024) similarly report that epistemic stance frequency in computer-mediated L2 speaking assessment was influenced by both proficiency and task type. If L2 stance is this malleable in speech, findings from academic writing cannot automatically be assumed to generalise to informal writing. Whether the calibration problems documentented above are reproduced, attentuated or transformed in register that actively licenses direct evaluation is an open question for this thesis.

4.5 Stance as phraseological patterning

The studies reviewed so far mostly operationalise stance through individually tagged items: modal verbs, hedging adverbs, attitude adjectives, metadiscursive signals. A complementary tradtion holds that stance, like much of discourse, is realised through recurrent multi-word combinations rather than isolated lexical choices. The foundational statement is corpus-based (cf. Chapter 2.X): Biber et al. (1999, 990) define lexical bundles as “recurrent expressions, regardless of their idomaticity, and regardless of their structural status”. They demonstrate that such sequences are pervasive building blocks that can differntiate register: far denser in conversationn, where they are predominantly clausal and interpersonal, than in academic prose, where the smaller inventory is predominantly phrasal and referential. The theoretical motivation for treating such sequences as psychologically real units comes from research into formulaic language, where a formulaic sequence is defined as “a sequence, continous or discontinuous, of words or other elements, which is, or appears to be, prefabricated: that is, stored and retireved whole from memory at the time of use” (Wray 2002, 9); the implications of formulaicity for second language acquisition are surveyed in Siyanova-Chanturia and Pellicer-Sánchez (2019) and not reviewed here.

Two developments made this tradition directly relevant to stance research. First, Biber et al. (2004), comparing bundles in university classroom teaching and textbooks with conversation and academic prose, proposed the functional taxonomy, stance expressions, discourse organisers and referential expressions, that nearly all subsequent learner studies have inherited; they also show that bundles “function as basic building blocks of discourse” whose distribution tracks communicative purpose (Biber et al. 2004). Second, the scope of bundle inventories was progressively documented for various regsiters. Biber and Barbieri (2007) extended the analysis across the full range of university spoken and written registers, finding that bundle density and functional composition track communicative purpose rather than mode alone, with stance bundles dominating speech-oriented registers and referential bundles dominating informational prose. Hyland (2008), analysing research articles and postgraduate theses across four disciplines, demonstrated that bundle inventories are strongly SOMETHING to discipline, with fewer than half of the top fifty bundles in any discipline occuring in any other. Simpson-Vlach and Ellis (2010) complemented this line methodologically, deriving an academic formulas list from frequency, association measures and expert judgement, with separate spoken and written components. The cummulative lesson of this tradition suggests that phraseological patterning is conditioned by register at every level, so that what counts as a nativelike stance expression is a moving target defined by community practice. The bundle approach has also been turned directly on stance itself: De Cock and Granger (2021) contrast the recurrent sequences of press releases with those of business news reports, showing that stance bundles differentiate the evaluative profiles of news-adjacent register, the closest precedent for the analytic unit adopted in this thesis. It is within this tradition that I situate the present thesis, with one difference: its unit of analysis is the stance-related phraseological pattern, a recurrent sequence defined over tagged stance features such as evaluative adjectives, stance adverbs and suasive verbs, whose lexical realisations are bundles.

4.6 Lexical bundles in learner and native-speaker production

When the bundle method is applied to learner production, the differences from native-speaker usage documented in Section 4.2 reappear at the level of recurrent sequences. An important antecedent predates the bundle terminology itself. Working with comparable corpora of native and learner speech, De Cock (1998) extracted recurrent word combinations and classified them functionally, showing that advanced learners rely heavily on formulae while commanding a differently distributed repertoire. The companion analysis of learner writing established the finding in its canonical form: advanced learners use as many prefabricated sequences as native speakers, and sometimes more, but the sequences they prefer are not the same ones, are not used with the same frequency, and do not serve the same syntactic and pragmatic functions (De Cock et al. 1998). De Cock’s (2000) extension to writing added the register dimension: advanced EFL production is repetitively formulaic in both speech and writing, with a less marked difference between the modes than native speakers display, an early corpus demonstration of the register insensitivity discussed in Section 3.4.

The fullest statement of this early line is De Cock’s (2004) comparison of fifty French-speaking learners from the Louvain International Database of Spoken English Interlanguage (LINDSEI) with the Louvain Corpus of Native English Conversation (LOCNEC). Quantitatively, learner speech proved repetitive, drawing on relatively few recurrent sequence types; qualitatively, the sequences the learners preferred were less interactional and less involved than those of the native speakers. Where native speakers signalled intersubjective alignment with vagueness tags such as sort of, kind of and and things like that, the learners reached for the more formal and so on and etcetera, underused I mean, and deployed of course in non-idiomatic ways. Learners, she concludes, lack “routinized ways of interacting and building rapport with their interlocutors and of toning down and weaving the right amount of imprecision and vagueness” (De Cock 2004, 243).

De Cock’s functional classification of recurrent sequences anticipated the taxonomy that Biber et al. (2004) would establish as the standard apparatus of bundle research, with its division into stance expressions, discourse organisers and referential expressions. Once that taxonomy was in place, comparisons of learner and native-speaker bundle use were recast in its terms. Ädel and Erman (2012) compared four-word bundles in advanced Swedish learners’ and native speakers’ undergraduate linguistics essays, finding that the native writers commanded a larger and more varied repertoire of bundle types, including hedging and negation bundles, and drew more heavily on participant-oriented stance bundles, while the learners’ narrower repertoire was skewed towards discourse organising frames. Chen and Baker (2010) triangulated the comparison across three corpora, published academic prose, L1 student writing and Chinese L2 student writing: published writing exhibited the widest bundle range and L2 writing the narrowest, with the L2 writers overusing informal expressions rarely found in expert prose while underusing high-frequency expert frames, and relying more on bundles based on verb phrases, and on discourse organisers, than either L1 group. Bychkovska and Lee (2017) supply a partial dissent: in high-rated argumentative essays by L1 Chinese and L1 English undergraduates, the L2 writers used substantially more bundle types and tokens, preferred clausal over phrasal bundles, and produced significantly more stance bundles, a pattern the authors attribute partly to register mixing and direct transfer from the L1. Read against Chen and Baker (2010) and Ädel and Erman (2012), the contrast suggests that whether learners use more or fewer bundles than native-speaker writers itself depends on task and population, which strengthens rather than weakens the calibration argument: overall quantity does not distinguish the groups.

Read together, these studies restate at the phraseological level the calibration argument of Section 4.2: learners differ from native-speaker writers less in the quantity of recurrent sequences they use than in their range, functional distribution and fit to register. Paquot and Granger (2012), synthesising this literature, single out L1 transfer as a persistent conditioning factor. Nor is informal phraseology unique to learners: Shin (2018) compared argumentative essays matched by prompt and written by L1 English and L1 Korean first-year undergraduates, and found remarkably similar bundle repertoires, with both novice groups favouring clausal bundles typical of spoken registers, so that conversation-type bundles in learner essays cannot be read as a straightforward learner marker. The studies also share the field’s register base, since every one of them examines academic writing or speech elicited in institutional settings; the phraseological behaviour of learners in more informal, public, evaluative registers remains correspondingly uncharted, the gap the present thesis addresses. 4.7 Proficiency and phraseological development

A distinct strand of bundle research asks not how learners differ from native speakers but how learner phraseology changes as proficiency develops, holding the task constant and varying rated proficiency. Chen and Baker (2016) tracked lexical bundles in learner essays rated at CEFR levels B1, B2 and C1, showing that bundle repertoires discriminate across proficiency levels, with lower-rated essays leaning disproportionately on a small set of high-frequency frames and higher-rated essays approximating the wider, more register-appropriate repertoire of expert academic prose. Assessment data complicate any assumption that more bundles signal higher proficiency: in TOEFL iBT writing responses, Staples et al. (2013) found that lower-rated writers used more bundles overall, including more bundles reproduced from the prompts themselves, while the functional distribution of bundles remained broadly similar across levels. Development, on this evidence, is not an accumulation of sequences but a redistribution: what changes with proficiency is which frames are preferred, what functions they serve, and how closely they track the norms of the target register.

This picture of phraseological development as conditioned by register aligns with the complexity findings reviewed in Section 3.2, where phrasal complexity rose and clausal complexity fell across levels of study in BAWE (Staples et al. 2016), and where both proficiency and task type predicted lexico-grammatical profiles in TOEFL iBT data (Biber et al. 2016). For the present thesis, the strand licenses treating proficiency as a conditioning variable rather than a nuisance factor: where proficiency information is available, phraseological differences must be read against it before they are attributed to L1 background or to register. It also leaves open the question taken up in Section 4.8, whether the developmental patterns established for institutional writing hold at all in the informal, public registers that institutional corpora have not sampled.

4.8 What existing L2 stance and phraseology research does not tell us

The field surveyed in this section can be summarised in terms of what is now reasonably well established. L2 writers differ from native writers in the deployment of stance, most relibaly in the calibration of epistemic commitment: a restricted range of devices, stronger and less precisely graded commitments, and coexisiting patterns of over- and undermarking (Hyland and Milton 1997; Hinkel 2005; Ädel 2006; Bax et al. 2019). Stance changes developmentally, toward delimited, dialogically aware positioning (Aull and Lancaster 2014; Crosthwaite and Jiang 2017). Differences between learner and native-speaker writing appear in recurrent multi-word sequences as well as in individual items: in bundle range, structural type and functional distribution (Chen and Baker 2010; Ädel and Erman 2012; Bychkovska and Lee 2017). And phraseological repertoires vary systematically with both proficiency and register (Chen and Baker 2016; Biber and Barbieri 2007; Hyland 2008).

What this literature does not tell us equally clear, and defines the gap the present study addresses. First, the evidence base is overwhlemingly academic: argumentative essays, examination scripts, dissertations and researcha articles. Where L2 stance has been examined in non-academic settings, it has been in speech, where it varies systematically with task, medium and individual style (Gablasova, Brezina, McEnery, and Boyd 2017; Gablasova et al. 2024). I was unable to locate any established corpus study of L2 stance in informal online written registers, and lexical bundle research on informal online registers is limited to L1 data, notably Barbieri’s (2018) study of blogs and my own earlier analysis of BBC news comments (Marchand 2013); the nearest other points of comparison are the conversation findings of the Longman Grammar (Biber et al. 1999) and multidimensional, rather than bundle-based, analyses of web registers (Grieve et al. 2010; Biber and Egbert 2016). Second, and consequently, it is unknown whether the calibration problems documented for academic writing are reproduced, attenuated, or transformed in a register such as ONC, whose communicative economy places a premium on direct evaluation and assertion rather than cautious qualification [CITATION and cf. chapter 2??]. If learners’ stance habits reflect a mismatch with the academic register rather than a general deficit, then an informal evaluative register may reveal a substantially different learner profile, and only a comparison with native-speaker data from the same register can show which is the case. The next section turns to the specific population at the centre of that comparison, Japanese learners of English, and to what is known about their stance and phraseological behaviour.

5 Japanese learners and sources of variation

The present section narrows the fouce from learner language in general to the population at the centre of this thesis: Japanese learners of English a a foreign language (EFL). The aim is not to characterise a homogenous “Japanese learner”; a construct that the critique of contrastive rhetoric has rendered untenable (Kubota 1999), but to do three more limited things: establish what corpus research has documented about this population, identify the explanations that compete whenever it is compared with native-speaker data, and assess how far findings drawn largely from argumentative essays and elicited speech extend to informal, online, evaluative discourse.

5.1 Japanese learners in learner corpus research: a well-studied population

Japanese learners are among the most extensively studied EFL populations in learner corpus research, so I should be precise about the gap I address: it is not an absence of research but the restricted register base on which that research rests. The Japanese component of the International Corpus of Learner English (ICLE) placed Japanese university students’ argumentative writing within the first large-sacle multinational learner corpus (Granger et al. 2009) and underpins early findings on connectors and pharseology discussed below (Kaneko 2007; Narita et al. 2004). At the school level, the Japanese EFL Learner (JEFLL) corpus comprises 10,038 short in-class compositions produced under controlled conditions (twenty minutes, no dicitonary, fixed topics) across the six years of Japanese secondary schooling, providing a quasi-longitudinal record of early instructed English development (Tono 2007). At the university level, the International Corpus Network of Asian Learners of English (ICNALE) supplies written and spoken modules collected under strictly standardised conditions, with fixed prompts, proficiency information for every contributor, and matched native-speaker data (Ishikawa (2013); Ishikawa (2023)]. Spoken proficiency is served by the NICT JLE Corpus of 1,281 rated oral proficiency interviews (Izumi et al. 2004), and Miki’s (2010) work compares learner essays with their native-speaker corrections. Claims about Japanese learners can this in principle be tested against large, well-documented datasets spanning school and university, speech and writing, and beginner to advanced proficiency.

5.2 Japanese learner proficiency and corpus research

A second well-developed strand concerns proficiency, which the major corpora operationalise differently, each solution carrying consequences for interpretation. ICNALE assigns writers to four bands linked to the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR), but those bands are derived by converting heterogeneous external measures such as TOEFL, TOIEC and IELTS scores or the Vocabulary Size Test, so they are best described as CEFR-linked rather than independently rated [Ishikawa2013]. JEFLL exploits the near-universal start of English instruction in the first year of secondary schooling, so that school year functions as a coarse but clean proxt for instructional exposure (Tono 2007), while the NICT JLE corpus relies on trained ratings from a standardised speaking test (Izumi et al. 2004). Corpus-based developmental research on this population is correspondingly mature, beginning with Tono’s (2000) analysis of the acquisition order of English grammatical morphemes in Japanese learner writing. Extending to recent large-scale work, Suzuki (2024) analysed over a thousand learners across spoken and written corpora, and finds that the variety and frequency of epistemic stance devices increase with proficiency, but in a non-linear fashion complicated by the multifunctionality of the devices themselves.

The implication for the present thesis is that proficiency is a live alternative explanation for any difference between the learner and reference corpora in stance or phraseology. Miki (2010) shows that the overuse of I think diminishes across TOEIC bands, with advanced writers deploying the expression more selectively at points in persuasive prose where it marks the writer’s position on a controversial issue. Ishikawa (2021) similarly reports a substantial gap between the vocabulary Japanese and other Asian learners are estimated to know (around 3,300 words on average) and the considerably smaller set they deploy in writing, constraining how any apparent phraseological restriction should be interpreted. The proficiency profile of the participants in the present study, and the procedures used to establish it, are accordingly treated as design variables in their own right and are described in Chapter 4.

5.3 L1 Influence and contrastive rhetoric

The oldest and most contested explanation for distinctive features of Japanese learner writing is first language (L1) influence, and its intellectual home is contrastive rhetoric. Kaplan (1966) initiated the field with the claim that “Logic (in the popular, rather the logician’s sense of the word) which is the basis of rhetoric, is evolved out of a culture; it is not universal” (p. 2). proposing patterns of paragraph development that are culture specific, including a famously indirect pattern attributed to “Oriental” writing. Connor (1996) consolidated this tradition into a research programme that sought to explain second-language composing difficulties by reference to the rhetorical strategies of the first language, and Hinds (1987) supplied its most influential claim as regards Japanese discourse: that Japanese prose is “reader-responsible”, leaving interpretive work to the audience, in contrast to “writer-responsible” English. Connor (2002) subsequently repositioned the field as intercultural rhetoric, broadening its methods in response to the critiques.

These critiques, however, substantially qualify what contrastive rhetoric can license for the present study. Kubota (1997), (1999) showed empirically that direct, deductive organisational patterns coexist with inductive onew n Japanese expository writing, and argued that the image of a uniquely indirect Japanese rhetoric is a discourse construct rather than a descriptive fact. Kubota and Lehner (2004) and Atkinson (2004) generalised the objecti0on, contending that contrastive rhetoric has operated with static, essentialised cultural dichotomies that obscure variation within cultures, across genres and over time (see also Leki 1991). Empirically, transfer itself appears partial rather than wholesale: Kubota (1998) found that Japanese university students carried some organisational features from L1 to L2 writing but by no means all. The constructive alternative is Matsuda’s (1997) dynamic model, in which L2 text feature emerge from the interaction of L1, proficiency, genre knowledge, instruction and individual variation. The position adopted in this thesis is accordingly a qualified one. Japanese L1 background is treated as one plausible explanatory factor among several, not as the first link in an inference from Japanese learners to Japanese rhetorical culture to distinctive stance phraseology; any putative L1 effect must survive comparison with the alternatives examined in Section 5.5, and the study’s design, set out there and in Section 6.4, attempts to separate differences attributable to learner status from those attributable to register and other sources of variation.

5.4 Japanese learners’ discourse-pragmatic and stance choices

The evidence most directly relevant to this thesis concerns how Japanese learners deploy specific linguistic resources: hedging devices, assertive and self-mention devices, organsiational connectors, evalautive language and recurrent phraseological sequences. This subsection organises that evidence by resource rather than by study. Two things should be clarified here: the evidence base consists almost entirely of argumentative essays and elicited speech, so it would be a stretch to generalise to other registers; the findings themselves admit several explanations, so they are reported here in descriptive terms, with interpretation to follow in Section 5.5.

5.4.1 Hedging and epistemic modality

A recurring finding is a narrow epistemic repertoire (which is admitedly sometimes mismatched by register). Hollmann et al. (2024) examined the modal verbs may, might and could in Japanese undergraduates’ academic writing and found that could was used with high frequency, but often informally an insufficientlu in its epistemice-possibility sense; notably, their central interpretive frame is register, following Gilquin & Paquot’s (2008) account of learner writing as “too chatty”, rather than L1 influence. Suzuki (2024), applying a Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL) value framework to spoken and written corpora, concurs with earlier studies that Japanese learners rely on epistemic adverbs and lexical verbs (such as I think, I guess and maybe) rather than modal verbs to realise epistemic modality, and that small repertoires concentrate on a few high-frequency items, especially at lower proficiency. Pemberton (2020), analysing a purpose-built corpus of 190 handwritten argumentative essays, adds two findings that complicate cultural explanations: the frequency of modal devices varied by topic, and learners used more hedges than boosters on every topic, a pattern that correlated with the school curriculum rather than any cultural variable. Taken together, these studies may suggest that Japanese learners hedge, but with a restricted inventory whose deployment is sensitive to topic, instruction and register rather than to culture alone.

5.4.2 Boosting, direct assertion and writer visibility

Counter to the stereotype of the indirect, non-commital Japanese writer, several corpus studies report overuse of assertive and self-mention devices. Kobayashi (2016), comparing six L1 groups in ICNALE using a Hyland-style metadiscourse taxonomy (Hyland 2005??), found that Japanese learners used self-mentions, boosters, frame markers and attitude markers more frequently than other groups, explicitly against the prediction that writers from a “reader-responsible” language background should underuse such markers. The single most studied item is I think. McCrostie (2008) documents its overuse, particularly in sentence-initial position, in a corpus of over 300 Japanese university essays, and Miki (2010), using native-speaker corrections as the comparison baseline, confirms significant overuse across proficiency bands while cautioning against a purely transfered-based account. “Not all of the categorical misuses by the Japanese learners are due to L1 transfer; they are lacking in the knowledge of the proper uses of I think” (pp. 178-179). Natsukari (2012) adds a functional qualification, arguing that learners’ I think is often closer in force to native-speaker I believe, a marker of committed stance rather than a hedge. The compostite picture is one of calibration rather than avoidance: stance is expressed, but sometimes at the wrong strength, in the wrong position or in the wrong register.

5.4.3 Discourse organisation

The classic connector study is Narita et al. (2004), who compared the ICLE Japanese subcorpus with LOCHNESS and found that Japanese learners “significantly oversue the logical connectors in sentence initial positions”, overusing connectors such as for example, of course and first, while underusing then, yet and instead (p. 1174). Crucially for the question of competing questions, a parallel comparison with French, Swedish and Chinese learner data showed that the four groups share a common core of over- and underused connectors, with [L1-specific residue superimposed]??; the authors attribute the overuse largely to learners’ incomplete grasp of the distinction between adverbial connectors and conjunctions, offering a developmental or instructional explanation rather than one derived from a cultural-rhetorical background. This pattern, in which an apparently Japanese feature turns out to be shared across L1 groups, recurs throughout this section, and is central to Section 5.5.

5.4.4 Evaluation and interpersonal positioning

Evaluative and affective resources have been less extensively studied than modality, but the available evidence again resists the deficit-and-indirectness stereotype. Kobayashi (2016) reports overuse of attitude markers alongside boosters and self-mentions in Japanese ICNALE esssays, and Kamimura and Oi (1998) identified differences in argumentative strategies between American and Japanese students’ English essays, including the use of generalisations and of I think as a softening rather than emphatic device, locating group differences in how evaluation is packaged rather than whether it is present. Evidence from informal online interaction remains extremely thin: Matsuoka (2021) reports that Japanese EFL learners in online forum discussion used a limited range of modal functions for social interaction, with certain pragmalinguistic norms appearing to have stabilised, but this is a small-scale conference study and, alongside my own work with Akutsu [CITATION], stands virtually alone as corpus evidence from an online register in Japan.

5.4.5 Phraseology and recurrent sequences

Finally phraseological research suggests that Japanese recurrent sequences form a restricted repertoire that is partially distinctive due to L1. Ishikawa (2021), analysing trigrams in the ICNALE edited essays across four Asian L1 groups, found that learners overused sequences built around basic verbs, bare singular nouns an certain logical connectors, while underusing subjunctive and epistemic modal constuctions; of particular relevance here, Japanese learners were distinctive in their high-frequency trigram profiles. The most direct bundle-level evidence is Sitler and Spivey (2025) multi-modal register analysis of four-word bundles across the ICNALE written essays and spoken monologues: Japanese learners relied on a larger proportion of verb-phrase-based bundles than the other groups, with a restricted range of bundle types and limited structural diversity in speech, and overall bundle frequency decreased as proficiency increased. Their title itself carries the alignment finding: I agree with this emerges as the dominant realisation of the first-person alignment frame in Japanese EFL argumentative writing, independently confirming the salience of a pattern this thesis pursues in the comment register. Kaneko (2007), in an early small-scale study of two- to four-word clusters in Japanese English majors’ writing, likewise described a restricted repertoire in which a few function words combine around a core content word; given its scale, its findings are best treated as suggestive. Whether such repertoires persist in, or are reshaped by, informal evaluative registers is unknown, because the phraseological studies reviewed here and in Section 4 all concern academic or argumentative writing.

5.5 L1, proficiency, task, register, topic and time as competing explanations

The studies reviewed above share a methodological vulnerability that matters directly for the design of the present thesis: each documents a difference between Japanese learner output and some native-speaker baseline, yet the explanations on offer are rarely pitted against one another within a single design. This subsection therefore confronts the inferential question on which the whole thesis turns: if the learner corpus differs from the reference corpus, what may legitimately be inferred from the difference? To make the problem concrete, return to the hypothetical finding introduced in Section 1.2: a hedged evaluative frame, characteristic of native-speaker comment discourse, that is markedly less frequent in the Japanese learners’ comments than in the BBC Have Your Say reference corpus. At least six explanations are available, each with prima facie support in the literature reviewed in this chapter.

First, L1 influence: the function the bundle serves in English comment discourse may be realised by different rhetorical means in Japanese, and learners may transfer those means, or avoid the function altogether (Connor 1996). Against this, transfer has repeatedly shown to be partial (Kubota 1998), and patterns attributed to Japanese rhetoric have turned out to be shared across unrelated L1 groups (Narita et al. 2004) [PLUS more CITATIONS SUPPORTING TRANSFER]?. Second, proficiency: the bundle may simply be beyond the learners’ current repertoire, since epistemic and phraseological repertoires expand, non-linearly, with proficiency (Ishikawa 2021; Suzuki 2024), and even robust overuses such as I think attenuate across proficiency bands (Miki 2010). Third, task: the conditions under which the comments were produced, whether timed or untimed, prompted or spontaneous, individually written or classroom-assigned, measurably alter linguistic output (Way et al. 2000), and the learners’ production conditions almost certainly differed from those of the native commenters. Fourth, register: the learners may be producing a different register from the reference corpus, or an informalised version of the target register; learner writing is pervasively “too chatty” (Gilquin and Paquot 2008), and Japanese learners’ epistemic choices in particular have been characterised as register-inappropriate rather than ungrammatical (Hollmann et al. 2024). Fifth, topic: stance and modality frequencies vary with subject matter, as Pemberton (2020) demonstrates directly for Japanese learners, whose modal usage tracked topic and curriculum rather than culture, and as topic effects on syntactic and lexical measurees confirm more generally (Yang et al. 2015). Sixth, time: the two corpora differ in their period of data collection, and registers are not static. The BBC comments date from the early 2000s, and some part of any difference between the corpora may reflect diachronic drift in the register itself, or in the wider ecology of online discourse, rather than any property of the learners.

Larsson, Paquot and Biber’s (2021) study is again instructive here: beyond establishing that register is a major source of variation in learner writing (Section 3.3), their multidimensional analysis modelled L1 background and register as co-predictors across five L1 groups and multiple written registers. Their demonstration that significant differences emerge both between writer groups and across registers implies that, for Japanese learners, register cannot simply be acknowledged and set aside: it must be actively disentangled from L1 and proficiency. Concretely, an L1 account requires evidence that the effect is specific to Japanese learners relative to other L1 groups of comparable proficiency writing in the same register; a proficiency account requires that bundle use vary with proficiency within the Japanese sample; task and topic accounts require comparisons across tasks and topics; and ruling register in or out requires, at minimum, that the learner and reference corpora represent the same register, as the comparability principle requires (Section 2.4), supplemented where possible by situating both corpora among a wider range of native-speaker registers. A time-based explanation, finally, is mitigated by treating the BBC data explicitly as a benchmark of the register at a defined historical moment, rather than as a timeless native norm. ICNALE’s design, with fixed prompts, controlled conditions and proficiency bands for ten Asian L1 groups (Ishikawa 2013), shows what such disentangling looks like for argumentative writing; and the proof-of-concept study for this thesis applied precisely that design, modelling sparse evaluative bundle frequencies in ICNALE Japanese and native-speaker essays with Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood and reading proficiency trajectories against the native benchmark (2026); no equivalent design exists for informal online evaluative discourse. 5.6 Synthesis

Japanese learners of English are among the most extensively studied EFL populations in learner corpus research, supported by large, well-documented resources spanning school and university, speech and writing, and multiple proficiecny levels (Granger et al. 2009; Ishikawa 2013; Izumi et al. 2004; Tono 2007). This research has documented differences, relative to native-speaker norms, in vocabulary development, grammatical development, discourse organisation, stance marking and phraseological patterning; crucially, the strongest studies simultaneously supply the counter-evidence that undermines single-cause accounts: connector overuse is shared across L1 groups (Narita et al. 2004), modality tracks topic and curriculum rather than culture (Pemberton 2020), I think overuse reflects incomplete register knowledge as much as transfer (Miki 2010), and Japanese learners tend to overuse, rather than avoid, assertive and self-mention devices (Kobayashi 2016). The contrastive-rhetoric tradition that once offered a deterministic cultural explanation for such findings has been thoroughly qualified (Atkinson 2004; Kubota 1997, 1999; Kubota and Lehner 2004), leaving L1 as one influence among many (Matsuda 1997). The pervasive limitation is one of register coverage: virtually everything known about this population comes from argumentative essays, examination conditions or elicited speech, and Japan-specific evidence from informal online interaction remains confined to a handful of small-scale studies (Matsuoka 2021). Whether the patterns documented in academic and controlled writing extend to informal, online, evaluative discourse, are attenuated there, or are transforemed, remains unclear.

6 Synthesis: From learner corpus research to learner stance in ONC

6.1 What this chapter has established: a chain of propositions

The argument of this chapter can now be stated as a chain of six propositions, each defended in the preceding sections and each a premise fot the next.

First, learner corpus research (LCR) establishes the value of systematically comparing learner and native-speaker production. By comparing large bodies of learner production with native-speaker production, Contrastive Interlanguage Analysis (CIA) makes visible quantitative divergences, the overuse and underuse of perfectly well-formed items, that error analysis was structurally unable to detect (Granger 1996; Granger 1998a). Seconc, CIA shows that such comparison is only as strong as its reference population: the native corpus is a sample of what a defined community of users does in a defined situation, not an idealised targer (Granger 2015; Gilquin 2022), which is why the BBC Have Your Say corpus functions in this thesis as a register-specific benchmark rather than a prescriptive model. Third, register research shows that learner production varies substantially with communicative context. Multidimensional studies repeatedly find register and tasks effects of the same order as, or larger than, proficiency and first langauge (L1) effects (Gardner et al. 2019; Larsson et al. 2021; Staples and Reppen 2016), and learners themselves show partial but real register awareness (Larsson and Kaatari 2020; Qin and Uccelli 2020). Fourth, L2 stance research shows systematic differences between learner and native-speaker writing in interpersonal and epistemic resources, differences located in the calibration of commitment rather than its quantity: restricted repertoires, stronger and less precisely graded claims, and simultaneous over- and under-marking (Hyland and Milton 1997; Ädel 2006; Aull and Lancaster 2014). Fifth, phraseological research shows that these differences extend to recurrent multiword sequences: learners command narrower and structurally different bundle repertoires whose composition varies with proficiency and register (Chen and Baker 2010, 2016; Ädel and Erman 2012; Biber and Barbieri 2007). Sixth, Japanese learner research identifies L1 and proficiency relevant, competing sources of variation, and repeatedly demonstrates that apparently L1-specific features are shared across unrelated L1 groups, attenuate with proficiency, or track topic and curriculum rather than culture (Miki 2010; Narita et al. 2004; Pemberton 2020).

Taken together, the six propositions yield this chapter’s central conclusion. Every explanation available for differences between learner and native-speaker stance phraseology, whether transfer, proficiency, task or topic, is sensitive to register, and register effects are independently large. Register therefore cannot be treated as a background variable when interpreting such differences: it is a constitutive condition of their interpretation.

6.2 The double gap

The conclusion of Chapter 2 and the conclusion of the present chapter intersect. Chapter 2 identified a register gap: the phraseological realisation of stance in online news commenting is not adequately understood, and no systematic account exists of how native-speaker commenters habitually express evaluation, commitment and reader orientation through recurrent sequences. The present chapter establishes a learner-context gap: research on L2 stance and stance phraseology, including the extensive work on Japanese learners (Ishikawa 2013, 2023; Tono 2007), has concentrated on academic or otherwise controlled writing, so that findings from those registers cannot be assumed to generalise to informal online evaluation. The thesis addresses the intersection of the two gaps, asking how Japanese learners realise stance phraseologically when producing in the ONC register. Stated as a single problem: the phraseological realisation of stance by Japanese learners in an informal evaluative online register has not been adequately investigated. Investigating it requires addessing both gaps at once, since a register-aware description of native ONC stance phraseology is the benchmark against which learner usage becomes interpretable at all.

6.3 Methodological implications for Chapter 4

The double gap translates into six explicit requirements that the design reported in Chapter 4 must satisfy.

  1. Register comparability. Because register effects can rival or exceed the effects of learner status (Larsson et al. 2021), the learner and reference corpora must represent the same broad register. The comparison is accordingly between Japanese learners’ online news comments and native-speaker online news comments, not between learner comments and native academic or journalistic prose.
  2. Task comparability Production condition shape linguistic output independently of register (Granger 1996). The communicative conditions under which the learner comments were produced must therefore be documented and, where possible, rendered comparable to those of the native data, with residual differences acknowledged rather than concealed.
  3. Learner comparability. Since L1 and proficiency are live competing explanations (Miki 2010; Suzuki 2024), the learner corpus must have both variables fully documented, so that both can be controlled and examined rather than assumed.
  4. An appropriate reference population. Consistent with CIA2 (Granger 2015; Gilquin 2022), the BBC Have Your Say corpus serves as a benchmark from the same register (Section 2.3.3), not as an abstract native-English model.
  5. Phraseological analysis. Because learners differ from native-speaker writers in recurrent combinations and not only in isolated items (Chen and Baker 2010; Ädel and Erman 2012), the analysis must capture recurrent multi-word sequences rather than relying on counts of individual stance markers alone.
  6. Functional interpretation. Frequency differences are evidentially inert until interpreted against discourse function (cf. Hyland and Milton 1997; Chen and Baker 2016): overuse or underuse of a form must be read in terms of what the form does in ONC, and raw counts alone cannot decide among the competing explanations reviewed in Section 5.

These requirements jointly operationalise the comparability principle set out in Section 2.4.

6.4 From review to research design

The three bodies of research reviewed in this chapter supply the components of the required response: learner corpus research the comparative framework and its interpretive cautions, register research the situational controls, and stance and phraseology research the analytical level at which differences between learner and native-speaker evaluation and epistemic commitment become visible. They are individually well developed, but they have not been jointly applied to informal, evaluative online discourse produced by Japanese learners. Chapter 4 describes the corpora and analytical design through which this thesis does so: it details the construction of the six-cohort learner corpus and the BBC Have Your Say reference corpus, and it sets out a two-stage analysis combining an additive multidimensional analysis (MDA), which situates both corpora among a wider range of native-speaker registers, with the identification of stance-related phraseological patterns, recurrent sequences defined over tagged stance features such as evaluative adjectives, stance adverbs, suasive verbs and private verbs, and their lexical realisations as bundles. # References
